## The intro

> "Hi [Name], thanks for making the time. I'm Rahul. I look after brand partnerships in SPORTFIVE's Formula 1 business, and Bjorn and George are with me.
>
> A bit about how we work, because it's different from what most people expect. We don't sell for one team. We look at all eleven, work out what you're trying to achieve, and then see whether any of it fits. The teams pay us, not you. And if it doesn't fit, we'll tell you."

### "So why did you reach out to us?"

> "Two simple reasons. The first is who you serve. Clothing brands, food companies, logistics firms and carmakers. All of them are in F1. adidas makes the kit for two teams, and DHL moves the whole sport around the world.
>
> The second is what you do. Your labels and RFID tags let a company follow every product, from the factory to the shop. An F1 team does the same with thousands of parts, across a season on five continents. Then there's the look of the car. It's covered in paint and film, and this year the rules say more of it has to be covered. And all of it has to survive heat, speed and weather.
>
> What I'm really trying to work out on this call is the right way into sport for Avery Dennison. It can be complex. There are a lot of options, and they do different jobs. Helping brands find the right one is what we do. So I'm happy to take the conversation beyond Formula 1. SPORTFIVE also works in football, with athletes and across motorsport, so we can look at other opportunities too.
>
> But I'd like to hear from you first. Where are things heading?"

## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who watches F1? Is it our audience?"

> "Yes, in two ways. Your buyers watch, and so do their shoppers.
>
> F1 fans index at 160 for business decision makers, where 100 is the general population. That's the highest of any sport. In plain terms, the person who chooses a label, a film or an RFID program is more likely than average to follow F1.
>
> For your customers, it's the shoppers that matter. F1 fans index at 200 for brand loyalty, and 132 for buying premium. Both are the highest in sport. That's who a clothing or food brand is trying to reach.
>
> And the sport goes where you are. There are more than twenty race weekends on five continents, including Mexico City, Austin, Miami, Las Vegas, São Paulo and Shanghai."

### "Our buyers are brand owners, retailers and converters. Who's in the paddock?"

> "Mostly business people. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. There are more than 350 partner companies across the grid, and every one of them brings guests.
>
> What I'd point out for you is how many are brands you serve, or brands like them. PUMA dresses Ferrari. adidas dresses Mercedes and Audi. Tommy Hilfiger is with Cadillac. PepsiCo joined Mercedes this year with Gatorade, Sting and Doritos. And DHL is with the sport itself.
>
> The carmakers are there too. General Motors co-owns Cadillac. Mercedes, Ferrari and Audi have their own teams. Ford works with Red Bull on its engines, and Honda with Aston Martin. Teams also introduce their partners to each other. So the people your guests meet are often people they'd want to meet anyway.
>
> Converters are less likely to be in the paddock already. But that's who you'd bring. A race weekend is three days with a customer you want to keep."

### "How are similar brands activating, and what are they getting back?"

> "As far as we can tell, no labels or RFID company has a deal with an F1 team. So here are the closest ones: your competitor, the companies behind the look of the car, the kit, the freight and the parts."

**3M at Cadillac**

> "Start with the one you'll have seen. 3M has been with Cadillac since June. It's 3M's first F1 deal, after about two decades with NASCAR. Wendy Bauer, who runs 3M's transportation and electronics business, said F1 is 'one of the most demanding test environments for materials anywhere in the world.'
>
> Through the deal, 3M's scientists and engineers work with the team on lightweight materials, bonding and surface preparation, and how parts are made. They test in the lab and help at the track. 3M's name goes on the car and the team. It says it wants to take what it learns to carmakers. And it already works closely with GM, which co-owns the team.
>
> It's too early for results, and none are published. Cadillac's head of commercial strategy, Tyler Epp, said the team looks for a 'value match' that is 'authentic'. So the signal is that a careful new team picked a materials company, and gave it a real job, not just a logo."

**HP at Ferrari**

> "HP has been Ferrari's title partner for two and a half years. The team is now called Scuderia Ferrari HP. Last year HP's CEO, Enrique Lores, said the deal shows 'how HP is pushing the boundaries of what's possible.'
>
> Through the deal, HP supplies the team's computers and workstations. It also prints the graphics on the car with its latex printers. For Miami last year, engineers in Maranello and HP's team in Barcelona made a new film for a special livery. HP says it was up to 14% lighter and up to 17% thinner than the year before, and fully recyclable. Last month in Austin they did another special livery. A display car had hidden graphics that glow blue under UV light.
>
> HP hasn't published a business return. The signal is where it puts its effort. It builds a new livery project with the team each year, and says the work gives it ideas for customers in other industries."

**Sherwin-Williams at Mercedes**

> "Sherwin-Williams has been with Mercedes since 2023. It's the approved supplier of paint and coatings for the team's cars. When it signed, the team's head of paint and graphics, Andrew Moody, described the job as 'balancing the aerodynamic and weight requirements of painting a Formula One car.'
>
> Through the deal, Mercedes paints its car with Sherwin-Williams' Ultra 9K. The team says it gives good coverage 'with minimum weight gain', and keeps its colour on camera as well as in person. Last November the two made a video series where the team's people show how they prepare and paint the car.
>
> No return is published. The signal is that its paint is still on this year's car. And the films show the craft, not a sales pitch."

**adidas at Mercedes**

> "adidas has been with Mercedes since last year. It was adidas' first F1 deal, and it took over from PUMA, which had dressed the team since 2012. adidas' CEO, Bjørn Gulden, said 'interest in motorsport in general and Formula 1 in particular has been growing a lot.'
>
> Through the deal, adidas makes the kit for the whole team, plus a fan range of clothes, shoes and accessories. This year it added a second team, Audi.
>
> This is one where there's a public signal. In April, adidas said its motorsport clothing grew by double digits in the first quarter, and that the new Mercedes and Audi teamwear was well received. Every piece in that fan range needs labels, tags and a way to track it. That's your world."

**DHL with Formula 1**

> "DHL has been Formula 1's logistics partner since 2004. F1 calls it its longest-standing global partner. DHL's brand chief, Arjan Sissing, put the reason simply: 'it is DHL who delivers Formula 1 across the globe.'
>
> Through the deal, DHL moves the cars, spare parts and kit to every race, by road, air and sea. DHL and F1 say its biofuel trucks cut average emissions from the European races' logistics by 83% in 2023. And DHL's name sits above the garages in the pit lane, through the DHL Fastest Pit Stop Award.
>
> No business return is published. The signal is the renewals. F1 extended the deal again two years ago, in its twentieth year. And logistics is one of your own markets."

**Siemens at Red Bull**

> "Siemens has worked with Red Bull since 2004. When they marked twenty years together, the team's boss at the time was Christian Horner. He said Siemens gives it 'the digital backbone that lets us turn ideas into reality faster than ever.'
>
> Through the partnership, Red Bull manages the design of its cars in Siemens software. Each car has about 10,000 unique parts, and every one is tracked in that system. The team makes thousands of design changes a season.
>
> This is one of the few with published numbers. Siemens says design changes that took weeks to sign off now take hours, and its composite software cut design-to-delivery time by 30%. It shows that tracking parts is a real job inside a team. But it's done in software, and we don't know what's physically on the parts."

**What that adds up to**

> "So the honest picture is this. As far as we can tell, no labels or RFID company has a deal with an F1 team. Your closest competitor, 3M, has just arrived, at one team. The companies closest to the look of the car, HP and Sherwin-Williams, have real jobs on the livery. Most don't publish numbers. What they show is that they stay and do more. DHL and Siemens have been there for over twenty years. adidas added a second team. HP comes back with a new livery each year.
>
> For you, I'd take three things from that. First, give it a real job people can see: the livery, the kit, or tracking what moves. Second, host the people who buy from you. Brand owners, retailers and carmakers are already on the grid. Third, make content about the work, like Sherwin-Williams did, rather than about the product."

### "Content is really important for us. How would a team help us make it?"

> "It's one of the most useful parts of a deal. And your product is often the thing people already look at: the name on a shirt, the colour on a car.
>
> Most deals come with a set number of filming days each year with the drivers and the team's people, and the team's content people help you make it. The team's own channels matter too. F1 is the number one sport on social media, ahead of the NBA.
>
> One thing to know: the race footage is controlled by Formula 1 itself, not the teams. So partner content is filmed around the race: at the factory, in the garage, or on filming days.
>
> Two examples. Sherwin-Williams and Mercedes made a series where the team's people show how they prepare and paint the car. And Ferrari and HP unveiled their Miami livery as an event of its own, with the team boss and the drivers.
>
> For you, I'd start with the livery. This season, F1 brought in a rule: at least 55% of every car, seen from the side or above, has to be painted or covered, rather than bare carbon. And the cars are lighter this year. So a team's paint and graphics people have to cover more of the car, and add as little weight as they can. Film one of them with one of your film engineers, through a single special livery. Print it, apply it, race it, peel it off. How many grams, how many hours, and what heat and gravel do to it. No stunt. Just two people who make things look good and stay on.
>
> One thing to check first. Teams do this differently. McLaren said a few years ago that most of its car is wrapped. Mercedes paints its car. Ferrari uses printed film. So we'd only take this idea to a team that wraps, and wants to show it.
>
> A second idea, for the RFID side: follow one fan shirt from the factory to a fan at a race, and scan it at each step. Your Premier League badges already let fans scan a shirt to check it's real.
>
> If content matters to you, we'd spell it out in the deal: how many days, which drivers and team people, what the team posts on its own channels, and how long and where you can use it."

### "Why are these brands in F1?"

> "Two reasons, mostly.
>
> The first is proof. A team builds a car that's light, fast and changing all season, and it has to work at every race, in heat, rain and dust. If your film or tape holds up there, people believe it will hold up for them. That's why 3M is there.
>
> The second is people: the customers they want to host, and the shoppers their customers sell to. adidas said it went into F1 to reach new consumers. Most of the business-to-business partners use races to spend time with the people who buy from them.
>
> For Avery Dennison, I'd guess it's both. The graphics and tapes side is about proof. The apparel and RFID side is more about hosting brand owners and retailers, and showing them something they can't see anywhere else."

### "Who are our competitors in F1, and which teams are available?"

> "I don't want to promise you anything on that yet, because right now it would be a guess. As far as we can tell, no labels or RFID company has a deal with an F1 team.
>
> But some teams have partners close to what you do. 3M is at Cadillac. HEXIS, the French film maker, has worked on Alpine's graphics since 2018, though the latest we can find on it is from two years ago. HP prints Ferrari's livery graphics, and we don't know whose film it prints on. Sherwin-Williams paints the Mercedes. And some teams have brands you may already serve, like adidas at Mercedes and Audi, and PUMA at Ferrari. adidas signed up to your atma.io platform in 2021, for example. That can open a door, or it can need care. Deals like these usually come with exclusivity.
>
> So the first thing we do is check where there's a conflict, and rule those teams out.
>
> The second thing is understanding what you want out of it. If it's graphics and the look of the car, that can point to different teams than if it's RFID and tracking, or hosting brand owners. Then we come back with the teams where it fits.
>
> One thing worth knowing: these contracts are often written quite narrowly. So something like 'Official Labeling and RFID Partner' would be narrower than 3M's 'material science'. But we'd check each team before saying any more.
>
> To give you an idea of what a partner like you does once they're in, look at HP at Ferrari. HP's printers make the graphics on the car. Its engineers worked with Ferrari's to make a lighter, thinner film. So HP gets to say its printing is on a Ferrari at every race, and that it helped make it lighter. That's a much stronger story than a logo."

### "It's very crowded. Won't we get lost?"

> "It's a real risk. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and a lot of them are just logos.
>
> The ones that get noticed do one of two things. Some give their product a real job people can see, like HP printing Ferrari's livery, or Sherwin-Williams painting the Mercedes. Others make something people want to watch.
>
> You have an advantage most partners don't. Your product is what people already see: the colour on a car, the name and number on a shirt. Most technology partners have to explain software in a data centre. You can point at the car."

### "Why would a team want a partner like us?"

> "Teams aren't short of technology partners. Tech companies now spend more on F1 teams than any other kind of business, and a Reuters report in May counted eight new AI deals in six months.
>
> What's rarer is a company that knows the things a team handles every day, but rarely talks about. Three of them overlap with you.
>
> The livery. This season, at least 55% of every car has to be covered in paint or film. And the cars are lighter, so every gram counts. McLaren has said wraps let it add a new sponsor's logo in seconds, rather than waiting for next season.
>
> The parts. Red Bull tracks about 10,000 unique parts on each car. And every team ships its kit round the world for more than twenty race weekends.
>
> The merchandise. Your Premier League badges already let fans scan a shirt to see it's real.
>
> And you've been near racing before. When you launched your tyre tag with Sumitomo for endurance racing, the FIA was quoted in your announcement.
>
> You'd also bring people a team would like to meet: brand owners, retailers and carmakers. Whether a team wants any of that, and whether a current partner already covers it, is what we'd check first."

### "What would a first deal look like for a company our size?"

> "You had about $8.9 billion in sales last year, so money isn't the limit. The conflict check is. A typical first deal for a company that sells to other businesses is one team, one clear role, some branding, a few race weekends with customers, and some content, over two or three years.
>
> Others start smaller, hosting customers at a few races to see whether it works. Passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend. For you, Mexico City is a few hours' drive from your Querétaro plant. Austin, Miami and Las Vegas work for American brand owners and retailers. And São Paulo and Shanghai cover two more of your big markets."

### "What does it cost?"

> "It depends a lot on the team and what's included. A supplier or entry-level partner deal starts in the low single-digit millions a year. Official partner deals sit above that. Hosting customers at a few races, a driver deal or a content deal costs a lot less. I'd rather understand what you want first, then show you options at different levels."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

> "You can go to a team directly. They'll tell you about their own team, which is fair enough. We worked only with Mercedes and Ferrari for over twelve years, and then stopped, so we could look at all eleven teams for the brand. The teams pay our fee, so having us compare them costs you nothing. And with 3M at Cadillac, HP at Ferrari and HEXIS at Alpine, someone has to check each team quietly before your name goes anywhere."

### "Which team would fit us?"

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. It depends on which part of the business leads, which markets matter most, and where there's no conflict. That's what we'd come back to you with."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "We'd agree that before anything's signed. For you I'd look at three things. Business: which brand owners, retailers, carmakers and converters you spent time with, and the orders that came from it. The work: if there's a real job with the team, what you learned and can show customers. And content: how far the films travelled, which we can measure.
>
> It can show up in the numbers. Airwallex says its revenue grew 50% year on year in its Europe and Americas regions during its McLaren partnership, and 57% in the UK from new customers."

### "3M is already in F1. Are we too late?"

> "No. 3M joined Cadillac in June, and it's 3M's first F1 deal. That's one team out of eleven.
>
> What it does tell you is that a team will give a materials company a real job. That's useful for you. It shows the idea works.
>
> It also tells you where you probably can't go. I'd expect Cadillac to be out for anything close to materials, films or tapes, and we'd confirm that.
>
> And you'd be telling a different story. 3M's deal is about what goes into the car. Yours could be about what goes on it, the livery, and about tracking parts, kit and merchandise.
>
> Being second isn't a problem in F1. adidas came in long after PUMA, and it's now with two teams."

### "We sell to brand owners and converters, not consumers. Why F1?"

> "Because your customers sell to consumers, and they care what consumers think. F1 fans are the most brand-loyal in sport, with an index of 200. That's who a clothing or food brand is trying to reach.
>
> You already do this in other sports. Your Premier League badges let fans scan a shirt to check it's real. Your Cowboys labels open content on a fan's phone. That's a consumer story you tell to brand owners.
>
> And F1 gives you three days with the brand owners themselves. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. Fans don't need to know the name Avery Dennison. The people who buy from you need to remember the weekend."

### "We already do a lot in football and US sports. Why add F1?"

> "Fair question, and you may not need it. Your work in football is mostly the product itself: the badges, the labels, the shirts fans buy. That's a strong story, and it sells to the clubs and the kit brands.
>
> F1 does a different job. It's global, with 827 million fans and more than twenty race weekends on five continents. It's also built for hosting: two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. And it's a place to show your films and tapes on a car, which football can't do.
>
> So the question is what it's for. If it's meeting brand owners, retailers and carmakers around the world, F1 is built for that. If it's mainly about the shirt, you may already have what you need. SPORTFIVE also works in football, with athletes and in rallying, so we'd help you choose rather than sell you everything at once."

### "What's the biggest risk?"

> "Signing a logo with no job. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and the ones that get lost are the ones that put a name on the car and stop there. A deal needs a real job, a plan for the people you'll host, and someone on your side who owns it.
>
> The second risk is a conflict you didn't see. 3M is at Cadillac, and contracts often cover more than the title says. That's why we check every team before your name goes anywhere.
>
> The third is picking the wrong option for the job. If what you need is to prove a film on gravel and snow, rallying might come first. If it's the shirt, it might be football. We'd rather tell you that up front than sell you the wrong thing."

### "Where do you see our product sitting, in real time?"

> "In three places, and you can see all of them live.
>
> The first is on the car. This year at least 55% of every car has to be covered in paint or film, and the cars are lighter, so every gram counts. If a team wraps its car, your film is on screen every session, at every race, in heat, rain and gravel. That's the most visible test there is. But teams do this differently. Mercedes paints its car, and Ferrari uses HP's printed film. So this only works with a team that wraps, and we'd check that first.
>
> The second is on people. The team kit, and the shirts fans buy at the track. adidas makes the kit for two teams, and every piece in a fan range needs labels and tags. Your Premier League badges already let a fan scan a shirt to check it's real. The same idea could work at a race.
>
> The third is everything that moves. A team ships its cars, spares and kit around the world for more than twenty race weekends, and Red Bull tracks about 10,000 unique parts on each car. We don't know what's physically on those parts today, so I won't claim a team needs RFID. But tracking what moves is a job every team does, every week.
>
> And one thing I'd hold to. If anyone builds AI on top of that data, the same rule applies as on the pit wall: if you can't watch it working, you shouldn't let it run. Where teams use AI, like Red Bull's strategy agent with Oracle, the race engineers watch it and make the call. Your tags are how a company sees each product in real time. That's the story."

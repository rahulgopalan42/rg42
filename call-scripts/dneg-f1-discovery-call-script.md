## Part 4: How Formula 1 partnerships work (8 to 10 minutes)

*[Calibrate to their answer from Part 2. If they know the sport, compress. Invite questions as you go: "Stop me anytime."]*

### 4.1 The sport in thirty seconds

> "Let me give you a simple picture of how the sport is set up, because it explains the options you have.
>
> Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company. They own the commercial side: the calendar, the broadcast deals, the races.
>
> The FIA is the regulator. They write the rules.
>
> And then there are eleven teams. From this coming season it is eleven, including two American teams, Haas and Cadillac. Each team is an independent business with its own owners and its own partnerships.
>
> So a brand can partner at three levels: with the sport itself, with a team, or with a driver personally. Most brands enter through a team, and I can explain why when we get there."

### 4.2 What a team partnership includes

> "What do you get as a partner? Think of it in five parts.
>
> **One, branding.** Your logo on the car, the race suits, the garage. Around 24 race weekends a year. Last season the total audience was 1.8 billion, and the average race reached 76 million people.
>
> **Two, the official title.** You become, for example, the Official Content Studio of a team. You use the team's name, logo and imagery in your own marketing and client conversations.
>
> **Three, hospitality.** Paddock access, the Paddock Club, garage tours, time with the drivers. 66% of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. For a company selling to studios and enterprises, every race is a room full of buyers.
>
> And one detail people miss. A Grand Prix runs over three days, and your passes work every day. Seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend, and hundreds across a season.
>
> **Four, content and digital.** Access to the team's channels, the drivers, the cars and the story. For most partners this is a right. For you it is raw material, and your craft turned loose on it becomes the showcase itself.
>
> **Five, people.** Driver appearances, filming days, team bosses at your events. Imagine a driver at your premiere, or your reel opening the team's season.
>
> And around all of this sits the business platform: every team carries 25 or more partners, more than 350 companies across the grid, meeting each other all season. Every one of them makes content and buys content. That room is Brahma's pipeline."

### 4.3 The audience

*[DNEG sits on both sides: B2B buyers and a fanbase that lives on content. Fans first, because that is the product story.]*

> "Now, who you reach. Two audiences matter for you.
>
> **The fans, because they live on content.** Formula 1 has 827 million fans, up 63% since 2018. 43% are under 35, 42% are women. This is the most engaged fanbase in sport: number one in social engagement, ahead of the NBA. And in the sport's own survey, 61% of fans consume F1 content every single day. The same survey says teams and drivers are the new front doors, and storytelling beats logo placement. This sport runs on exactly what you make.
>
> **The buyers, because they sign your contracts.** F1 has the highest concentration of business decision makers in sport, indexing 160 for management and strategy roles. The paddock holds 350 partner companies, and every one of them makes and buys content. And the media world is already here: Apple broadcasts F1 in the US, and Hollywood made the sport its biggest sports film in years."

### 4.4 Why this could fit DNEG

> "Let me tell you why I find this opportunity interesting for DNEG. Three reasons.
>
> **First, the empty seat.** This sport has become a content machine, but no team has a Hollywood-grade studio as a partner. The first team that does gets films, fan experiences and campaigns at a level nobody else on the grid can match. And the studio that takes the seat gets the most visible showcase in sport.
>
> **Second, the Brahma story.** You are selling an AI content platform to big organisations, and the NBA is already an anchor customer. An F1 team running its content operation on Brahma is the sequel: live proof, in the fastest growing sport, in front of 350 companies who all have the same problem.
>
> **Third, the timing.** Your group just raised at a two billion dollar valuation to take Brahma across industries. F1 is growing fastest in America, where Apple shows the races. And your own group already touched the sport through the F1 movie. The doors are aligned.
>
> One last thing on value. All of this sits on very small supply: eleven teams for the entire world, one seat per category, three to five year deals. Small supply, growing demand. That is why these partnerships hold their value."

### 4.5 Three activation ideas (examples, not proposals)

*[Offer these as sketches to make it concrete. Let them react; their reaction feeds the brief.]*

> "To make it concrete, three sketches. Early thoughts, nothing more.
>
> **One, the team film.** DNEG makes the team's season film at cinema level: the car's birth, the factory, the season's story. Premiered for fans, cut for social, shown to sponsors. Nobody on the grid has content at that level.
>
> **Two, the immersive garage.** A DNEG-built experience where fans stand in the garage, ride the lap, feel the pit stop. It travels: fan zones, races, partner events, your own showcases.
>
> **Three, the Brahma engine.** The team's daily content operation runs on Brahma: every race, every language, every channel. And the team's 25 partners see it working live, then buy it for themselves. The activation is also the sales demo."

### Q1. "Are we too small for Formula 1?"

> "I would not think about it as size. I would think about it as fit, and about the right structure.
>
> The sport has entry points at many levels: content-partner tiers, supplier designations, regional rights. And for a company like DNEG, the deal does not have to be all cash. Your work can be part of the package, and the rights follow the total value you bring.
>
> Some of the best stories in F1 are companies that entered at the right level and grew with it. Airwallex came in as a scale-up, and during the partnership revenue grew 50% year on year across EMEA and the Americas.
>
> The brief tells us what is realistic. Our job is to find the level where the numbers work, and to structure it so the partnership pays for itself in showcase and customers, not just exposure."

### Q2. "What is the difference between partnering with F1 itself, a team, or a driver?"

> "Three doors into the sport.
>
> **The series, Formula 1 itself.** You partner with the sport: branding at every race, global reach, neutrality. Bigger deals, premium prices. Salesforce and Globant sit here on the digital side.
>
> **A team.** The story lives here: the car, the drivers, the factory, the season. For a studio, this is where the best material is, and where a content seat makes the most sense. Many more entry points and price levels.
>
> **A driver personally.** The face, not the team. Campaign rights, personal channels, appearance days. Useful for a film campaign, but no team rights and no paddock hospitality.
>
> The brief will tell us which door, or which combination."

### Q3. "Teams have thirty partners. How does one brand stand out?"

> "Fair question. Three answers.
>
> First, structure. Partners sit in clear tiers, and your tier defines exactly what you own: positions, days, content, hospitality. All contractual.
>
> Second, exclusivity. Within your category you are alone. The team cannot sign another studio.
>
> Third, attention is won through activation, not logo size. And here you have an unfair advantage: you make the most watchable content on earth. A DNEG team film will out-travel every logo on that car.
>
> And the access, the hospitality, the introductions: those are private rights. They never get diluted by other partners."

### Q4. "How do teams help with B2B introductions?"

> "Very concretely. This is a managed process, not a cocktail party.
>
> Every team runs a family of at least 25 partners, and the team's partnership department actively connects them: compliant introductions, partner summits, business forums on race weekends, private dinners. And it continues off the calendar at events like CES and Web Summit.
>
> Two examples from our own work. CommScope's RUCKUS brand at Haas: enterprise wireless powering the team's operations, with a live showcase in the garage; our agency ran the grid-wide process. And Airwallex at McLaren: during the partnership, revenue grew 50% year on year across EMEA and the Americas, and UK revenue grew 57% from new customer acquisition.
>
> For DNEG and Brahma: 350 companies in that paddock, every one of them making and buying content, meeting you as a fellow partner. That is a pipeline, not a billboard."

### Q5. "Could a team not make its content themselves?"

> "They do, every day, and that is exactly the point. Team media teams are good at fast social content. They are not built for cinema, immersive experiences, or AI content operations at scale. Different craft, different tools, different people.
>
> That is why the partner model exists: teams take proven capability from partners, and the partner gets the showcase. RUCKUS runs the wireless at Haas. IBM works with Ferrari. The teams buy what they cannot build, and they move faster for it.
>
> And what you bring is not just capacity. It is the level: the difference between a clip and a film. Fans feel that difference immediately, and so do the team's sponsors."

### Q6. "How is ROI measured in Formula 1?"

> "Four ways, usually.
>
> **Media value.** Independent firms like Nielsen track every second your branding appears, across TV and digital, and give it a money value against your fee.
>
> **Brand tracking.** Awareness and consideration, measured before and after. In the US, fans are 32% more likely to consider a sponsor's product.
>
> **Business pipeline.** For B2B, you count introductions, meetings and deals closed through the platform. That is how Airwallex could point to 50% revenue growth during their partnership.
>
> **And for you, the content itself.** Views, engagement, reach of what you make with the team. Your activation measures its own value, which very few sponsors can say.
>
> You agree the framework upfront and review it with the team every quarter."

### Q7. "Who owns Formula 1? How does the money work?"

> "Three layers, very simple.
>
> Liberty Media, an American media company, owns Formula 1 itself. They bought it in 2017. They control the commercial rights: races, broadcast deals, the calendar.
>
> The FIA is the independent regulator.
>
> The eleven teams are independent businesses: Ferrari, Mercedes and Audi belong to car makers, Red Bull to the drinks company, others to investors. Each team earns a share of the sport's revenues plus its own sponsorships.
>
> Since 2021 the sport has a cost cap, so teams became profitable, stable businesses. Team sponsorship has doubled since 2020, to 2.2 billion dollars a year. You would be entering a healthy, growing market."

### Q8. "What does a partnership cost, and why is Formula 1 so expensive?"

> "Let me explain how the pricing works, because Formula 1 has its own logic.
>
> The price follows the rights inside the deal: your tier, your branding positions, driver time, content days, hospitality volume, and the category you lock. As a rule of thumb: entry-level and supplier positions start in the low single-digit millions per year. Meaningful official partner positions sit in the mid single-digit millions. Title partnerships are a different world, well above fifty million a year. And content or technology structures, where your work is part of the package, can be shaped differently.
>
> Why more expensive than other sport? Four reasons.
>
> First, supply. Football has hundreds of clubs. Formula 1 has eleven teams for the entire world, one seat per category, locked for three to five years.
>
> Second, demand. Team sponsorship has doubled since 2020, and new money keeps arriving.
>
> Third, one deal covers the world: around 24 races across five continents in one contract.
>
> And fourth, access. The paddock and the hospitality carry the price, because nobody else can offer them.
>
> This is why we start with the brief. Our job is to match the level to your goals, and to structure something the numbers can carry. And our advice costs you nothing."

# Obagi x SPORTFIVE: F1 Call, Questions and Answers

**Call:** intro call with [Name, Job title] at Obagi Medical. From SPORTFIVE: Rahul, Bjorn and George.

---

## The intro

> "Hi [Name], thanks for making the time. I'm Rahul. I look after brand partnerships in SPORTFIVE's Formula 1 business, and Bjorn and George are with me.
>
> A bit about how we work, because it's different from what most people expect. We don't sell for one team. We look at all eleven, work out what you're trying to achieve, and then see whether any of it fits. The teams pay us, not you. And if it doesn't fit, we'll tell you."

### "So why did you reach out to us?"

> "A couple of things stood out. The first is where Obagi is right now. Bridgepoint has owned the business since the end of July, Michel Brousset and Hind Sebti moved across to run it, and the first half of this year was up 27%, mostly from the skincare range rather than the new fillers. A brand growing that fast, with new owners behind it, usually has some big choices to make about where to spend.
>
> The second is a trend. Two years ago, F1 teams didn't really have skincare partners. Since then, ELEMIS has signed with Aston Martin, CLEAR with Red Bull, Gillette and Venus with Audi, Wilkinson Sword with Williams, and Sephora with F1 Academy, the women's series. And in June, SkinCeuticals became Ferrari's first skincare partner. That's a brand sold through dermatologists and plastic surgeons, so it's the closest thing to Obagi on the grid.
>
> None of them are there for TV time. They're there because the fans have changed, and because a race weekend is a good place to look after the people who matter most to them. For you, that might be the doctors who stock you as much as the people who buy online. So it felt worth a conversation, mainly to understand where you're heading under the new owners."

## Questions to ask Obagi

1. "Now the Bridgepoint deal has closed, what changes first? More US, more international, or more injectables?"
2. "The first half was up 27%. Where's most of that coming from: the practices, obagi.com and Amazon, or distributors abroad?"
3. "When a dermatologist or a med spa decides to start stocking Obagi, who makes that call, and what tips it?"
4. "Since the Rohto deal, Japan is theirs. How much does the rest of Asia matter to you now? Vietnam, the rest of Southeast Asia, China?"
5. "With saypha, you're starting with injectors before consumers. How does that change where the marketing money goes over the next two years?"
6. "Nu-Derm has been your best-selling range for three years, and parts of it are prescription. When you talk to consumers, which lines can you talk about freely?"
7. "The Skin Recharge truck with Next Health in West Hollywood in May: how did that go, and how did you judge it afterwards?"
8. "You worked with Priyanka Chopra a few years back. Do you see Obagi using well-known faces again, or does the face stay the doctor?"
9. "Has anyone at Obagi been to a race? And what did you make of SkinCeuticals going to Ferrari?"
10. "If something here made sense, who else would need to be involved? Does Bridgepoint sign off on this kind of spend? And is there budget for it, or would it need to be found?"

## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who watches F1? Is it our audience?"

> "Two numbers matter most for you. 42% of F1 fans are women, and 43% are under 35. So the fan base now includes a lot of people who buy skincare for themselves, not just the people you'd picture watching on a Sunday afternoon.
>
> The third number is about how they buy. On brand loyalty, F1 fans index at 200, the highest of any sport. That means they're twice as likely as the average person to stick with a brand once they've picked it. For a brand people buy again and again, that's the part I'd pay attention to."

### "Is it just men, or has the audience changed?"

> "It's changed a lot. In 2018, 37% of fans were women. Now it's 42%. And the women's series, F1 Academy, races on the same weekends as F1, with its own series on Netflix.
>
> The US is a good example, and I'd guess it's still your biggest market. Purchase consideration for F1 sponsors there is up 32%, and among women it's up 41%. In plain terms, more American fans, women especially, are open to buying from the brands they see in F1. And there are three US races now: Miami, Austin and Las Vegas.
>
> You can see the teams responding. When Audi signed Gillette in January, it brought in Venus as well, and said it was to welcome a more female, next-generation audience to the sport."

### "What are companies like us doing in F1?"

> "Two years ago there was almost no skincare in F1. Now there's quite a lot. A few examples."

- **SkinCeuticals at Ferrari.** L'Oréal's skincare brand for doctors signed in June as Ferrari's first skincare partner, on a multi-year deal. It's sold through more than 12,000 dermatologists, plastic surgeons and aesthetic clinics, and the plan is a global campaign plus hospitality and events at selected races. What it tells you: the brand most like yours has already decided this audience is worth it.
- **ELEMIS at Aston Martin.** It became the team's first skincare partner in early 2025. Guests in Aston Martin's Paddock Club suite get short treatments on race days, and it made limited-edition products with the team. What it tells you: skincare works best in F1 as something guests try, not something they watch.
- **Sephora at F1 Academy.** Since March, Sephora has had its own car in the women's series, driven by a Spanish rookie, Natalia Granada. It also runs its Glam Bars inside F1's Paddock Club at eighteen races this year. What it tells you: F1 now treats beauty as part of the hospitality, not just the advertising.
- **CLEAR at Red Bull.** Unilever's scalp care shampoo became Red Bull's first haircare partner this year, with its logo on the car and on the drivers' balaclavas. At the Shanghai race it set up a barbershop about helmet hair, and its marketing runs across Asia, Europe and Latin America. What it tells you: a small, true detail from the drivers' lives beats a big claim.
- **Gillette, Braun and Venus at Audi.** P&G signed all three with Audi in January, on a multi-year deal, with Gillette on the car. Venus was added to bring more women and younger fans to the sport. What it tells you: even shaving brands are using F1 to reach women.
- **Wilkinson Sword at Williams.** Williams' men's grooming partner since January, with its logo on the nose, the halo and the drivers' helmets. It's reported to be the biggest brand sponsorship its owner Edgewell has done, and in British Grand Prix week it ran a fan experience at Williams' fan zone in London. What it tells you: a midfield team can be a serious place to start.
- **Charles Leclerc with L'Oréal Paris.** In April, L'Oréal Paris made the Ferrari driver a global face of its Men Expert range, next to Carlos Sainz of Williams. Pierre Gasly does something similar for Givenchy's men's fragrance. What it tells you: a driver deal is a smaller way in, and you don't need a team to do it.

### "How are brands like us using it, beyond a logo?"

> "A few things keep coming up.
>
> Something for guests to try. ELEMIS gives treatments in Aston Martin's Paddock Club suite on race days, and Sephora has Glam Bars in the Paddock Club. People remember what they tried, not what they saw on a car.
>
> Hosting the people who sell for you. Hospitality passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests over one weekend. For you, that could be twenty of your best practices at Austin or Las Vegas.
>
> Races in the markets that matter. CLEAR did its helmet hair barbershop in Shanghai. Miami, Austin and Las Vegas cover the US, and Singapore is a short flight from Vietnam, where most of your Southeast Asia business is.
>
> A driver. L'Oréal Paris has Leclerc and Sainz. Sephora put its own car and driver into F1 Academy, and Charlotte Tilbury did the same for two seasons before that.
>
> Pop-ups. Your truck with Next Health is the kind of thing that works in a fan zone. Wilkinson Sword did something similar at Williams' fan zone in London.
>
> None of that needs you to say what a product does, which matters for a brand like yours."

### "Content is really important for us. How would a team help us make it?"

> "That's one of the most useful parts of a deal, if it's set up properly.
>
> Most partnerships come with a set number of days with the drivers and the team each year. On those days you film with them, at the factory, in the garage, or somewhere you choose. The team's own content people often help make it, and the good teams post it on their channels too, which is where most fans see it. F1 is the number one sport on social media, ahead of the NBA.
>
> One thing to know: the race footage is controlled by Formula 1 itself, not the teams. So most partner content is filmed around the race, not during it. Behind the scenes, travel, the garage, the drivers' routines.
>
> For skincare, that's a good fit. ELEMIS made limited-edition products with Aston Martin, and signed Jessica Hawkins from the team's F1 Academy programme as one of its faces. CLEAR at Red Bull built its campaign around helmet hair, something every driver deals with, and set up a barbershop at the Shanghai race. What a driver's skin goes through in a helmet, in the heat, flying across time zones all season, is a real story, and it doesn't need any product claims.
>
> So if content matters to you, we'd spell it out in the deal: how many days, which drivers, what the team will post, and how long you can use it."

### "Why are these brands in F1?"

> "Three reasons, and you can hear them in what the brands say themselves.
>
> The fans. Audi said it brought Venus in to reach more women and younger fans. Sephora's marketing chief talked about reaching fans from Shanghai to Austin. And F1 fans are the most likely of any sport's fans to pay more for a premium brand.
>
> The guests. Ferrari said SkinCeuticals would improve the guest experience. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives, so it's a quiet way to put a premium product in front of people who can afford it.
>
> And the link with doing hard things well. SkinCeuticals talked about discipline and high performance. CLEAR talked about performing under pressure. That suits a brand a dermatologist started 38 years ago.
>
> For Obagi, I'd guess the second one matters most, because your guests would be the doctors and practice owners who stock you."

### "Who are our competitors in F1, and which teams are available?"

> "I don't want to promise you anything on that yet, because right now it would be a guess. There are a few teams that already have a partner close to what you do. SkinCeuticals at Ferrari is the obvious one, and ELEMIS at Aston Martin. Then there are grooming and hair brands at Red Bull, Audi and Williams, and Sephora at F1 Academy and in the Paddock Club. And those deals usually come with exclusivity.
>
> So the first thing we do is check where there's a conflict, and rule those teams out.
>
> The second thing is understanding what you want out of it. If it's mainly about hosting doctors in the US, that can point you to different teams than if it's about reaching consumers in Southeast Asia. Then we come back with the teams where it fits.
>
> One thing worth knowing: a shaving or shampoo deal isn't the same as skincare, and these contracts are often written quite narrowly. So a team with a grooming partner isn't automatically off the table. Drivers can be separate too, though L'Oréal Paris already has Leclerc and Sainz.
>
> To give you an idea of what a partner like you does once they're in, look at ELEMIS at Aston Martin. It's not just a logo. On race days, guests in Aston Martin's Paddock Club suite can have a treatment, so ELEMIS gets real time with the people the team most wants to impress. It made limited-edition products with the team, and it signed Jessica Hawkins, who runs Aston Martin's F1 Academy programme, as one of its faces. A few months in, Aston Martin's road car business signed ELEMIS as well. So one deal led to a second. That's a much stronger story than a sticker on the car."

### "It's very crowded. Won't we get lost?"

> "It's a real risk. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and a lot of them are just logos. The ones that get noticed do one of two things.
>
> Some give people something to try. ELEMIS isn't the biggest name at Aston Martin, but a treatment in the Paddock Club stays with a guest longer than a logo on a wall.
>
> Others find a small, true story. CLEAR at Red Bull built its first big moment around helmet hair, something every driver deals with, and set up a barbershop at the Shanghai race.
>
> For you, the obvious version is the doctors. Apart from SkinCeuticals, the beauty brands in F1 mostly sell through shops. You sell through dermatologists, plastic surgeons and med spas. A weekend with twenty of them is something a shop brand can't copy."

### "Why would a team want a partner like us?"

> "Teams are working hard to reach women and younger fans, and they want partners those fans already know. Audi said as much when it signed Venus.
>
> Skincare is also new to F1. Ferrari called SkinCeuticals its first skincare partner, and ELEMIS was Aston Martin's first. So teams are still working out what a skincare partner can add, and most of the answer so far has been in hospitality.
>
> And you'd bring something different: a brand started by a dermatologist, with a network of practices behind it. Whether there's room for that at a given team is what we'd check first."

### "What would a first deal look like for a company our size?"

> "Usually smaller than people think. Most companies your size start with one team, one clear role, some branding, a few race weekends with guests, and a bit of content, over two or three years.
>
> Some start smaller still. Hosting your best practices at two or three races, a driver deal, or a car in F1 Academy, the way Sephora has this year. That lets you see whether it works for you before going further."

### "What does it cost?"

> "It depends a lot on the team and what's included. A supplier or entry-level partner deal starts in the low single-digit millions a year. Driver deals, content and hosting guests at a few races cost a lot less. I'd rather understand what you want first, then show you options at different levels."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

> "You can go to a team directly. They'll tell you about their own team, which is fair enough. We worked only with Mercedes and Ferrari for over twelve years, and then stopped, so we could look at all eleven teams for the brand. The teams pay our fee, so having us compare them costs you nothing."

### "Which team would fit us?"

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. It depends on whether this is about doctors or consumers, the US or Asia, and where there's no conflict. That's what we'd come back to you with."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "We'd agree that before anything's signed. For you, I'd look at three things. Which practices you hosted, and whether they ordered more or took on new lines afterwards. How many new practices you signed in the cities where you did something. And sales on obagi.com and Amazon around race weekends in those markets. TV exposure matters a lot less than that."

### "We sell through doctors. How does that fit?"

> "It's a fair question. F1 isn't a doctor's waiting room, and a lot of what you sell can't be advertised like a lipstick. So I wouldn't build anything around what a product does, and anything with your name on it would go through your medical and legal teams first.
>
> Where it fits is the people. The Paddock Club is mostly business people, and a race weekend is a good way to thank the dermatologists, surgeons and med spa owners who stock you. SkinCeuticals sells the same way, and it's just made the same bet. Then the lines you sell online can carry the fan side. If it doesn't make sense for you, I'll tell you."

### "SkinCeuticals is already at Ferrari. Are we too late?"

> "It rules out Ferrari, and maybe a team or two more once we've checked. But it also tells you something useful: a skincare brand sold through doctors, owned by L'Oréal, looked hard at this audience and decided it was worth it. And going second means you get to see what works for them before you commit."

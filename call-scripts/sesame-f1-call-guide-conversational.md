### "Content is really important for us. How would a team help us make it?"

> "Yeah, absolutely. And I think for you, content could be the main part of this. Your subject is conversation, and F1 has the best-known conversation in sport, which is the driver and the race engineer on the radio.
>
> Most team partnerships include a set number of filming days each year with the drivers and the team's people. The team's content people help you make it, and the team's own channels matter too. F1 is the number one sport on social media, ahead of the NBA, with 2.2 billion engagements. And 61% of fans engage with F1 content every day.
>
> The one thing to be aware of is that Formula 1 controls the actual race footage, not the teams. That includes the team radio you hear in the broadcast. So partner content is filmed around the race, at the factory, in the garage, or on filming days.
>
> There are a few good examples already. CrowdStrike made a series with Mercedes where the team's specialists explain the safety kit on the car, and Mercedes says it got around 6.2 million views. And teams already tell the radio story themselves. Mercedes has made a film on how the team talks to its drivers on track, and Red Bull has published its own guide to team radio.
>
> For Sesame, I'd start with one question. What makes a voice calming and clear at 300 kilometres an hour? A race engineer has to decide what to say, when to say it, and when to say nothing.
>
> There's some nice material on that already. Max Verstappen said of his engineer, back in 2021: 'I have a lot of respect of sometimes how calm he stays.' Zhou Guanyu, when he raced for Alfa Romeo, said he and his engineer tried to keep the radio 'as short as possible during the race.' >

So you'd film a race engineer on those rules, in their own voice. We wouldn't claim a team uses Sesame, there'd be no cloned voices, and there's no stunt.
>

>
> And we'd make sure the content rights are clearly written into the partnership. That means how many filming days, which drivers and team people, what the team posts on its own channels, and how long and where you can use it. For you, I'd add one more line, which is exactly what you may do with anyone's recorded voice."

### "AI companions get criticised. Would a team be comfortable with us?"

> "That's a fair question, and I'd rather be straight with you. Some teams will be comfortable, and some won't. Teams run background checks on every partner. For an AI you talk to, they'll look at three things: children, people leaning on it too much, and voices being copied.
>
> So here's where things stand, as I read it. In September last year, the US Federal Trade Commission ordered seven companies to report on AI companions and children. >

>

>

>

>

### "Could a driver or an engineer be a voice in our product?"

> "Yeah, it's possible in principle. But it's a rights question, and I wouldn't want to promise it. There are really three sets of rights.
>

And the radio you hear in the broadcast is part of Formula 1's coverage, so Formula 1 controls that, not the team. So there'd be no team radio clips, and nothing trained on them.
>
> It has been done once that I know of. Two years ago, Aston Martin and Fernando Alonso launched an AI version of his voice with ElevenLabs. It reads the team's website articles aloud, in English, Spanish and French. That was done with the driver and the team together, and it only reads what the team writes.
>
> There's your side as well. Your own policy bans a digital replica of a person without their consent. And the law is moving. A US bill on AI copies of a person's voice cleared a Senate committee in June, though it isn't law yet.
>
> Fake AI clips of drivers are already going around, too. So anything here would have to be clearly agreed and clearly labelled.
>
> So I think the realistic first step isn't a voice in the product. It's a real driver or engineer, in their own voice, on film, talking about conversation. And if that goes well, a licensed voice is a later conversation, with the driver's own people, the team and probably Formula 1 in it."

### "Our product is still early. Isn't this too soon?"

### "Our funding is for building the product, not marketing."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "I think we'd want to agree that before anything's signed. And for a consumer app, I'd use things you can already count. For Sesame, I'd look at four areas.
>

>

>

Now, that doesn't mean the sponsorship alone drove those results. But it shows the kind of business outcome a company would track."

### "What's the biggest risk?"

> "I think the biggest risk is signing a logo with no job behind it. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and the ones that get lost put a name on the car and stop there. With investors' money, that's hard to defend.
>

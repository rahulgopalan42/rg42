# Brother x SPORTFIVE: F1 Call, Questions and Answers

**Call:** intro call with [Name, Job title] at Brother. From SPORTFIVE: Rahul, Björn and George.

**How to use:** start with the first paragraph or two of each answer, then see how they respond. The rest is there if they want to go deeper.

## The intro

> "Hi [Name], great to meet you, and thanks for making the time. I've also got Björn and George joining me today.
>
> Just very briefly on myself, I look after strategic brand partnerships here at SPORTFIVE, with a particular focus on Formula 1. My role is essentially to work with brands, understand their commercial and marketing objectives, and help them identify the right opportunities across the sports landscape.
>
> Just to give you a bit of context on SPORTFIVE, we're a global sports marketing agency, but what really differentiates us is our brand-led approach. We work with one of the largest portfolios of rights holders across sports, which puts us in a position to be quite objective in terms of identifying what's actually right for a brand.
>
> Specifically within Formula 1, we previously held exclusive mandates with Mercedes and Ferrari, but we made a conscious decision to move away from that model. Today, we work on a non-exclusive basis across the grid.
>
> The thinking behind that was quite simple. We didn't want to be in a position where we're trying to sell a particular team to a brand just because that's the team we represent. Instead, we can look across the market, assess the different opportunities, benchmark the commercials and ultimately identify where a brand can get the most value.
>
> A good example is CFI. They initially approached us with an interest in Williams, but once we understood their objectives a little better, we felt Lewis Hamilton was actually a more relevant opportunity, and we ended up brokering that partnership.
>
> And I'm conscious that sport, and Formula 1 in particular, isn't new to Brother, given your years on the Jordan cars in the early 2000s and more than a decade on Manchester City's shirts before that.
>
> I think what's also worth saying upfront is that a full team partnership isn't necessarily the only way to get your foot in the door. There are opportunities around hospitality, content, individual drivers and more focused commercial arrangements that could potentially make more sense, depending on where Brother is as a business today.
>
> So, rather than coming into this conversation with a particular team or proposition in mind, we'd really like to understand a little more about the business, your priorities and whether there's a role for Formula 1 in supporting those."

**Optional, a more personal version of the line about yourself:**

Before that, I was based in Dubai and was actually one of the first people to join our Middle East office, where I worked across football, esports and gaming. ## Questions to ask Brother

1. "Maybe just to start with, it would be great to understand a little more about your role and how marketing is structured within Brother. As I understand it, the group is headquartered in Nagoya, Brother International Corporation in New Jersey looks after the Americas, there's a European headquarters in Manchester, and then you've got Domino and, since March, MUTOH within the industrial printing business. How does that work in practice? Would an international partnership like this be driven by a region, by a business unit or by Nagoya?"
2. "One thing that stood out in your medium-term plan, CS B2027, is the ambition to take the industrial side of the business to around 40% of sales, from 32% last year. You've identified machine tools, industrial printing and commercial and industrial labelling as the growth businesses, while office and home printing is there to protect the earnings base. If Brother were to invest in something like Formula 1, which of those would you most want it to support? Is it about accelerating the industrial side, or protecting and growing print?"
3. "Looking at your first-half results, Printing and Solutions was around 62% of revenue and Personal and Home around 6%. And yet the Brother name is probably best known for home printers, label makers and sewing machines. When you think about the brand, is the priority the business buyer, or is the consumer and small-office audience just as important? And does that answer differ between the US, Europe and Japan?"
4. "I came across some comments from your European sales and marketing lead around the 'More time for life' campaign, which ran across 15 European countries. The point he made was that Brother does well when a reseller is advising the customer, but that a lack of brand awareness is holding you and your resellers back with small and home office buyers. Is that a challenge you recognize in your own market as well? And is the job building awareness, or changing what people associate Brother with?"
5. "From a geographical standpoint, where do you most need the brand to work harder over the next two to three years? From your results, the Americas is your largest region in printing, Europe has been softer, and machine tools is very much an Asian story, with China, India and Japan as priority regions and Europe and Southeast Asia recently added. Are there markets where the product is strong, but the brand hasn't necessarily caught up?"
6. "A lot of your business goes through partners, whether that's resellers and retailers on the print side or authorized dealers on the sewing side. I noticed, for example, that one of this year's business inkjet models is sold exclusively through your Gold Authorized Partners. How important are those partners commercially, and do you already run incentive programmes or events with them? Because potentially, something like Formula 1 could be a platform you leverage together with them, rather than purely as a Brother initiative."
7. "On the industrial side, Domino lists beverage, food, life sciences, tobacco, industrial and personal care as its core industries, and several of the biggest names in those industries are partners of Formula 1 teams. Without asking you to name customers, are those the companies you'd want to be spending time with? And who is the buyer inside those businesses? Is it packaging, operations or procurement?"
8. "How do you think about the different brands within the group? You've got Brother, you've got Domino, and since March you've also got MUTOH on the wide-format side. Would a partnership like this sit under the Brother name, or could you see Domino or MUTOH using it with their own customers?"
9. "Brother has quite a history in sport. Manchester City from 1987 to 1999, Formula 1 with Jordan in the early 2000s, cycling in the UK, and you were an official supplier to the Asian Games in Nagoya this autumn. What did the business take from those? Is there anything you'd do differently, and is there still an appetite internally for sponsorship as a marketing tool?"
10. "If you had access to a Formula 1 team, which elements do you think you'd actually use the most? Is it hosting customers and dealers at races? A real role within the team that you could talk about? Content? Consumer promotions through your retailers? Or would the visibility of having the Brother name on the car be equally important?"
11. "How do you currently measure the effectiveness of your marketing? On the print side, I'd imagine it's awareness, consideration and sales through your partners, and on the industrial side, leads and orders. When you invest in something like NRF in New York or a brand campaign, what does success typically look like internally?"
12. "Is there anything that would make exploring a platform like Formula 1 particularly challenging at the moment? Your recent results mention higher parts and materials costs, tariffs and a print market that's shrinking overall. It would be helpful for us to understand those considerations early on, so we're not developing something that ultimately isn't realistic. What would be the biggest risk for you in doing something like this?"
13. "And just so we understand how best to support you, if we were to identify something interesting, how would that conversation typically progress internally? Would it be a regional decision, or would Nagoya need to be involved quite early on? Is there an existing brand or sponsorship budget it could sit within, or would it require a separate business case? And has anyone on the team been to a race, at Suzuka or anywhere else?"

## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "How relevant is Formula 1 to our target audience?"

> "I think there's actually quite a strong alignment, and for Brother it works on two levels.
>
> On the business side, Formula 1 fans index at 160 for business decision-makers, with 100 being the general population. So essentially, they're 60% more likely than the general population to be the person making a purchasing decision for a company.
>
> On the consumer and small-office side, fans index at 126 for researching products online before making a purchase, which is the highest of any sport. They also index at 200 for brand loyalty. I think that's quite relevant for a business where, according to your own results, consumables make up more than half of printing revenue.
>
> And then there's the reach. You're looking at around 827 million fans globally, 43% of them under 35, and more than 20 race weekends across five continents, including three races in the US.
>
> So, from both an audience and a geographical perspective, there's quite a natural alignment with the markets you're operating in."

### "We sell to businesses and through dealers, not to Formula 1 fans. How is that relevant?"

> "Absolutely, and I think that's where you need to look beyond the traditional sponsorship model.
>
> Around two-thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. You've also got more than 350 partner companies across the grid, many of which are bringing their own customers and leadership teams to races. And when you look at those companies, there's quite a bit of overlap with the industries you sell to. In food and drink, you've got PepsiCo at Mercedes, Heineken at Red Bull and Barilla at Racing Bulls. In logistics, DP World at McLaren and CEVA at Ferrari. In retail, M&S at Williams and Mercado Libre at Alpine. I'm not suggesting those are your customers. You'd know that far better than I would.
>
> The other part is your channel. Xerox goes to market through partners in a similar way to you, and its CMO has said they bring their channel partners to races alongside clients and prospects. For a dealer or reseller, a race weekend is quite a powerful incentive, and it gives your sales teams several hours with people they'd normally get 30 minutes with.
>
> And practically, hospitality passes generally work across the three days. So, with seven passes, you could potentially host around 20 different guests over one weekend."

### "How are other print and industrial brands using Formula 1?"

> "There are actually some quite relevant examples for Brother, including two of your direct competitors in office print. What's useful is that they've all come in at different levels."

**Xerox at Aston Martin**

> "Xerox is probably the closest example to your business. They became an Official Global Partner of Aston Martin on a multi-year agreement starting in 2025.
>
> Their CMO has been quite open about the reasons. A younger generation doesn't really know the brand, and the people who do know it think of it purely as a print company. So, for them, Formula 1 is about reaching an under-30 audience and telling a broader story.
>
> There is a technical element. Xerox provides print for the team, including the printed overlays that compare the two drivers' telemetry across a lap. But where they're really leveraging it is hospitality. They run four major client events a season, in Melbourne, Montreal, Silverstone and Austin, and they bring channel partners as well as clients. An event at the team's factory drew 200 people and, according to Xerox, had a waiting list.
>
> It's also one of the very few examples with a published number. Xerox says it's seeing a return of more than three to one on the investment, based on the relationships and opportunities it can reasonably attribute to the partnership. Their CMO was equally clear that it isn't an exact science."

**HP at Ferrari**

> "HP is at the other end of the scale. They've been Ferrari's title partner since 2024, so the team races as Scuderia Ferrari HP.
>
> Beyond the branding, HP supplies the team with PCs, workstations, printers and conferencing technology. On the print side, HP says its own teams worked on the design, printing and application of a special Ferrari livery for the US Grand Prix, and it now markets its large-format printers on the back of that.
>
> They've also turned it into a product. HP sells a limited-edition Scuderia Ferrari laptop, with each unit individually numbered.
>
> They haven't published a commercial return, so I wouldn't want to speculate on that. But it shows how a print and PC business can build a technical story, a brand story and a product around one partnership."

**Roland DG at Alpine**

> "Roland DG is a much smaller example, but I think it's quite a relevant one. They're listed as a team supplier to Alpine, and their printers were already in the team's graphics department when it rebranded from Renault to Alpine in 2021.
>
> According to Roland DG, the department prints stickers and graphics for the cars, the garage and the hospitality areas. The team carries more than 30 versions of every sticker, which comes to around 1,400 for every race, and around 80% of them are sponsor-related.
>
> There's no published commercial result, and this isn't a big branding deal. What Roland DG gets is a credible customer story from one of the most demanding print environments you could find, which it then uses in its own marketing."

**Komatsu at Williams**

> "Komatsu is a good reference as a Japanese industrial business. They came back into Formula 1 as Williams' principal partner in 2024, having made gear components for the team's title-winning cars in the 1990s.
>
> In September, they renewed, and the signing took place in Komatsu City. The role also got bigger. Komatsu engineers will be working on components for future cars, and there's a joint engineering academy that takes ten students a year.
>
> No commercial results have been published. But a renewal with a broader technical role is a positive indicator, and it shows how a Japanese manufacturer is using the sport for engineering credibility and recruitment as much as for visibility."

**Stratasys at McLaren**

> "Stratasys is on the manufacturing side. They've been McLaren's official supplier of 3D printing solutions since 2017, and they're still on the team's partner roster.
>
> It's very much a working relationship. According to McLaren and Stratasys, the equipment is used for prototypes, tooling and some production parts.
>
> Again, there are no published sales figures. But it's a good example of a manufacturing technology brand with a defined job, rather than a logo."

**Haas Automation at Haas**

> "Given you also make machine tools, Haas is worth mentioning. Gene Haas owns both the team and Haas Automation, the machine tool builder, and the reason he entered Formula 1 was to take the Haas Automation brand global.
>
> The Associated Press reported a few years ago that the business strategy had been a winner for the machine company, even while the racing itself was difficult. Obviously, owning a team is a very different proposition, but it tells you the sport can work for a machine tool brand selling to manufacturers."

**DEWALT at McLaren**

> "And then one on the product side. Stanley Black & Decker is a McLaren partner, and in 2023 its DEWALT brand launched a limited-edition range of McLaren-branded power tools and storage. It was sold exclusively through one UK trade retailer, with a prize draw for a McLaren VIP experience in Las Vegas.
>
> I think that's quite relevant for Brother, because it's a brand that sells to both trade and consumers, using the partnership to drive sales through a retail partner."

**What that adds up to**

> "I think there are three things I'd take away from all of that.
>
> Firstly, print already has a place in Formula 1, and two of your direct competitors in office print are in at very different levels. So, the question for Brother isn't really whether the category fits. It's which level and which story.
>
> Secondly, the one published return, from Xerox, came from hosting clients and channel partners rather than from the logo itself. Very few of these brands disclose their commercial returns, so I wouldn't want to read too much into one number, but it's consistent with what we see elsewhere.
>
> And thirdly, nobody is really telling the labelling, coding and tracking story. We haven't come across a labelling or coding brand on the team partner pages we've checked. That doesn't mean the category is available, but it's the part of Brother that's most distinct from what HP and Xerox are doing."

### "How could Brother leverage content?"

> "I think content could be quite an interesting opportunity for Brother, because Formula 1 gives you a very visual way of showing what are otherwise quite functional products.
>
> Typically, as part of a team partnership, you'd negotiate a number of filming days with the drivers and other members of the team, alongside distribution through the team's own channels. Formula 1 is the number one sport on social media, ahead of the NBA, with around 2.2 billion engagements, and around 61% of fans engage with content daily.
>
> One thing to be mindful of is that Formula 1 itself owns the race footage. So, the content you'd create with a team would generally be at the factory, in the garage or on dedicated filming days.
>
> There are some good examples in your own industry. Roland DG built a customer story around the head of Alpine's paint and graphics department. And Aston Martin's own website ran a long interview with Xerox's CMO about why they're in the sport.
>
> For Brother, one idea would be to follow a single part from the factory to the grid: how it's marked, packed, shipped across the world and checked back in. That's the labelling and tracking story, told through a team's operations people rather than a driver.
>
> On the consumer side, you could do something more playful around making things. A team's designers creating a design that fans can stitch, cut or print at home, for example, subject to the right licence being in place.
>
> And importantly, we wouldn't suggest that a team uses Brother products unless there's a genuine technical relationship in place. From a rights perspective, we'd want to define the filming days, the people you can access, what the team publishes and where and for how long you can use the content."

### "Why are technology and industrial brands investing in Formula 1?"

> "I think it primarily comes down to credibility and access.
>
> From a credibility perspective, a Formula 1 team is a manufacturer working to extreme deadlines, and it's been operating under a cost cap since 2021. So, if a team chooses to rely on your equipment, that's quite a strong endorsement.
>
> The second part is access. You've got global businesses, senior executives and technical leaders coming together more than 20 times a year.
>
> It's also where the money has been going. Team sponsorship has roughly doubled since 2020, to around $2.2 billion, and technology is now the largest category of team sponsorship spending, according to Reuters reporting from May.
>
> And on the manufacturing side specifically, machine tool builders have been in the sport for a long time. DMG MORI is with Red Bull, Camozzi is with Audi, and Haas Automation's owner runs his own team.
>
> For Brother, I think there's potentially a third reason, which is the one Xerox gave: reaching a younger audience that doesn't necessarily know the brand. 43% of Formula 1 fans are under 35."

### "Which teams could potentially be available?"

> "We've done some initial mapping, but we'd need to conduct a proper category conflict assessment before making any recommendations. And for Brother, it really depends on which part of the business we're talking about.
>
> On office print, Ferrari would be very challenging, given HP is the title partner and supplies printers. Aston Martin has Xerox.
>
> On wide-format and graphics, Alpine has Roland DG as a team supplier, which is relevant now that MUTOH is part of your group. HP has also worked on printing a special Ferrari livery, so large-format is covered there as well.
>
> On machine tools, Haas is owned by the founder of Haas Automation. Red Bull has DMG MORI, Audi has Camozzi and Alpine lists United Machining as a supplier. McLaren has historically bought its machine tools from Mazak, although I couldn't see Mazak on the current roster, so we'd need to check that.
>
> There are also 3D printing suppliers at McLaren, Alpine and Racing Bulls, which isn't your business, but it's the same part of the factory.
>
> On labelling and on coding and marking, we haven't identified a dedicated partner on the team partner pages we've checked. But that doesn't necessarily mean the category is available. Cadillac, for example, has 3M as its material science partner, and those definitions can be quite broad.
>
> With Mercedes, Williams and Cadillac, we haven't identified a print or machine tool brand. Again, I wouldn't read that as availability. The publicly communicated designation doesn't always reflect the full scope of the exclusivity, so we'd want to establish where there's genuine scope before introducing Brother to anyone.
>
> We'd also want to define the category around what you actually want to promote. We initially had Official Printing and Labelling Partner in mind, but printing, labelling, coding and machine tools would each sit next to different existing partners."

### "HP is Ferrari's title partner. Are we too late, or too small next to that?"

> "I wouldn't look at it that way.
>
> HP's title partnership is one team out of eleven, and it's a very different level of commitment. Xerox came in at official partner level and Roland DG is a supplier, so there's quite a range in how print businesses are involved. You certainly don't need to match HP to have a meaningful presence.
>
> On size, your first-half revenue was a little over 500 billion yen, so I don't think capacity is the question. It's more about which part of Brother is investing and what it needs the partnership to do.
>
> The more important point is differentiation. HP and Xerox are both telling an office technology story. Your story around labelling, coding and tracking on the industrial side, and around makers and small businesses on the consumer side, is quite distinct. That doesn't automatically mean the category is available, but it's worth exploring.
>
> And there's actually an advantage in not being first. You can see what Xerox has done with hospitality and what it says it's getting back, and decide what you'd do differently."

### "How do we avoid getting lost among all the other sponsors?"

> "I think that's a very fair consideration. Most Formula 1 teams have upwards of 25 commercial partners, and if the strategy is simply to put another logo on the car, there's definitely a risk of getting lost.
>
> The brands that stand out tend to have either a real job within the team or something people can actually buy. Roland DG has the first. HP has both.
>
> For Brother, I'd also separate the two audiences. On the industrial side, you don't need to reach everyone. You need a defined group of operations and packaging leaders, and your dealers, to spend time with you. On the print side, where awareness is the challenge, the visibility does matter, but I'd make it work through your resellers and retailers rather than relying on the car alone.
>
> So, I'd focus much more on the relevance of the story and how you use it through your own channels than on the size of the branding."

### "Why would a Formula 1 team be interested in Brother?"

> "I wouldn't want to speak on behalf of any team, particularly as several already have print or machine tool partners. But I think there are a few potentially interesting angles.
>
> Firstly, the scale and stability of the business. Your forecast for this financial year is a trillion yen in revenue, you've got close to 40,000 employees and you're present in more than 40 countries and regions. Teams are generally looking for partners who can grow with them over several years.
>
> Secondly, the network you bring. You sell to food and drink producers, logistics businesses and retailers, and you've got dealers and resellers around the world. Teams value partners that bring a wider commercial network with them.
>
> Thirdly, there's the consumer side. Your products are in homes and small offices, and a licensed product or design would give a team another way to reach fans, with a royalty attached.
>
> And then there's Japan. With Honda and Toyota both heavily involved, it's a market teams are paying attention to.
>
> But we'd need to establish whether those elements are genuinely attractive to each individual team rather than assuming there's a need."

### "Would a team actually use our products?"

> "Potentially, but I think it's important to separate the commercial conversation from the technical one.
>
> Teams clearly do print, label and ship a great deal. Alpine's graphics department and Aston Martin's telemetry overlays are two examples. But they already have equipment and suppliers in place, some of which come with contractual rights.
>
> Ultimately, the decision to use a product sits with the team's operations and IT people, not with the commercial department. They're working within a cost cap, so being a sponsor doesn't automatically mean your equipment gets used.
>
> What we could do is facilitate those conversations and establish whether there's a genuine use case, whether that's labelling in the stores and garage, mobile printing at the track or something else.
>
> If the technical and commercial elements come together, that's the strongest outcome because you've then got a credible case study. But equally, if product use is essential from your perspective, that's something we'd want to establish early on, before progressing too far commercially."

### "What commercial rights would be most relevant for Brother?"

> "I think Brother is quite an interesting case, because you're predominantly a B2B business but with consumer products that people know. For me, there are seven areas worth looking at, and I'd rank them slightly differently from a pure consumer brand.
>
> The first is hospitality. That's access to Paddock Club, garage tours and factory visits for your industrial customers, your dealers and your channel partners. It's where the one published return in your industry has come from.
>
> The second is a technical role and the right to talk about it. If there's a genuine opportunity for a team to use your products, we'd want you to have the contractual ability to communicate that through case studies, sales materials and access to the relevant people in the team. It can't be promised, but where it's possible, it's very valuable.
>
> The third is B2B introductions. That's access to the team's other partners, whether through partner events or facilitated introductions. Given how many food, drink, logistics and retail businesses are on the grid, I'd want that properly defined in the agreement rather than presented as a general benefit.
>
> The fourth is intellectual property and designation rights. That's the ability to call yourselves an official partner and use the team's name and marks across your marketing, packaging, trade show stands and, importantly, the materials your resellers and retailers use.
>
> The fifth is content, with engineers and operations people as much as drivers.
>
> The sixth is consumer promotions and licensing. Prize draws through your retailers, experiences for dealers' customers and potentially licensed designs or a limited-edition product. I'll come back to how licensing works.
>
> And the seventh is traditional branding on the car, team kit and garage. For the industrial businesses, I wouldn't prioritize it. But if the main objective is brand awareness with small businesses and younger buyers, it would move up the list.
>
> We'd also need to consider category exclusivity and territory, because those two elements influence the valuation more than anything else. And given how Brother is organized, a regional set of rights might be more relevant than a global one.
>
> Ultimately, we'd want to build a package around the rights you're actually going to use, with each one quantified in the agreement."

### "Could we sell team-branded products or designs? How does licensing actually work?"

> "Yeah, in principle, and I think it's quite a natural fit for parts of Brother. There is an important distinction, though, between a sponsorship and a licence.
>
> A sponsorship gives you the right to associate your brand with a team and use that in your marketing. A licence gives you the right to make and sell products that carry the team's intellectual property. Typically, that operates through a royalty on sales, usually alongside a minimum guarantee.
>
> The positive thing is that this isn't new to you. Your Stellaire embroidery machine comes with 101 Disney designs built in, your Disney ScanNCut has 182, and you sell additional designs through your own download store. So, team logos and liveries as embroidery or cutting designs would essentially be the same model with a new licensor.
>
> I'd see three potential routes. The first is licensed designs, as I mentioned. The second is a limited-edition product, similar to what HP has done with its numbered Ferrari laptop or DEWALT with its McLaren tools. That could be a label maker, a sewing machine or a printer. The third is personalisation, using your embroidery machines and garment printers to put a fan's name on a product at an event.
>
> A licence would typically cover the product categories, the territories, the duration, the royalty and minimum guarantee, and the approval process. The team would need to approve designs and packaging, so it's something you'd plan over months rather than weeks.
>
> There are also a couple of things to be mindful of. The team grants rights to its own marks. If you wanted to use the Formula 1 brand itself, that's a separate conversation with Formula 1, and a driver's name or likeness generally needs the driver's agreement as well.
>
> And where a product touches clothing, we'd need to check the team's apparel partner. adidas, for example, is Audi's official apparel partner and produces its teamwear and merchandise, so embroidered or printed garments could overlap with those rights."

### "Where could we sell those products? Could we personalise merchandise at the races?"

> "There are potentially three channels I'd look at.
>
> The first, and probably the most important, is your own. Your dealers, your retailers and your own online and download stores. That's where you already have the relationships, and it gives your partners something exclusive to sell.
>
> The second is the team's own retail channels, subject to the agreement.
>
> The third is the race itself, and that's where it becomes more complicated. Formula 1 granted a PUMA subsidiary exclusive trackside vending rights for licensed products and team merchandise at a number of races. So, you wouldn't simply be able to set up a personalisation stand at a Grand Prix.
>
> What you could potentially do is activate around the race rather than inside the circuit: in a dealer's store in the host city, in the team's hospitality, or at your own customer events. DEWALT's McLaren range is a useful reference. It was sold exclusively through one retail partner, with a prize draw attached.
>
> If I were looking at a sensible first step, I'd probably start with one set of licensed designs or one limited-edition product, with one team, in one or two markets, and see how it performs before expanding it."

### "What are the different ways we could enter Formula 1?"

> "I think people often associate Formula 1 with a multi-million-euro team sponsorship, but that's not necessarily the only way to get involved. There are a few different routes we could explore.
>
> The first is a hospitality-led approach. We could identify a handful of races that align with your key markets and build a customer and dealer programme around those. Looking at next year's calendar, Suzuka is 9 to 11 April, Miami is at the start of May, Silverstone is 2 to 4 July, and Austin and Las Vegas are in October and November.
>
> The second is a content-led partnership, working with a team or an individual driver on a defined content proposition, subject to the relevant rights.
>
> The third is a supplier-level partnership. That's something more focused with one team, potentially involving an official designation, a technical role, hospitality and a smaller branding element. That's broadly where Roland DG sits with Alpine.
>
> And the fourth is an official team partnership, a broader commercial relationship with more extensive rights and visibility, which is closer to what Xerox has with Aston Martin.
>
> For Brother, I'd potentially add a fifth, which is licensing-led: starting with a product or a set of designs.
>
> You don't necessarily need to commit to a full team partnership from day one. There could be quite a strong argument for starting with something focused in one region, demonstrating the value internally and then scaling it."

### "What kind of investment are we talking about?"

> "It really depends on the route we take. For an entry-level team partnership or supplier-level arrangement, you're generally looking at the low single-digit millions annually, with more comprehensive official partnerships sitting above that.
>
> If we're looking at selected hospitality, a content-led proposition or an individual driver, the investment could be significantly lower. Licensing works differently again, because it's generally built around a royalty and a minimum guarantee.
>
> Looking at the scale of the business, I don't think the primary question is capacity. It's more about which budget it would sit in, regional or global, and whether there's a clear commercial rationale.
>
> So, rather than putting an arbitrary number in front of you, I'd much rather understand what you're looking to achieve and then come back with a few different scenarios, so you can see what different investment levels actually deliver."

### "We're a Japanese company. How relevant are Japan and Suzuka?"

> "I think Japan is actually one of the more interesting parts of the story at the moment.
>
> Honda came back this season as Aston Martin's works engine partner. Toyota, through Toyota Gazoo Racing, is now the title partner of Haas, which races as TGR Haas F1 Team. And Komatsu renewed with Williams in September. So, Japanese industry is more visible in the sport than it has been for quite some time.
>
> Suzuka is also effectively your home race. Next year it's 9 to 11 April, it's one of the Sprint weekends, and from Nagoya it's about 50 minutes by train and then around 20 minutes by bus. For hosting customers, dealers or your own leadership, that's quite a natural starting point.
>
> The one thing I'd be careful with is building anything around a driver. There isn't a Japanese driver with a full-time race seat this season. Yuki Tsunoda is Red Bull's reserve and Ryo Hirakawa is the reserve at Haas, and I haven't seen a Japanese driver confirmed in a race seat for next year.
>
> And if the decision or the budget ultimately sits in Nagoya, a Japanese link might also make the internal conversation easier. But I wouldn't choose a team on that basis alone."

### "Brother was in Formula 1 before, with Jordan. What's different now?"

> "Yeah, quite a lot, actually. As I understand it, Brother was with Tyrrell and Williams in the late 1990s and then on the Jordan cars from 2000 to 2003. At that point, the deal was built around the Brother name on the cars and the drivers' overalls.
>
> The audience has changed significantly since then. Formula 1 now has around 827 million fans, up 63% since 2018. Around 42% are women, compared to 37% in 2018, and 43% are under 35.
>
> The US has also become a major market, with three races, two American teams and Apple broadcasting the sport there.
>
> The teams are in a different position too. There's been a cost cap since 2021, which has made them far more stable businesses, and team sponsorship has roughly doubled since 2020.
>
> And the way brands use it has changed. The partnerships that work now tend to have a defined job, a hospitality programme and content behind them, rather than relying on visibility alone.
>
> As a small aside, the team that raced as Jordan is, several owners later, now Aston Martin."

### "Decisions are split between Nagoya and our regions. Could a partnership be regional?"

> "Yes, potentially. Territory is one of the main variables in any agreement, and it's one of the two things that most influence the price, alongside exclusivity.
>
> A set of rights limited to Europe or the Americas would generally require a lower investment than global rights, and some teams do have regional designations.
>
> There are some practical limits. The car is seen globally, so branding on the car is difficult to restrict to one region. Where regional rights really apply is in how you use the team's name and marks, the content, the promotions and the hospitality.
>
> So, one route would be for one region to start with rights for its own markets, built around a few races, with an option to extend if other regions or Nagoya want to come in later. We'd just want to be clear from the beginning who owns it internally."

### "Why should we work with SPORTFIVE rather than approach a team directly?"

> "You absolutely could approach the teams directly. I think the difference is that naturally, when you speak to a team, they're going to position their own proposition as the best opportunity for your business. Whereas we're in a position to look across the entire market and assess those opportunities against your objectives.
>
> We understand how the teams are commercially positioned, how their rights packages are structured, what the market valuations look like and where there could potentially be better value. So, we can support you with benchmarking, category conflict assessments, evaluating competing offers and ensuring you're paying fair market value for the rights you're acquiring.
>
> In your case, I think the conflict mapping is particularly valuable. You've got HP, Xerox and Roland DG on the print side, several machine tool builders, and licensing rights that are often managed separately from sponsorship. Someone needs to check each team quietly before Brother is introduced to anyone.
>
> We've gone through a similar process with RUCKUS Networks, which is now the official networking partner of Haas, with a genuine technical application within the team.
>
> And from a commercial perspective, our agency fees are covered by the rights holders, so there's no additional agency fee for Brother."

### "Which team would you recommend for Brother?"

> "I think it's probably a little premature for us to recommend a particular team.
>
> If the priority is hosting industrial customers and dealers in North America, that might lead us towards certain teams. If it's brand awareness with small businesses in Europe, that could lead us in a different direction. If it's a technical role and a case study, it depends on where there's genuinely scope. And if the Japanese link matters internally, that's another consideration.
>
> We'd also need to know which part of Brother is leading it, because the existing partners look quite different for office print, wide-format, labelling and machine tools.
>
> So, once we've got a better understanding of your priorities, we can assess the market properly and come back with a few options that we genuinely believe are relevant. And it might not necessarily be a team partnership at all."

### "How would we measure the commercial return?"

> "I think the important thing is to establish the measurement framework before entering into the partnership, rather than trying to justify the investment afterwards. For Brother, I'd probably look at four areas.
>
> Firstly, the customers and dealers you host. Who attended, what opportunities followed, and what orders you can reasonably attribute. That's essentially how Xerox arrives at its figure. It tracks the people who attend, the prospects, the opportunities created and the deals that follow.
>
> Secondly, sales through your channel. If you run a promotion with resellers or retailers around the partnership, you can compare those markets and those periods against ones where you didn't.
>
> Thirdly, brand measures. Awareness and consideration among small business and home office buyers, particularly younger ones, in the markets where you're using the partnership.
>
> And fourthly, if there's a licensed product or set of designs, the units sold and designs downloaded.
>
> Airwallex is quite an interesting example. During its relationship with McLaren, it reported 50% year-on-year revenue growth across Europe and the Americas, and 57% growth in the UK driven by new customers.
>
> I'm not suggesting that growth was directly attributable to the partnership, but those are the types of business outcomes we'd want to track alongside the investment."

### "What does SPORTFIVE do outside Formula 1?"

> "Formula 1 is obviously my primary focus, but as an agency, we're active across pretty much the entire sports landscape, with more than 60 offices across over 20 countries.
>
> Football is a significant part of our business. We've been working with Borussia Dortmund since 1999, and that relationship has been extended through 2034. We're also the Australian Open's lead commercial partnerships agency across Europe, alongside our work across other sports.
>
> Internally, we're quite collaborative across the different departments, and we've also got a dedicated business intelligence team supporting us with audience insights, research, strategic planning and partnership evaluation.
>
> So, if we genuinely felt that another sport or platform could deliver more effectively against your objectives, we're in a position to explore that as well. Again, it comes back to taking a brand-led approach rather than being tied to a particular property."

### "What would be the biggest risk for Brother?"

> "I think the biggest risk would be entering into a partnership without being very clear on which part of Brother it's for and what it needs to deliver. You could invest quite significantly in visibility, but if there's no clear plan around customers, dealers or product, it becomes quite difficult to justify.
>
> The second is ownership. If a partnership is signed by one region or one business unit and the others don't use it, you end up paying for rights that sit on the shelf. So, I'd want to be clear on who owns it internally before anything else.
>
> The third is category conflicts. Your direct competitors are at Ferrari and Aston Martin, Roland DG is at Alpine, and several teams have machine tool partners.
>
> The fourth is building the business case around a team using your products before that's been validated with the people who'd actually make that decision.
>
> And then, more broadly, I think we need to establish whether Formula 1 is genuinely the most effective platform for the objective. If the sole objective is selling more home printers at the lowest possible cost, retail promotions and digital channels are probably more efficient.
>
> But if you're looking to spend time with industrial buyers and dealers, build awareness with a younger audience across Europe and North America, and give the brand a broader story, that's where Formula 1 becomes quite interesting."

# ispace x SPORTFIVE: F1 Discovery Call Script

**Call type:** First call / discovery, listening-led. Goal: open warm and short, say why ispace caught our eye, make the goal plain (understand if it makes sense, not sell), let them talk, fill the brief with sharp questions, and show how this sport could fit a lunar exploration company. Agree next steps only if the conversation earns them.

**How to use this script:** Every spoken block is written the way you'd say it out loud, contractions and all. Don't read it word for word; glance at the bold anchors and talk. This is a room of engineers and mission people who have lived through two landing attempts that didn't succeed, in public. Be warm, precise and respectful. Never make light of the missions, never hype, and let them talk more than you. If English is their second language, slow down and keep sentences short.

## Prep notes (not spoken)

### Who ispace is (verified 20 October 2026; re-verify on call day)
- Lunar exploration company, headquartered in Tokyo, founded in 2010 by Takeshi Hakamada, still founder and CEO and the largest shareholder at about 8%. Roots in Team HAKUTO, a finalist in the Google Lunar XPRIZE. Listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange Growth Market since April 2023 (ticker 9348). Entities in Japan, Luxembourg (ispace EUROPE, CEO Julien Lamamy) and the US (ispace-U.S., CEO Elizabeth Kryst). "More than 300 employees worldwide", per their own releases. CFO is Jumpei Nozaki.
- What they do, in plain words: they build lunar landers and small rovers and sell space on them. Customers pay to send payloads (instruments, experiments, even artwork) to lunar orbit and the surface. Newer lines: a lunar-orbit relay satellite for communications and positioning ("Lunar Connect"), and, announced in July 2026, a service using 500 kg of booked space on SpaceX's Starship with their own "Mobile Cargo System" to move cargo on the surface (as early as 2030, per their announcement).
- Mission history, said plainly: Mission 1 launched in December 2022 and reached lunar orbit; the landing attempt in April 2023 failed and the lander was lost. ispace traced it to landing software that misjudged the altitude over a crater rim. Mission 2 ("RESILIENCE", launched January 2025) also reached lunar orbit; the landing attempt in June 2025 failed and the lander was lost, which ispace traced to a hardware fault in the laser range finder. No soft landing yet. Treat this with respect: people on the call may have built those landers.
- What's next (all dates are ispace's own plans as stated March to August 2026; they have moved before): Mission 2.5, a lunar-orbit relay satellite, as early as 2027. Mission 3, the new ULTRA lander on Mitsubishi Heavy Industries' H3 rocket, in 2028 (originally agreed for end of 2027; the change is pending METI approval). Mission 4, a high-precision landing near the lunar south pole, in 2029. Mission 5 targeted for 2029 to 2030. In March 2026 they merged their Japanese and US lander designs into ULTRA and switched to a flight-proven engine. In July 2026 NASA and Draper ended the CP-12 lunar task order that ispace-U.S. was building the lander for; NASA cited delays and a landing projected for 2030 or later.
- The numbers (from ispace's results, May and August 2026): fiscal year to March 2026, net sales ¥3.3bn (down 26%), "project revenue" (sales plus grants) ¥5.9bn, net loss ¥8.2bn. For the year to March 2027 they forecast project revenue of ¥9bn and a net loss of ¥13bn. Cash ¥25.7bn and net assets ¥8.7bn at end of June; they call strengthening the financial position "a key management priority". Rough dollars at their own mid-2026 rate of about ¥160: sales around $20M, last year's loss around $50M, this year's forecast loss around $80M.

## Part 3: The smart questions (12 to 15 minutes)

*[Conversation starters, not a form. Ask one, stay quiet, follow what they give you. Their answers become the brief.]*

**Open the story**
- "Tell me where ispace is right now, in your own words. What's going well, and what's the next hill to climb?"
- "What are the key focus areas for the next twelve months? What comes up in every leadership conversation?"

**The direction, and where this could play a role**
- "How do you see something like Formula 1, or sport at all, maybe playing a role in where the brand is headed? Even a rough first instinct helps; there's no wrong answer."
- "And as a business, where could you imagine it adding value: winning payload customers, keeping partners close, hiring engineers, the brand in Japan?"

**Customers and partners**
- "Who buys space on your missions today: agencies, universities, companies? And which of those groups do you most want more of?"
- "Your partner program works a lot like a race team's. What do your partners get from you today, and what do they keep asking for?"

**The end goal**
- "Zooming out: if everything goes right over the next three years, what does ispace look like?"
- "And what would make a partnership like this a failure in your eyes? That answer usually tells us more than the first one."

**Evaluation and success**
- "If you ever did something like this, on what basis would it be judged internally, and by whom?"
- "What would it need to deliver for you to call it a success after year one? New payload contracts, partner renewals, something else?"

**Markets**
- "Is the growth story Japan first, or are the US and Europe just as important now? The calendar runs five continents, with a race in Japan and three in the US."

**Brand and marketing today**
- "In Japan, people know ispace. How do you keep the brand strong between missions, when there's no launch to talk about?"
- "Is the bigger job right now top of the funnel or bottom of the funnel: being known by companies that have never heard of ispace, or turning the customers already talking to you into signed contracts?"

**Budget and timing**
- "The unromantic question: is there a budget range in mind for something like this? I ask early because it decides which doors we even look at, and it saves us both time. I know every yen is going into the next mission."
- "And timing: is this a now conversation or a next-year conversation? Both are fine; they just change what we bring back."

**Decision process**
- "Apart from you, who'd be part of this decision? The CEO, the board, the team that runs your partner program?"

**Hospitality**
- "Last one, my favorite. If I gave you a paddock weekend tomorrow, who would you bring, and why those people? That answer usually tells me what a partnership is really for."

*[If they hesitate on budget:]*

> "No problem, you don't need a number today. If we get to recommendations, you'll see options at different levels, including the smallest sensible ones, with what each level buys."

## Part 4: How Formula 1 works, and where it could fit (8 to 10 minutes)

*[Calibrate to their answer from Part 2. Keep it simple and precise; this room respects engineering, not slogans. Invite questions: "Stop me anytime."]*

### 4.1 The sport in thirty seconds

> "Quick picture of how the sport is set up, because it explains a brand's options.
>
> Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company: the calendar, the broadcasts, the races, including the Japanese Grand Prix at Suzuka. The FIA writes the rules. And then eleven teams, including two American ones, Haas and Cadillac, each an independent business with its own partners.
>
> So a brand can enter at three levels: the sport, a team, or a driver personally. They come in very different sizes, and for ispace I'd be straight from the start: the interesting doors are the small ones."

### 4.2 What a team partnership includes

> "What do you get as a partner? Five parts, quickly.
>
> **Branding:** your name on the car, the suits, the garage, around 24 weekends a year, 1.8 billion total audience last season.
>
> **The official title:** Official Something Partner of the team, usable in all your own marketing and every customer conversation.
>
> **Hospitality:** paddock access and the Paddock Club, where 66% of guests are senior executives. One detail people miss: passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in a weekend.
>
> **Content:** access to the team's channels and drivers. This is the number one sport in the world for social engagement, 2.2 billion engagements, ahead of the NBA.
>
> **People:** driver appearances, filming days, hot laps for your top partners and customers.
>
> And around it all, the business platform: 25-plus partners per team, 350-plus across the grid. That room is a market in itself."

### 4.3 The audience

*[Three audiences for ispace: the room of partners and customers, the public, and the engineers they want to hire.]*

> "Who you reach. For ispace I'd think about three groups.
>
> **The room.** Formula 1 has the highest concentration of business decision makers in sport, index 160 against 100 for the general population. In the Paddock Club, two thirds are senior executives: the kind of people who sign a partner agreement or a payload contract. At Suzuka, that's the Japanese business world in one paddock.
>
> **The public.** 827 million fans worldwide, 43% under 35, and 61% engage with the sport's content every day. My bet is that people who love the engineering of a race car love the idea of a Moon landing too. That's where future fans of your missions come from.
>
> **The engineers.** This is the most engineering-obsessed sport on earth. The people you want to hire in Tokyo, Luxembourg and the US watch it because it's their own discipline at full speed.
>
> And the stage is already set for serious technology: AWS is the sport's cloud and machine learning provider, Oracle's name is on Red Bull, HP's on Ferrari, and Microsoft is reportedly with Mercedes. The room is used to taking engineering seriously."

### 4.4 Why this could fit ispace

> "Why we thought this was worth a call. Three reasons, and you tell me if they hold.
>
> **One, the same hard thing.** Formula 1 and ispace both do engineering at the edge. Hard limits on money and computing, hardware that has to work when it counts, and every decision made on data streaming back from a machine you can't touch. Every team in this sport has had days where something failed in front of the whole world. The good ones read the data, fix it, and come back. Nobody in that paddock thinks a hard day ends the story. That's a story you can tell with real authority.
>
> **Two, the room you already sell to.** Your business runs on partners and payload customers, many of them big Japanese companies. The Japanese Grand Prix at Suzuka puts that business world in one paddock, once a year. With seven passes you can host twenty different guests across the weekend. That's partner care and new business in one place.
>
> **Three, a model you already know.** ispace runs on partners who want to stand next to a hard technical project. Formula 1 teams run on the same idea, with 25-plus partners each. You'd be stepping into a world whose rules you already understand, and you might meet companies that fit your own partner program too.
>
> One honest note on size. You're a listed company putting every yen into the next landing, and your shareholders will see whatever you spend. So whatever we recommend will be small and easy to explain: hospitality, content, or later a seat paid partly in what you do best. If the honest answer is 'not yet', we'll say exactly that."

### 4.5 Three early sketches (ideas, not proposals)

*[Offer as sketches; their reaction feeds the brief. Sketch three only if the mood is right, and never make the landing the punchline.]*

> "To make it concrete, three sketches, all sized small on purpose.
>
> **One, the Suzuka weekend.** A small hospitality program at the Japanese Grand Prix: your partners, your payload customers and the companies you'd like to sign next, hosted properly, with your leadership in the room. Seven passes, twenty guests, no logo required at all.
>
> **Two, mission control meets the pit wall.** A content series on how two kinds of engineers work when the machine is out of reach: a race team's pit wall on a Sunday, and your mission control in Tokyo. Live data, pressure, decisions in seconds, and what you do after a bad day. Made with a team or a driver, in Japanese and English.
>
> **Three, a seat paid partly in what you do best.** If the brief earns it, later on: a supplier-style seat with one team where part of the fee is paid in kind. For example, a small item from the team flies on one of your future missions, and the team's fans follow the journey from launch. Product plus cash, so the cash stays small. Only if your payload rules and the team's appetite allow it."

### Q1. "What are comparable brands doing in Formula 1, and why are they using it?"

> "Good question, and there's a clear pattern.
>
> Serious technology brands have moved into this sport in a wave: Oracle's name is on Red Bull, HP's on Ferrari, Atlassian took the title at Williams, Microsoft reportedly signed with Mercedes in January, and AWS has been the sport's official cloud and machine learning provider since 2018.
>
> Why do they do it? Three jobs, usually. Trust: putting their name next to the hardest engineering in sport so buyers take them seriously. The room: hosting the exact executives they sell to, race after race. And proof: the working partnerships, where the brand visibly does a job for the team. CommScope's RUCKUS brand powers the Haas team's operations with a live showcase in the garage; that deal is marketing you can demo.
>
> And it can show up in revenue. Airwallex, a fintech at McLaren: during the partnership their revenue grew 50% year on year across EMEA and the Americas, and UK revenue grew 57% from new customers. A very different business from yours, but it shows what the room can do when the brief is right."

### Q2. "How do F1 teams drive introductions in business?"

> "Very concretely. It's a managed process, not a cocktail party.
>
> Every team runs a family of 25-plus partners, and the team's partnership department actively connects them: compliant introductions, partner summits, business forums on race weekends, private dinners. Add the hospitality rights, and you choose who sits at your table: your own partners and target customers, hosted at the race.
>
> For ispace that's two jobs at once: looking after the partners who already back you, and meeting the companies who might put something on your next lander. That's a pipeline, not a billboard."

### Q3. "How is ROI measured in Formula 1?"

> "Four ways, and you agree the framework upfront.
>
> **Media value:** independent firms like Nielsen track every second your brand appears, across TV and digital, and price it against your fee.
>
> **Brand tracking:** awareness and trust, measured before and after, in the audience you care about: Japanese business leaders, your customers abroad, and the engineers you want to hire.
>
> **Business pipeline:** introductions, meetings and contracts closed through the platform. That's how Airwallex could point at 50% revenue growth during their McLaren years.
>
> **Content performance:** reach and engagement on everything you build.
>
> For ispace I'd weight it to what moves your business: partner renewals, payload conversations started, contracts signed. Not impressions."

### Q4. "Which entry would fit us best?"

> "Today, instinct only: a hospitality weekend at Suzuka, a content idea, and maybe later a small seat paid partly in kind. A driver-level program is another small door worth a look. I wouldn't respect you by pretending that's a recommendation.
>
> The mapping after this call turns it into names, numbers and a sequence against your brief. And if the honest answer is 'not yet', that's what we'll bring back."

### Q5. "What does a partnership cost?"

> "The price follows the rights. Rule of thumb: entry-level and supplier positions with a team start in the low single-digit millions of dollars a year, official partner tiers sit above that, and driver, content and pure hospitality programs sit below, some well below.
>
> Why expensive? Eleven teams for the whole world, one seat per category, three-to-five-year deals. Scarcity.
>
> For ispace, I'd start at the small end: hospitality and content cost a fraction of a team seat, and paying part of a seat in kind can bring the cash down further. And our advice is free either way: the rights holders pay our fee."

### Q6. "Why work with SPORTFIVE? Could we not go to a team directly?"

> "You could. Two reasons brands use us anyway.
>
> One, information. A team sells you its own story; we put all eleven next to each other: inventory, pricing, appetite, and what each would mean for ispace specifically. For more than twelve years we worked exclusively with Mercedes and Ferrari; we gave that up deliberately to sit on the brand's side, across the whole grid. The neutral seat is the product.
>
> Two, process. We build the business case, benchmark the price and negotiate on your behalf, because we do it every week. For a company watching every yen, that matters.
>
> And since the rights holders pay our fee, it costs you nothing to use us. There's no version where you pay more."

### Q7. "Are any of our competitors already in Formula 1?"

> "I won't wing that from memory; this space moves and you deserve the precise picture. Aerospace and technology brands are active around the sport, and some of the big Japanese companies in your own partner family may have motorsport ties of their own, which matters if we ever host together.
>
> Mapping the space, aerospace and satellite categories across all eleven teams and the series is part of the analysis after this call. You'd see who's in, who's out, and where the open positions are."

### Q8. "We're focused on getting to the Moon. Why would we spend on sport?"

> "Fair, and maybe you shouldn't. That's a real possible outcome of this process, and if the numbers say so, we'll say so.
>
> But here's the case worth testing. Getting to the Moon is the mission; selling payload space and keeping partners on board is what pays for it. Your partners and customers are big companies, and they need reasons to stay close between launches. A small, well-run weekend at Suzuka, or a content idea about engineering at the edge, is a tool for exactly that job. It's not a marketing splurge; it's partner care and new business, in a room where those people already want to be.
>

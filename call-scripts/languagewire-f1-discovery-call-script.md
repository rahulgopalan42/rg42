# LanguageWire x SPORTFIVE: F1 Discovery Call Script

**Call type:** First call / discovery. Goal: introduce yourself and SPORTFIVE, understand LanguageWire's objectives, fill out the brief, show where language fits the world's most global sport, agree next steps.

**How to use this script:** Every spoken block is written the way you would say it out loud. Do not read it word for word. Glance at the bold anchors, then talk. Short sentences, slow pace, warm tone. Keep it simple and concrete for a non-native English listener. Let them talk more than you.

## Part 4: How Formula 1 partnerships work (8 to 10 minutes)

*[Calibrate to their answer from Part 2. If they know the sport, compress. Invite questions as you go: "Stop me anytime."]*

### 4.1 The sport in thirty seconds

> "Let me give you a simple picture of how the sport is set up, because it explains the options you have.
>
> Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company. They own the commercial side: the calendar, the broadcast deals, the races.
>
> The FIA is the regulator. They write the rules.
>
> And then there are eleven teams. From this coming season it is eleven, including two American teams, Haas and Cadillac. Each team is an independent business with its own owners and its own partnerships.
>
> So a brand can partner at three levels: with the sport itself, with a team, or with a driver personally. Most brands enter through a team, and I can explain why when we get there."

### 4.2 What a team partnership includes

> "What do you get as a partner? Think of it in five parts.
>
> **One, branding.** Your logo on the car, the race suits, the garage. Around 24 race weekends a year. Last season the total audience was 1.8 billion, and the average race reached 76 million people.
>
> **Two, the official title.** You become, for example, the Official Localization Partner of a team. You use the team's name, logo and imagery in your marketing and your sales conversations.
>
> **Three, hospitality.** Paddock access, the Paddock Club, garage tours, time with the drivers. 66% of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. For a company selling to global marketers, every race is a room full of buyers.
>
> And one detail people miss. A Grand Prix runs over three days, and your passes work every day. Seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend, and hundreds across a season.
>
> **Four, content and digital.** Access to the team's channels and drivers for content. Formula 1 is the number one sport in the world for social engagement, and in the sport's own survey, 61% of fans engage with F1 content every day. Content is the bloodstream of this sport, which is exactly your territory.
>
> **Five, people.** Driver appearances, filming days, team leadership at your events.
>
> And around all of this sits the business platform: every team carries 25 or more partners, more than 350 companies across the grid, meeting each other all season. That room is your pipeline."

### 4.3 The audience

*[Pure B2B framing for LanguageWire: the buyers first, the global fan picture as the product story.]*

> "Now, who you reach. Two audiences matter for you.
>
> **The buyers.** Formula 1 has the highest concentration of business decision makers in sport: fans index at 160 for management and strategy roles, where the average person is 100. In the Paddock Club, 66% of guests are senior executives. And the paddock itself holds 350 partner companies. Here is the line I want you to remember: every one of those companies activates this sport in twenty-plus countries a season. Every one of them has a localization problem. Your buyers are not just watching this sport. They are working inside it.
>
> **The fans, because they are your product story.** 827 million people, spread across every continent, most of them not native English speakers. And the sport's own survey says digital fandom is converting to real demand fastest in the US, India and Brazil. Two of those three markets do not speak English first. The growth of this sport is, quite literally, a translation project."

### 4.4 Why this could fit LanguageWire

> "Let me tell you why I find this opportunity interesting for LanguageWire. Three reasons.
>
> **First, the language gap is real and visible.** The most global sport on earth still publishes mostly in English, while its growth comes from markets that speak Spanish, Portuguese, Japanese, Hindi. A team that speaks to its fans in their own languages will feel the difference immediately, and the partner that makes it happen owns that story.
>
> **Second, the paddock is a localization prospect list.** 350 partner companies, each activating campaigns across twenty-plus countries every season: hospitality invitations, content, press, product launches, all needing language. You would stand next to those companies as a peer, with a live case study running in the same sport.
>
> **Third, the model is proven.** Teams take working technology from partners and give the showcase in return: RUCKUS runs the wireless at Haas, Salesforce built the sport's AI fan companion, Globant builds F1's digital fan experiences. A language platform doing the team's content is the same shape of deal, and it is a quiet category today.
>
> One last thing on value. All of this sits on very small supply: eleven teams for the entire world, one seat per category, three to five year deals. Small supply, growing demand. That is why these partnerships hold their value, at every level of entry."

### 4.5 Three activation ideas (examples, not proposals)

*[Offer these as sketches to make it concrete. Let them react; their reaction feeds the brief.]*

> "To make it concrete, three sketches. Early thoughts, nothing more.
>
> **One, the team in five languages.** LanguageWire localizes a team's site, social and fan communications into its top growth-market languages, Spanish, Portuguese, Japanese. The engagement lift becomes a public case study every quarter. The team grows its fanbase; you get the most visible localization proof in sport.
>
> **Two, speak their language.** At every race, the drivers greet the host country in its own language, subtitled and adapted by LanguageWire. Small, warm, endlessly shareable, and it makes the partner unmissable 24 times a year.
>
> **Three, the marketers' table.** A LanguageWire roundtable on global content at selected races, hosting the marketing and content leaders of the team's other partners and your own key accounts. The paddock brings them in; you own the conversation."

### Q1. "Are we too small for Formula 1?"

> "I would not think about it as size. I would think about it as fit, and about choosing the right level.
>
> The sport has entry points well below the headline numbers: supplier and technology tiers, official designations, regional rights. And partnerships where the partner's product does real work for the team are often structured differently, because the team gets value beyond the fee.
>
> Some of the best stories in F1 are companies that entered at the right level and grew with it. Airwallex came in as a scale-up, and during the partnership revenue grew 50% year on year across EMEA and the Americas.
>
> The brief tells us what is realistic. Our job is to find the level where the numbers truly work for LanguageWire, and to be honest with you if they do not."

### Q2. "What is the difference between partnering with F1 itself, a team, or a driver?"

> "Three doors into the sport.
>
> **The series, Formula 1 itself.** You partner with the sport: branding at every race, global reach, neutrality. Bigger deals, premium prices. Salesforce and Globant sit here.
>
> **A team.** The story lives here: the car, the drivers, the content, the hospitality, the partner ecosystem. Many more entry points and price levels, and for you the natural home of an embedded localization showcase. For most brands, this is the right door.
>
> **A driver personally.** The smallest door: personal channels, appearance days, campaign rights. Useful as a face, but no team rights and no paddock hospitality.
>
> The brief will tell us which door, and at what level."

### Q3. "Teams have thirty partners. How does one brand stand out?"

> "Fair question. Three answers.
>
> First, structure. Partners sit in clear tiers, and your tier defines exactly what you own: positions, days, content, hospitality. All contractual.
>
> Second, exclusivity. Within your category you are alone. The team cannot sign your competitor.
>
> Third, attention is won through activation, not logo size. A driver greeting Sao Paulo in Portuguese, made possible by you, is worth more than a bigger sticker. The partners people remember are the ones whose product visibly does something.
>
> And the access, the hospitality, the introductions: those are private rights. They never get diluted by other partners."

### Q4. "How do teams help with B2B introductions?"

> "Very concretely. This is a managed process, not a cocktail party.
>
> Every team runs a family of at least 25 partners, and the team's partnership department actively connects them: compliant introductions, partner summits, business forums on race weekends, private dinners. And it continues off the calendar at events like Web Summit and CES.
>
> Two examples from our own work. CommScope's RUCKUS brand at Haas: enterprise wireless powering the team's operations, with a live showcase in the garage; our agency ran the grid-wide process. And Airwallex at McLaren: during the partnership, revenue grew 50% year on year across EMEA and the Americas, and UK revenue grew 57% from new customer acquisition.
>
> For LanguageWire: 350 companies in that paddock, all running global campaigns, meeting you as a fellow partner. That is a pipeline, not a billboard."

### Q5. "Could a team not handle translation themselves, or with AI?"

> "They could try, and that is true of almost everything partners provide. Teams could build their own wireless, their own software, their own AI. They do not, because they are racing teams, and every hour goes into the car.
>
> That is why the partner model exists: proven products from partners, showcase in return. And language at this scale is not a casual job: brand voice across markets, speed on race weekends, quality a global brand can sign off. AI alone does not carry that responsibility. AI plus your human experts does, and that combination is exactly your platform.
>
> If anything, the AI wave makes the story stronger: everyone can translate words now. Trusted, on-brand language at speed is the hard part, and that is what you would be demonstrating."

### Q6. "How is ROI measured in Formula 1?"

> "Four ways, usually.
>
> **Media value.** Independent firms like Nielsen track every second your branding appears, across TV and digital, and give it a money value against your fee.
>
> **Brand tracking.** Awareness and consideration among fans and, for you, among the marketing community specifically.
>
> **Business pipeline.** For B2B, you count introductions, meetings and deals closed through the platform. That is how Airwallex could point to 50% revenue growth during their partnership.
>
> **And for you, product metrics.** Engagement lift on the localized content itself: the activation measures its own value, which very few sponsors can say.
>
> You agree the framework upfront and review it with the team every quarter."

### Q7. "Who owns Formula 1? How does the money work?"

> "Three layers, very simple.
>
> Liberty Media, an American media company, owns Formula 1 itself. They bought it in 2017. They control the commercial rights: races, broadcast deals, the calendar.
>
> The FIA is the independent regulator.
>
> The eleven teams are independent businesses: Ferrari, Mercedes and Audi belong to car makers, Red Bull to the drinks company, others to investors. Each team earns a share of the sport's revenues plus its own sponsorships.
>
> Since 2021 the sport has a cost cap, so teams became profitable, stable businesses. Team sponsorship has doubled since 2020, to 2.2 billion dollars a year. You would be entering a healthy, growing market."

### Q8. "What does a partnership cost, and why is Formula 1 so expensive?"

Title partnerships are a different world, well above fifty million a year. And technology or supplier structures, where your platform does real work for the team, can be shaped differently.
>

# Eton Solutions x SPORTFIVE: F1 Call, Questions and Answers

**Call:** intro call with [Name, Job title] at Eton Solutions. From SPORTFIVE: Rahul, Bjorn and George.

---

## The intro

> "Hi [Name], thanks for making the time. I'm Rahul. I look after brand partnerships in SPORTFIVE's Formula 1 business, and Bjorn and George are with me.
>
> A bit about how we work, because it's different from what most people expect. We don't sell for one team. We look at all eleven, work out what you're trying to achieve, and then see whether any of it fits. The teams pay us, not you. And if it doesn't fit, we'll tell you."

### "So why did you reach out to us?"

> "Two simple reasons. The first is who you look after. Family offices, and the very wealthy families behind them. Those families are already around F1, often as guests of the banks that manage their money. UBS has hosted its clients at races for years, and this year Standard Chartered became the sport's official wealth management partner.
>
> The second is what you do. You bring a family's companies, investments, accounts and documents into one system, so nothing gets missed. An F1 team works the same way. It tracks every part and every number, because one small mistake can cost a race.
>
> And you're growing fast: Dubai, Singapore, India, and now wealthy individuals as well as family offices. So it felt like a good time to talk. But I'd like to hear from you first. Where are things heading?"

## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who watches F1? Is it our audience?"

> "More of it than you'd think. F1 fans index at 131 for high income, where 100 is the general population. That's joint first of any sport. They also index at 160 for business decision makers, the highest of any sport. In plain terms, the people who own and run businesses are much more likely than average to follow F1.
>
> The other number I'd look at is age. 43% of fans are under 35. At your own client conference, next-generation principals came up again and again. Those are the people who'll decide what their family office runs on for the next twenty years, and a big share of F1's audience is their age.
>
> And the sport goes where you are. There are more than twenty race weekends on five continents, including Singapore, Abu Dhabi and three in the US."

### "Our clients are family offices and very wealthy families, not fans. Who's in the paddock?"

> "Mostly business people. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. There are more than 350 partner companies across the grid, and every one of them brings guests.
>
> What I'd point out for you is how many of those companies are in wealth. UBS is at Mercedes, Carlyle is at Red Bull, and Barclays, BNY and Nuveen all signed with Williams this year. Standard Chartered is the sport's wealth management partner, and LVMH is a global partner. When the banks explain why they're there, they all say the same thing: clients. So a lot of the guests at a race are the kind of families you serve, brought there by their bankers. Teams also introduce their partners to each other, so the people your guests meet are often people they'd want to meet anyway."

### "How are similar brands activating, and what are they getting back?"

> "Let me give you a few, starting with the ones closest to your clients, the banks and investment firms. Then the finance software companies, and one luxury group."

**UBS at Mercedes**

> "UBS has been with Mercedes since 2011. When it renewed in 2024, the co-head of its wealth business, Iqbal Khan, gave the reason in one line: to keep providing 'unparalleled experiences for our clients.'
>
> Through the partnership, UBS uses races to spend time with its wealth clients. It announced the renewal on the Singapore race weekend, and Khan, who also runs UBS in Asia, said it would help serve clients in the region.
>
> UBS doesn't publish a return. But a bank doesn't stay fifteen years without seeing it pay, so the length of the deal is the best evidence you'll get."

**Standard Chartered with Formula 1**

> "Standard Chartered has been with the sport itself, not a team, since January. It's F1's official wealth management partner. The head of its wealth business, Judy Hsu, said 'our clients value premium service, innovation and seamless global connectivity', and the plan is to use the bank's network to bring them closer to F1 through exclusive experiences.
>
> Through the deal, it offers its clients exclusive F1 experiences and sponsors F1 Academy, the women's series. It says it operates in 19 of the 21 F1 race markets.
>
> It's too new for numbers. What it shows is that you don't have to pick a team. And it's a bank that looks after wealthy families in Asia and the Middle East, the same places you're growing."

**Barclays, BNY and Nuveen at Williams**

> "All three signed with Williams this winter: Nuveen and BNY in January, Barclays in February. For Barclays it's the first time in F1. Its president, Stephen Dainton, said the partnership 'will deepen our client engagement and elevate our brand', and named private banking and wealth as part of the reason.
>
> Through the deals, all three are on the car. Barclays runs programmes for its investment banking, private banking and wealth clients. BNY's plan is to bring financial audiences together in 'key financial hubs around the world.'
>
> It's too early for results, and none are published. But three big finance names choosing the same team in one winter tells you how much they value time with clients at a race."

**Carlyle at Red Bull**

> "Carlyle, the private equity firm, has been with Red Bull since September last year. It's the first big private markets firm to partner with an F1 team. Its CEO, Harvey Schwartz, said 'our industry is undergoing an extraordinary transformation, fuelled by greater access to private markets.' In plain words: Carlyle wants to reach wealthy families and their advisers, not just pension funds.
>
> Through the partnership, its name is on the car and in the garage, and it uses the team's hospitality to meet people it wants as clients. Bloomberg reported that its head of private wealth was at Red Bull's 2026 car launch in Detroit, meeting financial advisers.
>
> No return is published. But Schwartz was at Monaco in June talking it up, and Bloomberg's headline was that Carlyle is banking on the deal to catch up in wealth. The people Carlyle wants to reach are your clients."

**Airwallex at McLaren**

> "Airwallex has been with McLaren since 2024. Its CFO, Pranav Sood, put the reason simply: 'Formula 1 is built on the pursuit of marginal gains,' and that's how it sells its own product.
>
> Through the partnership, it supports McLaren's global finance operations and runs the payments on the team's merchandise platform for partners.
>
> It's published more than most. Revenue grew 50% year on year across Europe, the Middle East, Africa and the Americas during the partnership, and 57% in the UK from new customers. And on 21 October, McLaren made it the team's first Official AI Finance Partner, from 2027."

**SAP at Mercedes, and Oracle at Red Bull**

> "These two are the closest to what AtlasFive does, because the job is the team's own books. SAP has been with Mercedes since 2024, and the announcement put the reason in one word: efficiency, 'on and off the racetrack.' Oracle has been Red Bull's title partner since 2022.
>
> Through the partnerships, Mercedes runs its finances on SAP's business software, and SAP's AI forecasts costs against the spending cap. Red Bull is moving its finance, HR and marketing onto Oracle's business software, and Oracle is building an AI agent to help the race engineers make strategy calls.
>
> Neither publishes a business return. The signal is the job itself: a team only lets a partner near its numbers if it trusts them. Oracle signed again in February, and Mercedes moved onto SAP's newest system for this year's rule change."

**LVMH with Formula 1**

> "LVMH has been with the sport since 2025, on a ten-year deal. Bernard Arnault's reason: 'In motorsport as in fashion, watchmaking or wines and spirits, every detail counts on the path to success.'
>
> Through it, TAG Heuer is the official timekeeper, Louis Vuitton makes the trophy trunks, and Moët is the official champagne. LVMH said from the start it would use the deal for hospitality, limited editions and experiences for its clients.
>
> No return is published, and it's early. But ten years is a long bet, and these brands sell to the same families you do."

**What that adds up to**

> "So the honest picture is this. The banks and luxury brands rarely publish numbers. What they show is that they stay a long time, like UBS, and that more keep arriving, like Barclays, BNY, Nuveen and Carlyle in the last year. Airwallex is the one that's published real business results.
>
> For you, I'd take three things from that. First, hosting: a small number of principals, CIOs and COOs at a few races, quietly, the way UBS and Barclays do it. Second, a true story about the team's own books, the way SAP and Airwallex do, rather than just a logo. Third, look at who's standing next to your guests. The banks and investment firms on the grid already work with the families you serve, so a race weekend puts you alongside them."

### "Content is really important for us. How would a team help us make it?"

> "It's one of the most useful parts of a deal, and for a company that sells on trust, it may matter more than the logo.
>
> Most deals come with a set number of filming days each year with the drivers and the team's people, and the team's content people help you make it. The team's own channels matter too. F1 is the number one sport on social media, ahead of the NBA.
>
> One thing to know: the race footage is controlled by Formula 1 itself, not the teams. So partner content is filmed around the race: at the factory, in the garage, or on filming days.
>
> The best partner content usually isn't about the product. CrowdStrike made a series with Mercedes where the team's specialists explain the safety kit on the car, from the halo to the helmet, and Mercedes says it got 6.2 million views.
>
> For you, I'd start with what your CEO said at your conference: software does the routine work, and people make the judgment calls. A team's pit wall runs the same way. Film a team strategist explaining which calls the computer makes during a race and which ones a person always makes. That's exactly the conversation your clients are having about AI. A second idea for the next generation: a young driver and a senior team figure talking about learning from the people who built the team before them.
>
> If content matters to you, we'd spell it out in the deal: how many days, which drivers and team people, what the team posts on its own channels, and how long and where you can use it."

### "Why are these brands in F1?"

> "Two reasons, mostly.
>
> The banks and investment firms are there for their clients. UBS, Barclays and Standard Chartered all say the same thing. A race weekend is three days with the people who matter most to your business, somewhere they really want to be.
>
> The software companies are there to prove something. A team runs on one set of numbers, under a spending cap, and every part has to be tracked. If your software works there, people believe it will work for them. That's why SAP and Oracle are there.
>
> For Eton, I'd guess it's both. Your clients are the people the banks are there to host. And your whole pitch is one system, so nothing gets missed. An F1 team would say the same about every part on the car."

### "Who are our competitors in F1, and which teams are available?"

> "I don't want to promise you anything on that yet, because right now it would be a guess. As far as we can tell, no family office software company has a deal with a team.
>
> But a few teams have partners close to what you do. SAP at Mercedes and Oracle at Red Bull run their teams' finance software, and Airwallex becomes McLaren's AI Finance Partner from 2027. Some teams also have finance partners with broad titles. Carlyle is Red Bull's exclusive investment management partner, UBS is at Mercedes, and Williams has Barclays, BNY and Nuveen. And Standard Chartered holds wealth management for the sport as a whole. These deals usually come with exclusivity.
>
> So the first thing we do is check where there's a conflict, and rule those teams out.
>
> The second thing is understanding what you want out of it. If it's mainly about meeting family offices in the Gulf and Asia, that can point you to different teams than if it's American families, or the wealthy individuals you're reaching with EtonAlpha. Then we come back with the teams where it fits.
>
> One thing worth knowing: software that runs a family's office isn't the same as banking or managing money, and these contracts are often written quite narrowly. So a team with a bank isn't automatically off the table.
>
> To give you an idea of what a partner like you does once they're in, look at SAP at Mercedes. Mercedes runs its finances on SAP's business software, the same kind of system AtlasFive is for a family. The job is very specific. The team can only spend so much each season, so SAP's AI forecasts costs and predicts where the budget will end up. For this year's new rules, the team moved onto SAP's newest version. So SAP gets to say that a team working under a hard spending limit trusts it with its books. That's a much stronger story than a sticker on the car."

### "It's very crowded. Won't we get lost?"

> "It's a real risk. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and a lot of them are just logos. And there are already several banks. You'd never win on logo size next to UBS or Barclays.
>
> But you don't need to. Your chairman's line is 'money talks and wealth whispers.' For you, F1 is less about being seen by millions and more about the right twenty people at the right race. Passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend.
>
> And if you do want to be noticed, do what Airwallex did. It sits next to Mastercard at McLaren, so it was never going to win on size. It took on a real job in the team's finance department, and three seasons later McLaren has made it its AI Finance Partner."

### "Why would a team want a partner like us?"

> "Teams aren't short of technology partners. Tech companies now spend more on F1 teams than any other kind of business, and a Reuters report in May counted eight new AI deals in six months.
>
> What's harder to find is a partner who brings the right people. More than 1,100 families use your platform, and you've said they include founders of big technology, hotel and energy businesses. Those are people a team would be glad to have at a race, as guests and as future partners.
>
> You'd also bring a story the team can relate to. A team is a business that has to account for every cent under a spending cap. A company whose whole job is getting a family's books right is easy for them to understand.
>
> Whether there's room for that at a given team is what we'd check first."

### "What would a first deal look like for a company our size?"

> "I'll be straight with you: a full team partnership is a big step for a company your size. Most companies like yours start with hosting. A few race weekends with your best clients and prospects, to see whether it works. Passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend. For you, Singapore, where your international headquarters is, Abu Dhabi, close to your Dubai office, and one of the three US races would be the obvious ones.
>
> If that works, the next step is one team, one clear role, some branding, client weekends and some content, over two or three years. Some companies sign a driver or a content deal instead of a team, which also costs less. And Standard Chartered shows you can go with the sport itself, though that sits at a much higher level."

### "What does it cost?"

> "It depends a lot on the team and what's included. A supplier or entry-level partner deal starts in the low single-digit millions a year. Official partner deals sit above that. Hosting clients at a few races, a driver deal or a content deal costs a lot less. I'd rather understand what you want first, then show you options at different levels."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

> "You can go to a team directly. They'll tell you about their own team, which is fair enough. We worked only with Mercedes and Ferrari for over twelve years, and then stopped, so we could look at all eleven teams for the brand. The teams pay our fee, so having us compare them costs you nothing. And with SAP, Oracle, Carlyle and several banks already on the grid, someone has to check each team for conflicts before you spend time on any of them."

### "Which team would fit us?"

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. It depends on what you want out of it, whether your clients are mainly in the US, the Gulf or Asia, and where there's no conflict. That's what we'd come back to you with."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "We'd agree that before anything's signed. For you I'd look at three things. Business: which principals, CIOs and COOs you hosted, and how many became AtlasFive clients or introduced you to someone who did. EtonAlpha: how many of the wealthy individuals you met signed up. And content: how far the films travelled, which we can measure.
>
> It can show up in the numbers. Airwallex says its revenue grew 50% year on year across Europe, the Middle East, Africa and the Americas during its McLaren partnership, and 57% in the UK from new customers."

### "Our clients value privacy. Won't F1 look flashy to a family office?"

> "Some of the most careful names in wealth are on the grid. UBS has been with Mercedes since 2011, and Barclays brought its private banking clients into F1 this year. If they're comfortable, an invitation from you won't look strange.
>
> And it doesn't have to be loud. Most of what wealth firms do there is quiet: a small group, a walk through the garage, a dinner. Nobody outside sees it. That fits 'wealth whispers'. What your clients remember is that you got them there."

### "We raised our Series C last year, and our investors watch every dollar. Is this the right time?"

> "It might not be. Navis put money in to grow the AI side and new markets, so anything here has to be tied to business, not visibility. If F1 helps you win family offices in the Gulf and Asia, and puts EtonAlpha in front of wealthy individuals, that's easier to justify than a logo.
>
> So we'd start from your plan for the next two or three years and work back. If the answer is a couple of race weekends with clients first, that's fine. If it's 'not yet', we'll tell you."

## The intro

> "Hi [Name], thanks for making the time. I'm Rahul. I look after brand partnerships in SPORTFIVE's Formula 1 business, and Bjorn and George are with me.
>
> A quick word on what we do, because we cover more than people expect. We look after the whole journey for a brand. It starts with finding the right partner, one that makes sense for your business. Then we help you bring it to life and get the full value out of it, from what happens at the track to what goes out online.
>
> We work across a lot of sports, and we represent some of the biggest names in them. So we don't need to push one thing. We can act more like an adviser, and help you find what fits, whether that's Formula 1, tennis or something else.
>
> I'd suggest we start with Formula 1 today. But if there's interest in looking wider, we're happy to do that too. Either way, we'd like to keep this open-ended: answer your questions, build a brief with you, and then map out the right way in. In F1, the teams pay our fee, not you. And if something doesn't fit, we'll tell you."

### "So why did you reach out to us?"

> "Two simple reasons. The first is who you'll serve. Drug makers, chemical companies, carmakers and energy companies. Those companies are already in F1. AbbVie, the drug maker, joined a team last month. And several carmakers run their own teams.
>
> The second is what you do. An F1 team can't test much on the track. So it builds the car on a computer first, and tests it there. You're building computers to simulate what today's computers can't: the molecules in a new drug, a better battery or a new material. Even the fuel in an F1 car is now designed this way. Shell ran millions of simulations to make this season's race fuel.
>
> And you've had a big year, with Chicago and Brisbane going up. So it felt like a good time to talk. But I'd like to hear from you first. Where are things heading?"

## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who watches F1? Is it our audience?"

> "More of it than you'd think. F1 fans index at 160 for business decision makers, where 100 is the general population. That's the highest of any sport. In plain terms, the head of research at a drug or chemical company is much more likely than average to follow F1.
>
> The other number I'd look at is age. 43% of fans are under 35. You're hiring physicists and engineers, and that's who you want to reach.
>
> And the sport goes where you are. There are more than twenty race weekends on five continents. Melbourne covers Australia. There are three races in the US: Miami, Austin and Las Vegas. And Silverstone is in the UK, where you have a facility."

### "Our buyers are heads of research at drug, chemical and car companies, not fans. Who's in the paddock?"

> "Mostly business people. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. There are more than 350 partner companies across the grid, and every one of them brings guests.
>
> What I'd point out for you is how many of them work in chemistry, energy and cars. INEOS, the chemical company, owns a third of the Mercedes team. Petronas is Mercedes' title partner. Shell fuels Ferrari. Aramco is Aston Martin's title partner, and Eni is at Alpine. AbbVie joined McLaren last month. And for finance, UBS is at Mercedes.
>
> The carmakers are there too. Mercedes, Ferrari and Audi run their own teams. General Motors co-owns Cadillac. Honda builds Aston Martin's engine, and Ford works with Red Bull on its engines. Teams also introduce their partners to each other. So the people your guests meet are often people you'd want to meet anyway.
>
> And one door is already open. Mercedes-Benz's research team wrote a paper with you in 2022 on simulating battery molecules."

### "How are similar brands activating, and what are they getting back?"

> "As far as we can tell, only one team has a quantum computing project, and it's at an early stage. So here are the closest ones: that project, the big computing companies, the teams' simulation partners, and two of the companies you want as customers."

**SEALSQ at Alpine**

> "Start with the closest. SEALSQ has been with Alpine since the Las Vegas race a year ago. It's a chip company based in Geneva, and it makes security chips built to stand up to future quantum computers. Its CEO, Carlos Moreira, said 'racing is not only about speed on the track, but also about innovation in the lab and the cloud.'
>
> Through the deal, SEALSQ protects the team's data, and it launched its new chip at last year's Las Vegas race. In September it widened the deal. With ColibriTD, a company it backs, it's testing software that mixes quantum and normal computing, next to the team's own computers. The targets are airflow, heat, and how parts carry load. Alpine's executive technical director, David Sanchez, called it 'an exciting opportunity to explore the potential of quantum.'
>
> No results are published. It's a test, not a result. The signal is that the deal grew from security into simulation in under a year. And notice how carefully the team talks about it: explore, test, alongside its own computers."

**IBM at Ferrari**

> "IBM has a big quantum business, and it's been with Ferrari since the start of last year. Its marketing chief, Jonathan Adashek, said 'in Formula 1, seconds matter whether it's on or off the track.'
>
> But look at what the deal covers. It isn't quantum. IBM rebuilt the Scuderia Ferrari fan app with its watsonx AI. Fans get race recaps soon after the finish, quizzes, and an AI guide to the team. IBM adds new features each season.
>
> This is one of the few with published numbers. IBM says that since the app relaunched in May last year, downloads are up 35%, monthly users are up 36%, and users on race day are up 56%. What it tells you is that IBM chose to show something fans can use today, not a promise about tomorrow."

**Microsoft at Mercedes**

> "Microsoft signed a multi-year deal with Mercedes in January. It also runs its own quantum programme. Its commercial chief, Judson Althoff, said F1 is where 'milliseconds matter and data determines outcomes.'
>
> Through the deal, Mercedes runs more of its simulation, performance analysis and race strategy on Microsoft's cloud. Each car carries more than 400 sensors, sending over a million data points a second. The cloud lets the team add computing power when it needs it.
>
> It's too new for results. What it shows is that simulation is the job a big computing partner gets inside a team. And like IBM, Microsoft sells what it has today in F1, cloud and AI, not quantum."

**Oracle at Red Bull**

> "Oracle has been Red Bull's title partner since February 2022, and it extended and expanded the deal this February. The team boss, Laurent Mekies, said the partnership 'has been hugely successful.'
>
> Through the deal, Red Bull runs its race simulations on Oracle's cloud. This year's rules made those models harder: energy use, moving wings and tyres all have to be in them. Red Bull's new engine, built with Ford, was designed and tested on Oracle's cloud too. And the two built an AI strategy agent that works alongside the race engineers.
>
> Oracle doesn't publish a business return. The signal is the renewal, and what the team points to since Oracle arrived: three drivers' titles and two constructors' titles."

**Shell at Ferrari**

> "Shell has been with Ferrari since 1950. Its principal scientist for motorsport, Valeria Loreti, says F1 'has always been about much more than brand visibility.' And she adds: 'That's why we describe it as an Innovation Partnership rather than a sponsorship.'
>
> Through the deal, Shell makes Ferrari's fuel and oil. This year the rules call for 100% sustainable fuel. Shell says it ran more than two million fuel simulations to build it. Its scientists work from its technology centre here in Hamburg, and it brings a lab to every race to test samples.
>
> No business return is published. The signal is more than 75 years together, and the fact that Shell leads with its scientists, not its logo. For you, it's the clearest sign that chemistry on a computer is already behind what goes in the car."

**AbbVie at McLaren**

> "AbbVie, one of the world's biggest drug makers, joined McLaren last month. It's AbbVie's first F1 deal. Its head of corporate affairs, Tracie Haas, said the team and the company are 'both driven by a mindset of continuous improvement.'
>
> Through the deal, AbbVie runs a campaign called Race to Innovate. For every 10 points McLaren scores, it gives $10,000 to charities that support people with immune diseases. It started at the Austin race, and it will tell stories about the people behind progress in medicine and in racing.
>
> It's far too new for results. The signal is who it is. A big drug company decided F1 is a place to talk about science. Those are the people you'll be selling to."

**What that adds up to**

> "So the honest picture is this. As far as we can tell, only one team has a quantum computing project, Alpine with SEALSQ, and it's a test. The big computing companies with quantum programmes, IBM, Microsoft and Google, all sell what works today in F1: apps, cloud and AI. The simulation partners, Oracle and Microsoft, show that simulation is a real job inside a team. Shell shows that chemistry on a computer is already behind the fuel. And AbbVie shows your future customers are starting to arrive. Few of them publish numbers. IBM is the exception.
>
> For you, I'd take three things from that. First, don't promise anything about the car. Lead with the science, and be exact about what's ready and what isn't, the way Alpine talks about its test. Second, host the people who will buy from you: heads of research at drug, chemical, car and energy companies, many of them already on the grid. Third, use it to hire. The people who build simulations for F1 teams are the kind of people you need."

### "Content is really important for us. How would a team help us make it?"

> "It's one of the most useful parts of a deal. For a company whose product isn't on sale yet, it may matter more than the logo.
>
> Most deals come with a set number of filming days each year with the drivers and the team's people, and the team's content people help you make it. The team's own channels matter too. F1 is the number one sport on social media, ahead of the NBA.
>
> One thing to know: the race footage is controlled by Formula 1 itself, not the teams. So partner content is filmed around the race: at the factory, in the garage, or on filming days.
>
> The best partner content usually isn't about the product. CrowdStrike made a series with Mercedes where the team's specialists explain the safety kit on the car, from the halo to the helmet, and Mercedes says it got 6.2 million views. And Shell has paid for articles on The Race where its principal scientist explains how this year's fuel was made.
>
> For you, I'd start with one question: what can't you simulate yet? Put one of a team's simulation engineers next to one of your scientists. The team engineer shows what they can model well, and where it runs out. How a tyre heats up and wears. Or what goes on inside the battery and the new fuel under this year's rules. Your scientist explains why some molecules are too hard for today's computers, using the battery work you've already published. No claim that the team uses quantum, and no stunt. Just two people whose job is simulation, being straight about the limits.
>
> A second idea, for hiring: the build. A team builds a new car every year. You're building a new kind of computer, with chips made in New York and sites going up in Chicago and Brisbane. Film people from each side walking through how they build something nobody has built before.
>
> If content matters to you, we'd spell it out in the deal: how many days, which drivers and team people, what the team posts on its own channels, and how long and where you can use it."

### "Why are these brands in F1?"

> "Two reasons, mostly.
>
> The first is proof. A team designs its car on computers, under a spending cap, and with limits on how much computing it can use for aerodynamics. Then the car has to work first time at every race. If your technology helps there, people believe it will help them. That's why Oracle and Microsoft are there.
>
> The second is people: the customers they want to host, and the engineers they want to hire. Shell calls its deal an innovation partnership, and it's about both.
>
> For PsiQuantum, I'd guess it's people first, for now. Your machine isn't ready to prove itself on a car, and nobody should pretend it is. But the people who will buy time on it, and the people who will help build it, are already in F1."

### "Who are our competitors in F1, and which teams are available?"

> "I don't want to promise you anything on that yet, because right now it would be a guess. As far as we can tell, no quantum computer maker has a deal with an F1 team.
>
> But several teams have partners close to what you do. IBM is at Ferrari, Google at McLaren and Microsoft at Mercedes. All three run quantum programmes, even though their F1 deals don't cover it. Microsoft is also the other company left with you in the final stage of DARPA's test of quantum computers. SEALSQ runs the quantum test at Alpine. And Aramco, Aston Martin's title partner, installed a quantum computer from Pasqal in Saudi Arabia late last year. Deals like these usually come with exclusivity, and contracts often cover more than the title says.
>
> There are friendly names too. AMD, your partner, is at Mercedes. INEOS co-owns that team, and Mercedes-Benz did battery research with you. That can open a door, or it can need care.
>
> So the first thing we do is check where there's a conflict, and rule those teams out.
>
> The second thing is understanding what you want out of it. If it's mainly meeting drug and chemical companies, that can point you to different teams than if it's hiring, or the US. Then we come back with the teams where it fits.
>
> One thing worth knowing: 'Official Quantum Computing Partner' is a narrow title. That helps when we check for conflicts. But we'd check each team before saying any more.
>
> To give you an idea of what a partner like you does once they're in, look at Oracle at Red Bull. The team runs its race simulations on Oracle's cloud, and its new engine was designed and tested there. So Oracle gets to say a championship-winning team builds and races on its technology. That's a much stronger story than a sticker on the car."

### "It's very crowded. Won't we get lost?"

> "It's a real risk. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and Reuters counted eight new AI deals in six months. If you're one more tech logo, you'll get lost.
>
> The ones that get noticed do one of two things. Some take on a real job, like Oracle with Red Bull's simulations, or Shell with Ferrari's fuel. Others make something people want to watch.
>
> You'd have ground nobody else is standing on. IBM, Google and Microsoft all build quantum computers, and all chose to talk about something else in F1. And you've got a story F1 tells well. A group of scientists take on a very hard problem, then build the answer at scale: in a chip factory in New York, and in new buildings in Chicago and Brisbane."

### "Why would a team want a partner like us?"

> "Teams aren't short of technology partners. Tech companies now spend more on F1 teams than any other kind of business, and a Reuters report in May counted eight new AI deals in six months.
>
> What's rarer is a partner that lives in simulation the way a team does. Teams have a spending cap and limits on how much computing they can use for aerodynamics, and they test very little on track. So they spend their days asking what they can model, and how far to trust it. Your scientists ask the same question about molecules. That's a conversation a team's engineers would enjoy.
>
> You'd also bring people a team would like to meet. BlackRock, Temasek and NVIDIA's venture arm invested in you, AMD plans to, and you already work with drug, chemical and car companies.
>
> Whether a team wants that, and whether a current partner already covers it, is what we'd check first."

### "What would a first deal look like for a company our size?"

> "I'd keep the first step modest. You raised a billion dollars last year, but you're still building the machines, so every dollar has to earn its place. A typical first deal for a company that sells to other businesses is one team, one clear role, some branding, a few race weekends with customers, and some content, over two or three years.
>
> Others start smaller, hosting customers at a few races to see whether it works. Passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend. For you, Melbourne covers Australia. Austin, Miami and Las Vegas cover the US. And Silverstone covers the UK and Europe.
>
> A hiring programme and a content series about the science can sit inside the deal from day one."

### "What does it cost?"

> "It depends a lot on the team and what's included. A supplier or entry-level partner deal starts in the low single-digit millions a year. Official partner deals sit above that. Hosting customers at a few races, a driver deal or a content deal costs a lot less. I'd rather understand what you want first, then show you options at different levels."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

> "You can go to a team directly. They'll tell you about their own team, which is fair enough. We worked only with Mercedes and Ferrari for over twelve years, and then stopped, so we could look at all eleven teams for the brand. The teams pay our fee, so having us compare them costs you nothing. We also work across a lot of sports, so we can tell you plainly when F1 isn't the best fit. And with IBM, Google, Microsoft and SEALSQ on the grid, someone has to check each team quietly before your name goes anywhere."

### "Which team would fit us?"

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. It depends on what you want out of it, which markets matter most, whether that's the US, Australia or the UK, and where there's no conflict. Mercedes has AMD and the battery research, which is a door. But it also has Microsoft. That's what we'd come back to you with."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "We'd agree that before anything's signed. For you I'd look at three things. Business: which heads of research at drug, chemical, car and energy companies you spent time with, and the projects that came from it. Hiring: how many good engineers and physicists applied. And content: how far the films travelled, which we can measure.
>
> It can show up in the numbers. Airwallex says its revenue grew 50% year on year in its Europe and Americas regions during its McLaren partnership, and 57% in the UK from new customers."

### "We don't have a commercial product yet. Isn't this too early?"

> "It might be too early for some things, and I'd rather say so. A big logo deal before you sell anything would be hard to justify. And nobody should put a quantum computer on a car.
>
> But three things don't need a product. Meeting the people who'll buy from you. Hiring. And telling the story of building the machine. Your Omega chips coming out of a factory in New York, and the sites in Chicago and Brisbane, are a story right now.
>
> And research doesn't wait for a product. Mercedes-Benz wrote the battery paper with you in 2022, years before any machine. Boehringer Ingelheim and Mitsubishi Chemical work with you now. F1 is full of the same kind of companies. So a small first step, a few race weekends with the right people, can make sense before the machine is ready. When it is ready, you'd already know them."

### "IBM is already at Ferrari, and Google is at McLaren. Are we too late?"

> "No. Look at what those deals cover. IBM's is a fan app and data. Google's is Gemini, Pixel phones, Android and Chrome. Neither is about quantum computing. Microsoft at Mercedes is cloud and AI. As far as we can tell, the only quantum project with a team is SEALSQ's test at Alpine.
>
> What it does tell you is that big companies with quantum programmes are already close to some teams. So I'd expect Ferrari, McLaren and Mercedes to need care, and maybe to be out. We'd confirm that first.
>
> And you'd be telling a different story. They talk about apps and AI you can use today. You'd be there for the science of simulation. Being second isn't a problem in F1. And nobody has told the quantum story in F1 yet."

### "Much of our funding is from governments and defense. Is that a problem?"

> "Fair question, and I'd rather be straight with you. It might be at some teams, and not at others. As far as we can tell, neither F1 nor any team has published rules on this. So it comes down to each team, its owners and its partners. Alpine signed Indra in January, and Indra calls itself a technology and defense group. Boeing is on Alpine's list too. So it isn't a closed door everywhere.
>
> Your government backing is public: the US Commerce Department, DARPA, a loan offer from the Department of War, and the Australian and Queensland governments. A lot of new computing starts out with government money.
>
> So we'd check team by team, quietly, before your name goes to anyone. And we'd lead with the commercial and scientific story: drug chemistry with Boehringer Ingelheim, materials with Mitsubishi Chemical, and batteries with Mercedes-Benz. That's the story F1 fans and guests would see.
>
> And where we don't know the answer for a team, we'll tell you that, rather than guess."

### "Quantum gets called hype. Won't F1 make it look like a stunt?"

> "It could, if it's done badly. If the message is 'quantum makes the car faster', it would look like a stunt, and it wouldn't be true. So we wouldn't do that.
>
> The partners who come across well in F1 are exact about what they do. Shell talks about its scientists and its two million fuel simulations. Alpine calls its quantum work a test that runs alongside its own computers. IBM showed a fan app people use every race, with numbers behind it.
>
> For you, the honest version is the strong one. Here's what we're building, here's what it will be able to simulate, and here's what isn't ready yet. A team engineer saying 'this is what I can't model today' makes your case better than any claim. People in F1 respect someone who knows the limits of their tools."

### "We need to hire physicists and engineers. Can F1 help?"

> "It can, if it's built in from the start. 43% of F1 fans are under 35, and F1 teams hire a lot of the same people you do: physicists, mathematicians, and engineers who build simulations.
>
> The deals that do this well make hiring part of the job. Komatsu runs an academy at Williams that takes ten engineering students a year, found through Formula Student, the university race car competition. A team's name gets you noticed on campus.
>
> For you, I'd tie it to the sites. You're already putting money into schools and training around the Chicago site. Melbourne covers Australia, and Silverstone covers the UK.
>
> One thing to know: teams compete for the same graduates. So the best version is shared, not a tug of war: a programme where students see both kinds of simulation, the car and the molecule."

### "What's the biggest risk?"

> "Signing a logo with no job. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and the ones that get lost put a name on the car and stop there. A deal needs a real job, a plan for the people you'll host, and someone on your side who owns it.
>
> The second risk is a conflict you didn't see. IBM, Google and Microsoft all have quantum programmes, SEALSQ is at Alpine, and Aramco has a quantum computer from Pasqal. Contracts often cover more than the title says. That's why we check every team before your name goes anywhere.
>
> The third is picking the wrong option for the job. If what you need most is hiring in Chicago, there's no F1 race there, and another sport might do it better. If it's meeting heads of research, a few race weekends may be all you need for now. We work across a lot of sports, so we'd rather tell you that up front than sell you the wrong thing."

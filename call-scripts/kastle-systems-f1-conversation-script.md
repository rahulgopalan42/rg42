# Kastle Systems x SPORTFIVE: F1 Conversation Script

**The call:** introductory call with [Name, Job title] at Kastle Systems. Joining from SPORTFIVE: Rahul, Bjorn and George. First conversation as far as we know (check the register). Kastle is a practical, numbers-first services business whose chairman owns sports teams, so keep it plain, specific and short.

**How to use this:** Spoken lines are in quotes; notes to you are in [brackets]. Say it your own way, in your own words. Glance at it, don't read it.

Buyers now include asset managers, CFOs and CEOs, not only property managers.

## 2. The strongest F1 connection

Kastle's whole business is deciding who gets through which door, and a Formula 1 paddock is the best-known place in sport where every pass decides which door opens. Their sale is also moving up the building: Haniel says buyers now include asset managers, CFOs and CEOs, and the question they ask is "who do I trust to run this for me?" Those are the kind of people standing in the paddock at Miami, Austin and Las Vegas, in Kastle's home market. So the sport gives Kastle a story that explains what it does in a minute and a room with its buyers in it, without paying for global reach it has no use for.

## 3. The conversation

### A. Warm introduction (30 to 45 seconds)

> "Hi [Name], thanks for making the time. Really good to meet you.
>
> So, quickly, who we are. I'm Rahul, I look after brand partnerships in SPORTFIVE's Formula 1 business. And I've got Bjorn and George with me."

*[Bjorn and George: one line each, no pitching. Then:]*

> "The way we work is probably a bit different from what you'd expect. We don't sell for one team. We work right across motorsport, the teams, the drivers, the races, and our job is to help a brand work out whether any of it makes sense for them. Sometimes the answer's no, and that's fine.
>
> So this isn't a pitch. I'd just like to have a proper conversation and understand Kastle a bit better."

*[If they ask why not go straight to a team: "You could. We used to work only with Mercedes and Ferrari, for more than twelve years, and we gave that up on purpose so we could sit on the brand's side across all eleven teams. And the rights holders pay us, so it doesn't cost you anything."]*

### B. Why I reached out (45 to 60 seconds)

> "So, why Kastle. It started with something Haniel said in July, when you announced the new leadership team. He said the question your customers ask now is 'who do I trust to run this for me?' And that the person asking isn't just the property manager any more. It's the asset manager, the CFO, sometimes the CEO.
>
> What's interesting is that's a different sale. You're not selling badge readers to the person who runs a building. You're asking the person who owns it to trust you with the whole portfolio.
>
> And the world I work in runs on the same idea you do. Nobody gets into a Formula 1 paddock without a pass, and every pass decides which door opens. The paddock, the garage, the hospitality. Who gets in, where, and when. It's your business, running flat out for three days.
>
> And, you know, at the American races, the people walking around with those passes look a lot like the people Haniel was talking about. That's why I got in touch."

### C. Why Formula 1 might be relevant (1 to 2 minutes)

*[Three reasons, said loosely. If one lands, stay on it and drop the rest.]*

> "Let me give you the three reasons I think it could be relevant, and you tell me which ones hold up.
>
> The first one's the room. If the person signing is now a CFO or an asset manager, you need proper time with them, and a booth at a trade show doesn't give you that. F1 has the highest concentration of business decision makers of any sport. It indexes at 160, where the general population's 100. And with hospitality you're not hoping they walk past. You choose who you invite. There are three races in the US now, Miami, Austin and Vegas. And one detail people miss: passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend.
>
> Second is trust. If you look at the business names that have come into F1 over the last few years, a lot of them sell to big companies, not to fans. They're there because standing next to that level of engineering makes a serious buyer take them seriously. That's pretty close to the 'who do I trust' question."

*[If you want a name for the second point, use the B2B drop-in in section 4.]*

> "And the third one's more your customers' problem than yours. Your own numbers showed it last December. In the record week, the A-plus buildings were back to nearly 79% of their pre-pandemic level, when the average was 56. People come in for the better building and the better experience. And F1's audience is young and spends on premium. 43% of fans are under 35, and they're number one in sport for buying premium. That's the tenant your customers are trying to get back through the door.
>
> One thing I'd say upfront, though. Your buyers are mostly American, and there aren't millions of them. So most of this sport's global reach is wasted on you, and I won't pretend otherwise. If something here makes sense, it'd be sized around the room and the story, not the TV numbers. There are a few ways in, a team, a driver, or hospitality on its own, and which one depends on what you're trying to get done."

### D. Handing over to them

> "Anyway, that's the view from the outside, and it's only desk research. I think the more useful bit is hearing it from you. Where's Kastle right now, and where's it heading? And do tell me where I've got it wrong."

*[Then let them talk and take notes. Pick from section 5 as the conversation goes; don't run the list in order. If they ask "so where do you see us fitting?", keep it to instinct: "I don't know yet, and I'd be guessing. If you pushed me, I'd start with hosting the right owners at the US races and the access story, and only look at anything bigger if it earns it. But tell me more first."]*

## 5. Discovery questions

*[Ask, then stop talking. Follow what they give you. Get to question 8 inside the first fifteen minutes; budget decides which doors are worth looking at.]*

**1. What the new team is for** (priorities)

> "You brought in five senior people this summer. What's the one thing that team's been asked to make happen over the next year or so?"
>
> *Follow-up:* "Is that more about winning new portfolios, or growing the ones you've already got?"

*[If they say enterprise or data centers: "So i2G's a big part of the story now. Is the data center buyer a different person from the office owner, or often the same people?"]*

**2. Who makes the call** (customers and decision makers)

> "Haniel talked about customers wanting one phone call to take care of all of it. When a big owner decides to consolidate, who's making that call now?"
>
> *Follow-up:* "And what tips it your way? Track record, price, or knowing someone at the top?"

*[If they say CFO, asset manager or CEO: "That's who I had in mind. And every F1 team has 25-plus partner companies, all with offices and data centers somewhere. So it's not only who you host, it's who you end up sitting next to."]*

**3. Where** (markets)

> "Is this mainly a US story for you, or does Australia come into it? And which cities matter most right now?"
>
> *Follow-up:* "Are there metros where you're the challenger rather than the name everyone already knows?"

*[If they name Florida, Texas or the West: the US races are Miami, Austin and Las Vegas on the 2026 calendar (verify 2027 dates). Note which ones line up; don't oversell the overlap.]*

**4. The two audiences** (B2B and B2C)

> "You've got two quite different audiences, the people who sign and the people who carry the badge. When you think about the brand, which one matters more right now?"
>
> *Follow-up:* "On the apartment side, do residents know the Kastle name, or is it pretty much invisible to them?"

*[If tenants or residents matter: that opens the lobby idea in section 6. If it's purely the signer, stay on the room.]*

**5. How marketing works today** (marketing and partnerships)

> "The Barometer gave you a voice most security companies don't have. Beyond that, where does the marketing effort go? Industry events, BOMA, your own customer events?"
>
> *Follow-up:* "And with the new leadership in since July, is that plan being rewritten at the moment?"

*[If they mention sport or Mark's teams: acknowledge it simply ("so you know this world from the other side of the table") and skip the basics. Don't raise it yourself.]*

**6. What good looks like** (how they measure success)

> "When you put money into an event or a customer program, how do you decide afterwards whether it was worth it?"
>
> *Follow-up:* "Who owns that number? Marketing, sales, or finance?"

*[If they say pipeline or deals: "Good, that's how I'd want this judged too. Meetings, portfolios, revenue, not impressions." If they say brand awareness: ask among whom, and how they track it today.]*

**7. How close they are to F1** (familiarity)

> "How much do you follow Formula 1, if at all? And has Kastle done anything in sport before?"
>
> *Follow-up:* "Has anyone on the leadership team been to a race?"

*[If they're fans: compress and skip the basics. If they barely know it, thirty seconds: Liberty Media, an American company, has owned the sport since 2017; there are eleven teams, two of them American, Haas and Cadillac; and Apple broadcasts it in the US now.]*

**8. How decisions get made** (decision process, budget, timeline)

> "The unglamorous one. If this went anywhere, who else would be part of the decision? And is there a rough range in mind, or is it too early for that?"
>
> *Follow-up:* "And timing. Is this a this-year conversation or a next-year one? Both are fine, they just change what we come back with."

*[If they hesitate on budget: "No problem, you don't need a number today. If we get to options, you'll see them at different levels, including the smallest sensible ones." If they name Haniel or Mark: expect sharp price questions.]*

## 6. Potential commercial opportunities

*[Directions to test against what they tell you, not packages. No numbers unless they ask; if they do, use the pricing line in the prep notes.]*

**1. The owners' room (hospitality-led program)**
A small, chosen group of owners, asset managers and CFOs hosted at the US races, a few at a time, with Haniel and the leadership in the room. No logo needed. It speaks straight to the shift in who signs, and the paddock does some of the explaining for them: guests feel every door check. It could sit with a team, a driver or a hospitality program on its own. The test: who would they invite, and does it beat what the same money does at conferences and their own events?

**2. Who gets in (driver or content program)**
A content series with a team or a driver on how a race weekend decides who goes where: passes, zones, guests, the garage. Kastle is the expert voice explaining it, never the company running it. It explains their business to a CFO in two minutes and gives the new marketing team something no competitor has. Whether a team would open that up is something we'd test.

**3. A reason to come in (regional activation, possibly growing into a team partnership)**
Race-week moments in the lobbies of Kastle buildings around the race cities: a driver visit, a screening, a show car where rights allow. It works on their customers' problem, getting tenants back in, and Kastle's own badge data could show whether it moved occupancy. If the brief earns it, this could grow into a right-sized team partnership later. Event and appearance rights vary by team and driver; promise nothing specific before we check.

## 7. Closing script

> "[Name], this has been really useful, thank you. Let me try and play it back in a couple of lines.
>
> Your sale's moving up to owners and CFOs, and what they're buying is trust. The US races put a lot of those people in one place, and it's a place where access is the whole experience. Whether that's worth anything to Kastle, I don't know yet, and I'd rather find out properly than guess.
>
> So the next step is just understanding what you need. What I'd suggest: we write up a short brief from today, you correct it, and nothing moves until you say it's right. Then we come back in [two to three weeks] with the realistic options, what each one costs, and a plain 'not yet' if that's where it lands. No cost to you either way, the rights holders pay our fee.
>
> Would it make sense to have [whoever owns marketing or the enterprise push] on that next call?"

*[Last word, keep it warm:]*

> "Thanks again, [Name]. I enjoyed that. You'll hear from me by [date]."

*[Optional, only if the call went well: "And if you ever want to walk a paddock with a pass round your neck, we'd love to host you at a race."]*

# Community.com x SPORTFIVE: F1 Discovery Call Script

**Call type:** First call / discovery. Goal: introduce yourself and SPORTFIVE, understand Community's objectives and their ongoing team discussion, fill out the brief, show where conversational messaging fits F1, agree next steps.

**How to use this script:** Every spoken block is written the way you would say it out loud. Do not read it word for word. Glance at the bold anchors, then talk. Short sentences, slow pace, warm tone. Keep it simple and concrete for a non-native English listener. Let them talk more than you.

## Part 4: How Formula 1 partnerships work (8 to 10 minutes)

*[Calibrate to their answer from Part 2. If they know the sport, compress. Invite questions as you go: "Stop me anytime."]*

### 4.1 The sport in thirty seconds

> "Let me give you a simple picture of how the sport is set up, because it explains the options you have.
>
> Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company. They own the commercial side: the calendar, the broadcast deals, the races.
>
> The FIA is the regulator. They write the rules.
>
> And then there are eleven teams. From this coming season it is eleven, including two American teams, Haas and Cadillac. Each team is an independent business with its own owners and its own partnerships.
>
> So a brand can partner at three levels: with the sport itself, with a team, or with a driver personally. For Community, all three doors are interesting, and I will explain why when we get there."

### 4.2 What a team partnership includes

> "What do you get as a partner? Think of it in five parts.
>
> **One, branding.** Your logo on the car, the race suits, the garage. Around 24 race weekends a year. Last season the total audience was 1.8 billion, and the average race reached 76 million people.
>
> **Two, the official title.** You become, for example, the Official Fan Engagement Partner of a team. You use the team's name, logo and imagery in your own marketing and sales conversations.
>
> **Three, hospitality.** Paddock access, the Paddock Club, garage tours, time with the drivers. 66% of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. For a company selling to brands, every race is a room full of buyers.
>
> And one detail people miss. A Grand Prix runs over three days, and your passes work every day. Seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend, and hundreds across a season.
>
> **Four, content and digital.** Access to the team's channels and drivers for content. Formula 1 is the number one sport in the world for social engagement, 2.2 billion engagements, ahead of the NBA. For you this part is not just a right, it is raw material.
>
> **Five, people.** Driver appearances, filming days, and for Community something more interesting: the drivers as the product demo itself.
>
> And around all of this sits the business platform: every team carries 25 or more partners, more than 350 companies across the grid, meeting each other all season. Consumer brands, banks, airlines, retailers. Every one of them needs customer engagement. That room is your pipeline."

### 4.3 The audience

*[Community sits on both sides: B2B buyers and B2C fans. Give both, fans first because that is their product story.]*

> "Now, who is on the other end of the phone. Two audiences matter for you.
>
> **The fans, because they are your product story.** Formula 1 has 827 million fans, up 63% since 2018. 43% are under 35, 42% are women, and this is the most engaged fanbase in sport: number one in social engagement, ahead of the NBA, and in the sport's own survey, 61% of fans engage with F1 content every single day. The same survey says something you will like even more: teams and drivers are the new front doors of the sport, and storytelling beats logo placement. Fans want to get closer to the people, not the corporation. That is precisely the itch your product scratches.
>
> **The buyers, because they sign your contracts.** F1 has the highest concentration of business decision makers in sport, indexing 160 for management and strategy roles. And the paddock itself holds 350 partner companies. Your future customers are already standing there.
>
> And since you are a US company: the sport is booming at home. Three US races, two American teams from this season, and Apple now broadcasts F1 in the US."

### 4.4 Why this could fit Community

> "Let me tell you why I find this opportunity interesting for Community. Three reasons.
>
> **First, the product-story fit.** You built the direct channel between famous people and their audiences. F1 has twenty of the most followed athletes in the world and 827 million fans who want to get closer to them. Nobody in this sport owns the direct line yet. The team that does it first with you gets a world-first story, and you get the most visible product demo in sport.
>
> **Second, the room.** More than 350 partner companies stand in that paddock: consumer brands, banks, airlines, retailers. Every single one of them spends money on customer engagement. As a partner you meet them as a peer, race after race. That is a pipeline, not a billboard.
>
> **Third, the timing.** The sport's growth is young, digital and American, exactly your home ground. Three US races, two American teams, Apple broadcasting. A US engagement platform riding the US wave of F1 is a story that writes itself.
>
> One last thing on value. All of this sits on very small supply: eleven teams for the entire world, one seat per category, three to five year deals. Small supply, growing demand. That is why these partnerships hold their value."

### 4.5 Three activation ideas (examples, not proposals)

*[Offer these as sketches to make it concrete. Let them react; their reaction feeds the brief.]*

> "To make it concrete, three sketches. Early thoughts, nothing more.
>
> **One, the team hotline.** Fans text the team and the team texts back: race alerts, behind-the-scenes drops, ticket moments, run on Community with your AI doing the scale work. The team finally owns its audience instead of renting it from social platforms.
>
> **Two, the driver number.** A driver shares a number and says: text me. After the podium, a voice note goes back to a million fans. You invented this play with celebrities. Done with an F1 driver, it becomes a global story in one weekend.
>
> **Three, the buyer showcase.** The team's hospitality and partner events run over Community: invitations, logistics, follow-ups. Every partner company in that paddock experiences the product as a guest, then your team follows up with them as prospects."

### Q1. "Are we too small for Formula 1?"

> "I would not think about it as size. I would think about it as fit and ambition.
>
> The sport has entry points at many levels: technology partnerships, supplier tiers, driver deals, regional rights. Some of the best partnerships in F1 are scale-ups that used the platform to become global names. Airwallex entered as a fast-growing payments company, and during the partnership their revenue grew 50% year on year across EMEA and the Americas.
>
> The brief tells us what is realistic. Our job is to find the level where the numbers work for you, and to structure it so the partnership pays for itself in customers, not just exposure."

### Q2. "What is the difference between partnering with F1 itself, a team, or a driver?"

> "Three doors into the sport, and for Community each one is worth a look.
>
> **The series, Formula 1 itself.** You partner with the sport: branding at every race, global reach, neutrality. Bigger deals, premium prices.
>
> **A team.** The story lives here: the car, the drivers, the content, the hospitality, the partner ecosystem. Many more entry points and price levels. And for you, the natural home of the product showcase.
>
> **A driver personally.** For most brands the smallest door. For Community, maybe the smartest one to open first: your product was built for exactly this, one famous person, millions of fans, a direct line. Cheaper, faster, and the story is pure.
>
> The trend in the sport supports you: fans follow teams and drivers even more than the sport itself. The brief will tell us which door, or which combination."

### Q3. "Teams have thirty partners. How does one brand stand out?"

> "Fair question. Three answers.
>
> First, structure. Partners sit in clear tiers, and your tier defines exactly what you own: positions, days, content, hospitality. All contractual.
>
> Second, exclusivity. Within your category you are alone. The team cannot sign your competitor.
>
> Third, attention is won through activation, not logo size. The partners people remember are the ones whose product does something visible with the team. A driver texting a million fans is more memorable than any logo on the car, and it costs a fraction.
>
> And the access, the hospitality, the introductions: those are private rights. They never get diluted by other partners."

### Q4. "How do teams help with B2B introductions?"

> "Very concretely. This is a managed process, not a cocktail party.
>
> Every team runs a family of at least 25 partners, and the team's partnership department actively connects them: compliant introductions, partner summits, business forums on race weekends, private dinners. And it continues off the calendar at events like CES and Web Summit.
>
> Two examples from our own work. CommScope's RUCKUS brand at Haas: enterprise wireless powering the team's operations, with a live showcase in the garage; our agency ran the grid-wide process. And Airwallex at McLaren: during the partnership, revenue grew 50% year on year across EMEA and the Americas, and UK revenue grew 57% from new customer acquisition.
>
> For Community: 350 companies in that paddock, all of them spending on customer engagement, meeting you as a fellow partner. That is a pipeline, not a billboard."

### Q5. "Could a team not just build fan texting themselves?"

> "They could try, and that is true for almost every partner on the grid. Teams could build their own wireless networks, their own software, their own logistics. They do not, because they are racing teams. Their engineers build cars.
>
> That is exactly why the partner model exists: teams take proven products from partners, and the partner gets the showcase. RUCKUS runs the wireless at Haas. IBM works with Ferrari. The teams buy speed and proof, not projects.
>
> And what you bring is not just software. It is the playbook: you have run direct lines for some of the most famous people in the world. That experience is the product."

### Q6. "How is ROI measured in Formula 1?"

> "Four ways, usually.
>
> **Media value.** Independent firms like Nielsen track every second your branding appears, across TV and digital, and give it a money value against your fee.
>
> **Brand tracking.** Awareness and consideration among fans, measured before and after. In the US, fans are 32% more likely to consider a sponsor's product.
>
> **Business pipeline.** For B2B, you count introductions, meetings and deals closed through the platform. That is how Airwallex could point to 50% revenue growth during their partnership.
>
> **And for you, product metrics.** Subscribers on the team line, engagement rates, response rates. Your activation measures itself, which is a luxury most sponsors never have.
>
> You agree the framework upfront and review it with the team every quarter."

### Q7. "Who owns Formula 1? How does the money work?"

> "Three layers, very simple.
>
> Liberty Media, an American media company, owns Formula 1 itself. They bought it in 2017. They control the commercial rights: races, broadcast deals, the calendar.
>
> The FIA is the independent regulator.
>
> The eleven teams are independent businesses: Ferrari, Mercedes and Audi belong to car makers, Red Bull to the drinks company, others to investors. Each team earns a share of the sport's revenues plus its own sponsorships.
>
> Since 2021 the sport has a cost cap, so teams became profitable, stable businesses. Team sponsorship has doubled since 2020, to 2.2 billion dollars a year. You would be entering a healthy, growing market."

### Q8. "What does a partnership cost, and why is Formula 1 so expensive?"

## Part 3: The smart questions (12 to 15 minutes)

*[Conversation starters, not a form. Ask one, stay quiet, follow what they give you. Their answers become the brief.]*

**Open the story**
- "Tell me where Fisher & Paykel is right now, in your own words. What's going well, and what's the next hill to climb?"
- "What are the key focus areas for the next twelve months? What comes up in every leadership conversation?"

**The direction, and where this could play a role**
- "How do you see something like Formula 1, or sport at all, maybe playing a role in where the brand is headed? Even a rough first instinct helps; there's no wrong answer."
- "And as a business, where could you imagine it adding value: hiring engineers, your own people, the home side of the business, investors, or something I haven't thought of?"

**Who needs to know the name**
- "Clinicians know Fisher & Paykel well, I'd guess. Who doesn't know you as well as you'd like? Patients, future employees, investors, someone else?"

**The talent question**
- "How hard is it to hire the engineers you need, in Auckland and beyond? And with the new building opening next year, does that get harder or easier?"

**The end goal**
- "Zooming out: if everything goes right over the next five years, what does Fisher & Paykel look like?"
- "And what would make a partnership like this a failure in your eyes? That answer usually tells us more than the first one."

**Evaluation and success**
- "If you ever did something like this, on what basis would it be judged internally, and by whom?"
- "What would a partnership of this size need to deliver for you to call it a success after year one?"

**Customers and buyers**
- "On the hospital side, who really decides: the clinicians, procurement, or a purchasing contract? And apart from the evidence, what moves them?"
- "On the sleep side, how much does the patient's own preference decide which mask they end up wearing, compared with the doctor or the equipment provider?"

**The rules**
- "I know your industry has strict codes on hospitality for clinicians. How does your compliance team think about sport and events in general? I'd rather know the lines now than design something you can't use."

**Markets**
- "North America is almost half your revenue. Is that where the growth focus is, or is it Europe, China, somewhere else? The calendar runs five continents, with three races in the US and one right next door in Melbourne."

**Brand and marketing today**
- "How does Fisher & Paykel market itself today? I'm guessing most of it is clinical education, conferences and evidence. Is there any brand or consumer marketing at all, maybe on the sleep side?"
- "Is the bigger job right now top of the funnel or bottom of the funnel: being known by people who've never heard of you, or converting the people who already know you?"

**Budget and timing**
- "The unromantic question: is there a budget range in mind for something like this? I ask early because it decides which doors we even look at, and it saves us both time."
- "And timing: is this a now conversation or a next-year conversation? Both are fine. I know your half-year results land in late November, so I'll work around that."

**Decision process**
- "Apart from you, who'd be part of this decision? Marketing, people and culture, compliance, the CEO, the board?"

**Hospitality**
- "Last one, my favorite. If I gave you a paddock weekend tomorrow, who would you bring, and why those people? That answer usually tells me what a partnership is really for."

*[If they name clinicians or hospital buyers, don't argue; say: "Understood, and I know your rules may not allow that. Who else would be on the list?" Their answer, engineers, top performers, partners, investors, is the real brief.]*

## Part 4: How Formula 1 works, and where it could fit (8 to 10 minutes)

*[Calibrate to their answer from Part 2. Keep it careful and plain; this room trusts evidence, not adjectives. Invite questions: "Stop me anytime."]*

### 4.1 The sport in thirty seconds

> "Quick picture of how the sport is set up, because it explains a brand's options.
>
> Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company: the calendar, the broadcasts, the races. The FIA writes the rules. And then eleven teams, including two American ones, Haas and Cadillac, each an independent business with its own partners.
>
> So a brand can enter at three levels: the sport, a team, or a driver personally. They come in very different sizes, and for Fisher & Paykel I'd be straight from the start: the interesting doors are the right-sized ones."

### 4.2 What a team partnership includes

> "What do you get as a partner? Five parts, quickly.
>
> **Branding:** your name on the car, the suits, the garage, around 24 weekends a year, 1.8 billion total audience last season.
>
> **The official title:** Official Something Partner of the team, usable in your own marketing, your hiring and your investor story.
>
> **Hospitality:** paddock access and the Paddock Club. One detail people miss: passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in a weekend. For you, that means the guests your own rules allow: your people, partners, investors.
>
> **Content:** access to the team's channels and drivers. This is the number one sport in the world for social engagement, 2.2 billion engagements, ahead of the NBA.
>
> **People:** driver appearances, filming days, hot laps for your best people.
>
> And around it all, the business platform: 25-plus partners per team, 350-plus across the grid. That's a network of serious companies in its own right."

### 4.3 The audience

*[Three audiences for Fisher & Paykel, and one honest gap. Say the gap out loud.]*

> "Who you'd reach. For Fisher & Paykel I'd think about three groups, and one I'd leave out.
>
> **The engineers you want to hire.** This sport is an engineering magnet, and its audience skews young: 43% of fans are under 35. If the next great Fisher & Paykel engineer is out there, there's a decent chance they watch this.
>
> **The patient at home.** 827 million fans, 42% of them women. F1 fans rank number one in sport for researching online before they buy, and for sticking with brands they like. For something a person wears every night, that loyalty is interesting, if your home business wants a brand. And in the US, almost half your revenue, purchase consideration among fans is up 32%.
>
> **Your own people and your investors.** Half your workforce is in New Zealand, and the nearest race is Melbourne. And the room in the Paddock Club is two thirds senior executives.
>
> **The one I'd leave out: your hospital customers.** This sport won't put you in front of intensive care doctors, and your rules wouldn't let it. I'd rather say that now than have you spot it later."

### 4.4 Why this could fit Fisher & Paykel

> "Why we thought this was worth a call. Three reasons, and you tell me if they hold.
>
> **One, it's your story on a bigger stage.** Fisher & Paykel exists because engineers sat down with a doctor and solved a problem with the human body. Formula 1 is the world's loudest example of engineers doing that under hard limits: capped money, capped computing, and from this year, fully sustainable fuel. A human being in the middle of it, breathing hard, for two hours. You wouldn't be borrowing a story. You'd be telling yours where 827 million people are already watching.
>
> **Two, the people job.** You've got nearly a thousand people in R&D, a new building opening next year, and a global hunt for engineers. Standing next to this sport tells young engineers something about you. And for seventy-six hundred people, half of them in New Zealand and over two thousand in Tijuana, it's something to be proud of at work.
>
> **Three, the home side.** Your sleep business sells something deeply personal: a mask someone wears every night. That's the one part of Fisher & Paykel where a patient's own view of the brand might matter, and this audience is the most brand-loyal in sport. Whether that's a job worth doing is your call, not mine.
>
> Two honest notes. First, this won't sell a single humidifier to an intensive care unit, and your rules keep clinicians out of the paddock, so we'd never build the case on that. Second, size: you can afford a team seat, but a company that puts ten percent of revenue into research shouldn't buy one on instinct. Whatever we recommend will start small and sharp, and if the honest answer is that nothing here earns its place, we'll say exactly that."

*[Optional, only if verified on call day: McLaren, the team that won the last two Constructors' titles, was founded by a New Zealander, Bruce McLaren. It's a lovely line for a Kiwi engineering company. Do not say it unchecked.]*

### 4.5 Three early sketches (ideas, not proposals)

*[Offer as sketches; their reaction feeds the brief.]*

> "To make it concrete, three sketches, all sized for Fisher & Paykel on purpose.
>
> **One, the breathing story.** A content series about how engineers design for the human body under pressure, from a race car cockpit to a hospital bed, told by your own engineers with a team's channels behind it. Your fruit-jar beginning is a better origin story than most brands could buy. Content-level money, well below a team seat.
>
> **Two, the engineers' program.** A right-sized partnership with one team, built around people: a graduate engineering challenge across Auckland and Tijuana, team engineers visiting East Tāmaki when the new building opens, and your best people hosted in Melbourne. Seven passes, twenty guests, all of them your own people or partners your rules allow. Entry-level money at most, and only if the brief earns it.
>
> **Three, the sleep and recovery story.** If the home side wants a brand: a driver program around sleep and recovery. Around twenty-four weekends on five continents, with all that travel, makes sleep and recovery a natural story for a driver. Everything patient-facing would go through your own regulatory review. Driver-level money, below a team seat, and ideally a driver with a link to your key markets, which we'd check when we map it."

### Q1. "What are comparable brands doing in Formula 1, and why are they using it?"

> "Straight answer first: I can't point you to a big medtech pattern in this sport with confidence, and I won't invent one. We'd map health and medical names properly in the analysis.
>
> The closer comparisons are in two places. One is human performance: Technogym has been McLaren's fitness partner since 2004, which tells you a body-and-performance story can last decades in this sport. The other is engineering-led companies using it for trust and talent: Atlassian took the title at Williams, Oracle's name is on Red Bull, HP's on Ferrari, Microsoft reportedly signed with Mercedes in January, and AWS has been the sport's official cloud provider since 2018. And there are working partnerships, where the brand visibly does a job for the team: CommScope's RUCKUS brand powers the Haas team's operations with a live showcase in the garage.
>
> Why they do it? Usually trust, the room, and proof. And when the brief is right, it shows up in numbers: Airwallex, a fintech at McLaren, grew revenue 50% year on year across EMEA and the Americas during the partnership, and UK revenue 57% from new customers. For you, the room part mostly doesn't transfer. Trust, talent and proof might."

### Q2. "How do F1 teams drive introductions in business?"

> "Very concretely. It's a managed process, not a cocktail party.
>
> Every team runs a family of 25-plus partners, and the team's partnership department actively connects them: compliant introductions, partner summits, business forums on race weekends, private dinners. Add the hospitality rights, and you choose who sits at your table.
>
> For Fisher & Paykel I'd be careful about what that's worth. It's not a way to meet hospital buyers. It could be a way to meet suppliers, technology and logistics partners, and investors, inside whatever your compliance team is comfortable with."

### Q3. "How is ROI measured in Formula 1?"

> "Four ways, and you agree the framework upfront.
>
> **Media value:** independent firms like Nielsen track every second your brand appears, across TV and digital, and price it against your fee.
>
> **Brand tracking:** awareness and trust, measured before and after, in the audience you care about: engineering talent, patients at home, investors.
>
> **Business outcomes:** for you that's mostly people numbers: applications, offer acceptance, engagement scores. Plus partner introductions, and home-side sales if that's a goal. That's how Airwallex could point at 50% revenue growth during their McLaren years.
>
> **Content performance:** reach and engagement on everything you build.
>
> For Fisher & Paykel I'd weight it to the job you name today. If it's talent, we measure hiring, not impressions."

### Q4. "Which entry would fit us best?"

> "Today, instinct only: content first, a people-focused program second, and maybe a right-sized team seat later if those earn it. I wouldn't respect you by pretending that's a recommendation.
>
> The mapping after this call turns it into names, numbers and a sequence against your brief and your compliance rules. And if the honest answer is that nothing fits, that's what we'll bring back."

### Q5. "What does a partnership cost?"

> "The price follows the rights. Rule of thumb: entry-level team positions start in the low single-digit millions a year, official partner tiers sit above that, and driver, content and pure hospitality programs sit below, some well below.
>
> Why expensive? Eleven teams for the whole world, one seat per category, three-to-five-year deals. Scarcity.
>
> For Fisher & Paykel, the shapes I'd explore first mostly live at the smaller end. And our advice is free either way: the rights holders pay our fee."

### Q6. "Why work with SPORTFIVE? Could we not go to a team directly?"

> "You could. Two reasons brands use us anyway.
>
> One, information. A team sells you its own story; we put all eleven next to each other: inventory, pricing, appetite, and what each would mean for Fisher & Paykel specifically, including what works under your rules. For more than twelve years we worked exclusively with Mercedes and Ferrari; we gave that up deliberately to sit on the brand's side, across the whole grid. The neutral seat is the product.
>
> Two, process. We build the business case, benchmark the price and negotiate on your behalf, because we do it every week.
>
> And since the rights holders pay our fee, it costs you nothing to use us. There's no version where you pay more."

### Q7. "Are any of our competitors already in Formula 1?"

> "I won't answer that from memory. Health, medical and sleep brands show up around this sport in different forms, and you deserve the precise picture, not my best guess.
>
> Mapping medical devices, health and sleep across all eleven teams and the series is part of the analysis after this call. You'd see who's in, who's not, and where the open positions are."

### Q8. "Our customers are intensive care doctors and hospital buyers. They choose on clinical evidence, and our codes stop us taking them to a race. So what's the point of Formula 1 for a company like ours?"

> "You're right on both counts, and I'd rather agree with you than argue.
>
> A clinician picks your products because of the evidence and what they see at the bedside. A logo on a car won't change that, and I'd be wary of anyone who says it will. And the industry codes on hospitality are clear: clinicians and hospital buyers don't belong on a race weekend guest list. So if the only job you'd hire this for is selling to hospitals, the answer is no, and I'll put that in writing.
>
> But that's not the only job a company your size has. You need engineers, lots of them, in Auckland, Tijuana and beyond, and you're competing with every tech company for them. You've got seventy-six hundred people who could be proud of where they work. You've got investors in two markets, and a race next door. And you've got a home business where a patient wears your mask every night.
>

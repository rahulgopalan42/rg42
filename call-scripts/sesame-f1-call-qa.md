> "Some will, and some won't, and I'd rather be straight about that. Teams run background checks on every partner. For an AI you talk to, they'll look at three things: children, people leaning on it too much, and voices being copied.

### "Could a driver or an engineer be a voice in our product?"

> "It's possible in principle, but it's a rights question, and I won't promise it. There are three sets of rights. The driver owns their own voice and image, and usually has their own management for that. The team usually has rights to the driver in team kit, for the team's partners. And the radio you hear in the broadcast is part of Formula 1's coverage, so Formula 1 controls that, not the team. So no team radio clips, and nothing trained on them.
>
> It has been done once that I know of. Two years ago, Aston Martin and Fernando Alonso launched an AI version of his voice with ElevenLabs. It reads the team's website articles aloud, in English, Spanish and French. That was done with the driver and the team together, and it only reads what the team writes.
>
> There's your side as well. Your own policy bans a digital replica of a person without their consent. And the law is moving. A US bill on AI copies of a person's voice cleared a Senate committee in June, though it isn't law yet. Fake AI clips of drivers are already going around, too. So anything here would have to be clearly agreed and clearly labelled.
>
> So the realistic first step isn't a voice in the product. It's a real driver or engineer, in their own voice, on film, talking about conversation. If that goes well, a licensed voice is a later conversation, with the driver's own people, the team and probably Formula 1 in it."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

### "What's the biggest risk?"

> "Signing a logo with no job. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and the ones that get lost put a name on the car and stop there. With investors' money, that's hard to defend. A deal needs a real job, something fans can do, and someone on your side who owns it.
>
> The second risk is a conflict you didn't see. Every one of the eleven teams has an AI name or an eyewear name close to you, and contracts often cover more than the title says. That's why we check every team before your name goes anywhere.
>

> The fifth is picking the wrong option for the job. If the job is reaching young people in the US, F1 is one way to do it, and there are others. We work across a lot of sports, so we'd rather tell you that up front than sell you the wrong thing."

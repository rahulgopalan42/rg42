# Redo x SPORTFIVE: F1 Discovery Call Script

**Call type:** First call / discovery. Goal: introduce yourself and SPORTFIVE, understand where Redo stands, fill out the brief, explain how F1 partnerships work, agree next steps.

**How to use this script:** Every spoken block is written the way you would say it out loud. Do not read it word for word. Glance at the bold anchors, then talk. Short sentences, slow pace, warm tone. The listener is a non-native English speaker, so keep it simple and concrete throughout. Let her talk more than you.

## Part 3: The brief. Key discovery questions (12 to 15 minutes)

*[Work through these as a conversation, not a checklist. Follow her energy. The bold labels are for you; the questions are for her. Capture answers, they become the brief.]*

**Objectives**
- "What is the business goal behind this interest? Is it awareness for Redo, winning new merchants, credibility for the global expansion, or something else?"
- "Imagine this works perfectly. Three years from now, what has changed for Redo because of Formula 1?"

**Audience**
- "Who do you want to reach first: the brands and retailers who could run on Redo, or the shoppers who see Redo at checkout?"
- "Describe your ideal customer for me. What size of merchant, which sectors, which platforms?"

**Markets**
- "Which markets matter most for the next two to three years? Is this a US story, a Europe story, or truly global? Where does the expansion go first?"

**Product and integration**
- "Here is one I am curious about. Would you want Redo working inside the sport, not just visible on it? Every team runs its own online store, selling merchandise to fans all over the world. Real orders, real shipping, real returns. A team store powered by Redo would be a live showcase of your product. Is something like that interesting, or is this more about visibility and access?"

**Budget and timing**
- "Have you set a budget range for this? Even a rough range helps me filter what is realistic and saves us both time."
- "When would you want to be live? Are we thinking about the start of a season, or could you enter mid-season?"
- "Partnerships here usually run multi-year, three to five seasons. Is that a horizon you can plan on?"

**Decision process**
- "Apart from you, who is part of this decision? Founders, board, anyone else we should keep in mind?"

**Marketing context**
- "What is working for you today in marketing? Have you done sponsorship before, in any sport?"
- "Do you have a team that could activate a partnership, content, events, hospitality? Or would you want support there too?"

**Hospitality and relationships**
- "How important is client entertainment for you? Are there key merchants, partners or investors you would love to host at a race?"

*[If she answers the budget question with hesitation:]*

> "No problem. You do not need a number today. When we come back with recommendations, we will show options at different levels, and you will see what each level buys."

## Part 4: How Formula 1 partnerships work (8 to 10 minutes)

*[Calibrate to her answer from Part 2. If she knows the sport, compress this. If she is new, go slowly. Invite questions as you go: "Stop me anytime."]*

### 4.1 The sport in thirty seconds

> "Let me give you a simple picture of how the sport is set up, because it explains the options you have.
>
> Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company. They own the commercial side: the calendar, the broadcast deals, the races.
>
> The FIA is the regulator. They write the rules.
>
> And then there are eleven teams. From this coming season it is eleven, including two American teams, Haas and Cadillac. Each team is an independent business with its own owners and its own partnerships. Some belong to car makers, like Ferrari, Mercedes and Audi. Some to consumer giants, like Red Bull. Some to investors, like Williams, owned by a US investment firm.
>
> So a brand can partner at three levels: with the sport itself, with a team, or with a driver personally. Most brands enter through a team, and I can explain why when we get there."

### 4.2 What a team partnership includes

> "Now, what do you get as a partner? Think of it in five parts.
>
> **One, branding.** Your logo on the car, on the drivers' race suits, on the garage. Around 24 race weekends a year, in front of a global TV audience. Last season the total audience was 1.8 billion, and the average race reached 76 million people.
>
> **Two, the official title.** You become, for example, the Official E-commerce Technology Partner of a team. You can use the team's name, logo and imagery in your own marketing. Your website, your sales decks, your campaigns. For a young company, that badge buys instant credibility.
>
> **Three, hospitality.** This is where Formula 1 is special. Paddock access, the Paddock Club, garage tours, time with the drivers. To give you a feel: 66% of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. Every race is a weekend where you host the people you most want to do business with, in a place money alone cannot book.
>
> **Four, content and digital.** Access to the team's channels, the drivers, behind-the-scenes content made with you. Formula 1 is the number one sport in the world for social engagement, 2.2 billion engagements, ahead of the NBA. And in the sport's own fan survey, 61% of surveyed fans engage with F1 content every day.
>
> **Five, people.** Driver appearances, team bosses at your events, hot laps for your top clients, filming days for your campaigns. The team becomes part of your marketing engine, all year, not only on race days.
>
> And around all of this sits the business platform, which for a B2B company might be the most valuable part. Every team carries at least 25 partners. Across the grid, that is more than 350 companies. These companies want to do business with each other, and the teams make the introductions. I can go deeper on that whenever you like."

### 4.3 The audience

> "A quick picture of who watches, because it may surprise you.
>
> Formula 1 has 827 million fans, and the base has grown 63% since 2018. It is the fastest growing major sport property in the world.
>
> The profile has changed completely. 42% of fans are women. 43% are under 35. This is a young, digital audience now.
>
> They are also valuable shoppers. F1 fans are the most brand-loyal fans in sport. They research online before they buy, more than fans of any other sport. And they buy premium versions of products, again more than any other sport's fans.
>
> One more thing, since your home market is the US. The sport is booming there. Three US races: Austin, Miami, Las Vegas. Two American teams from this season. And Apple now broadcasts F1 in the US. US fans are 32% more likely to consider a sponsor's product. Among women it is 41%."

### 4.4 Why this could fit Redo

> "Now let me tell you why I find this opportunity interesting for Redo. Two reasons.
>
> **First, the audience multiplier.** The fans the teams are chasing are the same people your merchants are chasing. Young, digital, shopping online, loyal to brands they trust. So one activation works twice for you. It builds Redo with the brands you sell to, and at the same time it puts Redo in front of their shoppers. Very few platforms give you both sides in one buy.
>
> **Second, the room itself.** More than 350 partner companies stand in that paddock, and consumer and retail brands alone are spending close to half a billion dollars on the sport this year. Those are exactly the companies that could run on Redo. As a partner, you are not a vendor knocking on their door. You are a fellow guest, standing next to them at every race.
>
> And here is the thing most people miss about Formula 1. What really drives the value of these partnerships is not the logo. It is the access. The premium experiences the sport delivers, on track and off track, are the best in all of sport. Nobody does hospitality like Formula 1. That is what partners are buying, and that is what opens the doors."

## Part 5: Questions she may ask. Prepared answers.

### "What is the difference between partnering with F1 itself, a team, or a driver?"

> "Good question. Three doors into the sport.
>
> **The series, Formula 1 itself.** You partner with the sport, not with one team. Trackside branding at every race, race title rights, global reach, and you stay neutral, you never lose on a Sunday. But these are fewer, bigger deals at premium prices, and you are not part of a story. You are everywhere, but you belong to no one.
>
> **A team.** This is where the emotion lives. The car, the drivers, the wins, the setbacks. You get season-long visibility, content, hospitality, and the team's whole business ecosystem. There are many more entry points and price levels than at series level. For most brands, this is the right door.
>
> **A driver personally.** The smallest and simplest option. Space on a cap or helmet, the driver's personal social channels, some appearance days. Cheaper, yes. But you get no team rights, no car, no paddock hospitality, and the driver's team has to approve everything.
>
> The clear trend in the sport is that fans follow teams and drivers even more than the sport itself. Story beats logo. That is why our conversations usually start at team level, and the brief will tell us which team."

### "How do teams help with B2B introductions?"

> "Very concretely. This is a managed process, not a cocktail party.
>
> Every team runs a family of at least 25 partners, and the team's partnership department actively connects them. They make compliant introductions between partners, they host partner summits, business forums on race weekends, private dinners and roundtables. And it goes beyond the calendar, at events like CES, Davos and Cannes Lions.
>
> The best example from our own work is Airwallex, the payments company, a partner of McLaren, a deal our agency brokered. During the partnership, their revenue grew 50% year on year across EMEA and the Americas, and UK revenue grew 57% from new customer acquisition. That is what the platform can do when a partner works it properly.
>
> For Redo, picture it simply: 350 companies across the grid, many of them consumer brands and retailers, meeting each other all season. That is a pipeline, not a billboard."

### "How is ROI measured in Formula 1?"

> "Four ways, usually, and good teams report on all of them.
>
> **Media value.** Independent measurement firms, Nielsen for example, track every second your branding is on screen, across TV and digital, and give it a money value. You compare that number to your fee.
>
> **Brand tracking.** Awareness and consideration among fans, measured before and after. And F1 fans reward sponsors: in the US they are 32% more likely to consider a sponsor's product.
>
> **Business pipeline.** For B2B, you count introductions made, meetings held, and deals closed through the platform. That is how Airwallex could point to 50% revenue growth during their partnership.
>
> **Hospitality outcomes.** Which clients and prospects you hosted, and what those relationships produced afterwards.
>
> You agree the framework upfront, and you review it with the team every quarter. The good teams manage a partnership like a business, not like a sticker on a car."

### "Who owns Formula 1? How does the money work?"

> "Three layers, very simple.
>
> Liberty Media, an American media company, owns Formula 1 itself. They bought it in 2017. They control the commercial rights: races, broadcast deals, the calendar.
>
> The FIA is the independent regulator. Rules and safety.
>
> The eleven teams are independent businesses. Ferrari, Mercedes and Audi belong to car makers. Red Bull to the drinks company. Williams to a US investment firm. Aston Martin to a consortium led by Lawrence Stroll. Each team earns money in two ways: a share of the sport's revenues, and its own sponsorships.
>
> One thing worth knowing: since 2021 the sport has a cost cap. Teams cannot outspend each other without limit anymore. The result is that teams became profitable, stable businesses, and their values have grown sharply. Team sponsorship has doubled since 2020, from about one billion to 2.2 billion dollars a year, across 335 deals. So you would be entering a healthy, growing market. You can see it in the calibre of brands arriving: Microsoft joined Mercedes just this year."

### "What does a partnership cost?"

*[Only if she asks. Stay qualitative, ranges not quotes.]*

> "A wide range, and that is the honest answer.
>
> As a rule of thumb: entry-level and supplier positions start in the low single-digit millions per year. Meaningful official partner positions with real rights and hospitality usually sit in the mid single-digit millions. Title partnerships, the name on the team, are a different world, well above fifty million a year.
>
> This is exactly why we do the brief first. Our job is to match the level to your goals, and to negotiate it on your side. And remember, our advice costs you nothing."

*[Background for you, never quoted on the call: reported title benchmarks are around $75M to $110M a year at the top teams.]*

### "Why work with SPORTFIVE? Could we not go to a team directly?"

> "You could, and some brands do. Brands use us for two reasons.
>
> First, information. A team will sell you their own story. We know the whole grid: every team's inventory, pricing, appetite and roadmap. You see all your options next to each other, not one option in isolation.
>
> Second, negotiation. We do this every week, so we know what things should cost and which rights matter. We build the business case, we run the process, we negotiate on your behalf.
>
> And because the teams pay our fee, all of that costs you nothing. There is no scenario where you pay more by using us."

### "Are we too small for Formula 1?"

> "I would not think about it as size. I would think about it as fit and ambition.
>
> The sport has entry points at many levels, and some of the best partnerships in F1 are scale-ups using the platform to become global names. Airwallex is the example I keep coming back to: they entered as a fast-growing payments company, and the partnership became part of how the world got to know them.
>
> You have just raised a big round to go international. That is usually exactly the moment this platform makes sense. The brief will tell us if it does."

### "Is the audience not mostly men who love cars?"

> "It was, years ago. Not anymore.
>
> Today 42% of F1 fans are women, up from 37% in 2018. In the sport's own fan survey, three out of four new fans are women, and half of the Gen Z respondents are women. The average age of fans is falling.
>
> This is now one of the youngest, most digital, most female audiences in major sport. That shift is exactly why consumer brands are arriving."

### "Are any of our competitors already in Formula 1?"

> "I will not answer that from memory, because this market moves weekly and you deserve an exact answer.
>
> Mapping your category across all eleven teams is part of the grid analysis we do after this call. When we come back with recommendations, you will see precisely who from your world is in, who is not, and where the open positions are."

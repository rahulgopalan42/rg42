**The call:** intro call with Alex Lloyd, Head of Marketing at Advanced Navigation (from the outreach email; confirm), following Rahul's outreach about Formula 1. Joining from SPORTFIVE: Rahul, Bjorn and George. The email put them forward as a possible Official Navigation Technology Partner, on the idea that an F1 team depends on measuring position and movement precisely, with Hexagon at Red Bull as the reference.

## 1. Brand snapshot (not spoken)

- **In plain English:** "They make the navigation that tells a machine exactly where it is and which way it's pointing, and it keeps working when GPS drops out. Mining trucks underground, underwater drones, even Moon landers."
- **What they do (verified):** fibre-optic gyroscopes, inertial navigation units (Boreas, Certus), underwater positioning, AI software and a small subsea drone (Hydrus). Founded in Sydney in 2012 by Xavier Orr and Chris Shaw; Shaw is CEO.
- **Model (verified):** hardware plus software, sold B2B to engineering teams. Builds in Sydney and Perth, has a UK base for Europe and a US arm, Advanced Navigation America.
- **Scale and funding (verified):** Series C of A$158M (US$110M) in March 2026, led by Airtree, with A$50M from the National Reconstruction Fund Corporation. Filed FY2025 revenue A$57.7M, up 73%, with a net loss of A$14.3M, more than halved (ASIC filings via Forbes Australia). 170+ staff in Australia, 172 roles planned.
- **Their claims:** unicorn status (no valuation given); revenue above US$100M this year and roughly double next year; over 80% of revenue from the US and Europe; 100,000+ systems deployed (Shaw to Forbes Australia and company releases).
- **Civilian customers (their case studies):** BHP and Fortescue underground, NOAA at sea, Intuitive Machines in space.
- **Recent news (verified):** 6 October 2026, expanded US manufacturing in Huntsville, Alabama, a navigation "Centre of Excellence" that mining outlets framed as kit for America's largest mines; production reported to start in 2027. New head of APAC this year. Acquisitions planned within 12 to 18 months (Shaw, March 2026). Big defense news too (prep notes only).
- **Priorities:** factories, the US and Europe, hiring, acquisitions. The raise is for that, not marketing.
- **Sports and marketing history:** no sports sponsorship found (fresh search, 25 October 2026). Marketing looks technical: trade shows, case studies, webinars, and they're hiring field and product marketers (our read from job ads; ask).

## 3. Questions to ask them

*[A conversation, not a form. Ask one, then stop and listen.]*

**1. Priorities**

> "From the outside, the big job looks like going from an Australian company that sells abroad to a properly global one, building in the US and growing in Europe. Is that fair? What's the hardest part?"

- *Follow-up:* "And the acquisitions you've talked about, is that more about technology or about new markets?"
- *If they say getting known abroad is the hard part:* "That's useful. It decides which doors we'd even look at."

**2. Markets**

> "Which markets matter most for you over the next two or three years? And where does Australia sit, beyond being home?"

- *Follow-up:* "Is there anywhere you feel less known than you should be?"
- *If they say the US:* "The US side of the sport's changed a lot. Three races there now, two American teams, and Apple's broadcasting it."

**3. Buyers and decision makers**

> "Who signs for your systems, usually? The chief engineer, the head of autonomy, procurement? And is that the same person in mining as in marine or space?"

- *Follow-up:* "If I gave you a paddock weekend tomorrow, who would you bring?"
- *If they name accounts:* "That's exactly the kind of list a hosting program gets built around."

**4. Marketing today**

> "What does marketing look like for you right now? I'm guessing trade shows, test data and case studies more than brand. What's working, and what isn't?"

- *Follow-up:* "Have you ever done anything in sport? And is there anything you'd never do?"
- *If they say events carry most of the load:* "Makes sense. Part of what F1 does well is hosting, so it might sit next to your events plan rather than replace any of it."

**5. B2B, and the other audiences**

> "I'm assuming it's B2B all the way. But are there other people you care about reaching? The engineers you want to hire, or people at home in Australia?"

- *Follow-up:* "With the hiring you've got planned, how hard is it to find the people you need?"
- *If hiring's a pain:* "Then that goes in the brief. It changes what a good result looks like."

**6. Measuring success**

> "If you did something like this, how would it get judged internally, and by whom? Pipeline, being known abroad, hiring?"

- *Follow-up:* "And what would make it a failure in your eyes? That usually tells me more."

**7. F1 familiarity**

> "How much do you follow Formula 1, if at all? It's pretty hard to miss in Australia when Melbourne comes round."

- *If they barely know it:* "Quick version: there's the series, eleven teams that each run as their own business, and the drivers. A brand can come in at any of those levels, and they're very different sizes."

**8. Decision, budget and timing**

> "The unromantic one. Is there a rough budget range in mind, and who else would be part of a decision like this? I ask early because it decides which doors are worth your time."

- *Follow-up:* "And is this a this-year conversation or a next-year one? Both are fine."
- *If they won't give a number:* "That's fine. We'd come back with options at different levels, including the smallest sensible ones."

## 7. If they ask: "Where could our product fit in the team?"

*[Ideas to test with each team's technical department, never claims. Never say or imply any team uses or needs their kit, or what positioning, sensor or timing systems any team or the series uses.]*

- **Design and simulation (most plausible to test):** teams run driver-in-the-loop simulators that need accurate models of every circuit, and correlate the sim with the track. Precise position and orientation data is part of how surveys and mobile maps get built, a market Advanced Navigation sells into (check how each team sources circuit models).
- **Testing and vehicle dynamics (test with care):** measuring exactly how a car, or a rig in the factory, moves: position, attitude, speed. Testing is limited and the rules restrict what goes on a race car, so this starts as a factory rig, filming day or older-car test idea.
- **Trackside, logistics, IT, fan platforms, merchandise, hospitality:** no believable product job we can see; these stay marketing-led.
- **What an Official Navigation Technology Partner role could look like:** the title to use in their own marketing and sales; one defined technical project with the engineering team; product supplied in kind plus a cash component; content rights to tell the story; and hosting. Hexagon at Red Bull is the shape, not the size.

> "I'd be careful here, because I don't want to guess what a team's engineers need. But if I think about where precise navigation could do a real job, two places come to mind. One is simulation, where teams need very accurate models of every circuit. The other is testing, measuring exactly how a car or a rig moves. Whether there's a real job in either is a question for each team's technical people, and we'd ask it properly. If there is, that's your navigation partner role. If there isn't, we'll say so and look at the marketing side instead."

## 8. Other questions they may ask

**"What does it cost?"**

> "The price follows the rights. Entry-level and supplier positions with a team start in the low single-digit millions a year, official partner tiers sit above that, and driver, content and pure hospitality programs sit below. For where you are, I'd start at the smaller end."

**"Why SPORTFIVE? Why not go to a team directly?"**

> "You could. But a team will sell you its own story. We put all eleven side by side, prices, appetite, what each would mean for you, and we negotiate on your side. We worked only with Mercedes and Ferrari for more than twelve years and gave that up to work this way. And the rights holders pay us, so it costs you nothing."

**"Which team would fit? Your email said some teams were looking for a navigation partner."**

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. What I meant is there are teams we work with where an engineering partner like you could make sense. But the right one depends on your markets, your buyers, your budget, and whether the category's free at each team. That's what the grid mapping after this call is for."

*[Before the call, check with Bjorn which teams sat behind that line in the email, and only repeat what's still true. Claim no category open.]*

**"How would we measure ROI?"**

> "We agree it upfront. Media value, so what the exposure's worth. Brand tracking with the people you care about, engineering buyers in the US and Europe. Pipeline, so meetings and deals that come through the platform. And hospitality outcomes, who came and what happened next. For you I'd add hiring."

**"Our buyers are engineers who buy on test data, not logos. Why spend money here?"**

> "Fair, and you're right on the first part. Nobody buys a navigation system because of a logo. But you only get to run the test if the buyer knows you and trusts you enough to try. That's where this can help: getting in the room, getting on the shortlist, and hiring. And on the money, that's why we'd start small. If none of it beats what the same spend does at your next trade show, we'll tell you."

*[If they raise their defense work, or the defense wins the email mentioned: "Fair question. It's something we check with the rights holders before anything moves, not after. And the story we'd build is the engineering one." Then move back to mining, ocean, space and autonomy.]*

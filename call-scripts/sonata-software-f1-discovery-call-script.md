# Sonata Software x SPORTFIVE: F1 Discovery Call Script

**Call type:** First call / discovery, listening-led. Goal: open warm and short, say why Sonata caught our eye, make the goal plain (understand if it makes sense, not sell), let them talk, fill the brief, and show why this sport is where mid-size IT challengers buy stature with enterprise buyers. Agree next steps only if the conversation earns them.

**How to use this script:** Every spoken block is written the way you'd say it out loud, contractions and all. Don't read it word for word; glance at the bold anchors and talk. The listeners are IT services leaders; they pitch enterprises for a living and will notice padding instantly. Keep it plain, concrete and short, and let them talk more than you.

## Part 3: The smart questions (12 to 15 minutes)

*[Conversation starters, not a form. Ask one, stay quiet, follow what they give you. Their answers become the brief.]*

**Open the story**
- "Tell me where Sonata is right now, in your own words. What's going well, and what's the next hill to climb?"
- "What are the key focus areas for the next twelve months? What comes up in every leadership meeting?"

**The direction, and where this could play a role**
- "How do you see something like Formula 1 maybe playing a role in where the brand is headed? A rough first instinct is fine; there's no wrong answer."
- "And as a business, where could you imagine it adding value: brand stature, named accounts, markets, talent?"

**The challenger question**
- "When you lose a deal to a bigger firm, what's the real reason? I ask because if the answer is ever 'they felt safer', that's a brand problem, and it's the exact problem this platform is known for solving."

**The end goal**
- "Zooming out: at the end of it, what are you trying to accomplish? If everything goes right over three years, what does Sonata look like?"
- "And the other side: what would make a partnership like this a failure in your eyes?"

**Evaluation and success**
- "On what basis would a partnership be evaluated internally, and by whom?"
- "What would a partnership of this magnitude need to deliver for you to call it a success after year one?"

**Customers and accounts**
- "Which sectors and accounts matter most right now: retail, travel, manufacturing, financial services? And who signs: the CIO, the transformation lead?"
- "Are there named accounts you'd build a program around? The partner rooms of this sport overlap heavily with the world you sell to."

**Markets**
- "Where does growth come from in the plan: the US, Europe, the Middle East? The calendar covers all of them, and the US alone has three races."

**Marketing today, and the funnel**
- "What does your marketing focus on today, and what's working?"
- "Is the bigger job top of the funnel or bottom of the funnel: being known by enterprise buyers who've never heard of Sonata, or converting and growing the accounts already in play?"

**Budget and timing**
- "The unromantic question: is there a budget range in mind? I ask early because it decides which doors we even look at, and it saves us both time."
- "And timing: now conversation, or next-financial-year conversation? Both are fine; they just change what we bring back."

**Decision process**
- "Apart from you, who'd be part of this decision? The CEO, the board? And as a listed company, is there anything about how a decision like this gets made that we should understand early?"

**Hospitality**
- "Last one, my favorite. If I gave you a paddock weekend tomorrow, who would you bring, and why those people? That answer usually tells me what a partnership is really for."

*[If they hesitate on budget:]*

> "No problem, you don't need a number today. If we get to recommendations, you'll see options at different levels, including the smallest sensible ones, with what each level buys."

## Part 4: How Formula 1 works, and where it could fit (8 to 10 minutes)

*[Calibrate to their answer from Part 2. The comparable-brands pattern is the heart of the pitch for Sonata; give it real airtime. Invite questions: "Stop me anytime."]*

### 4.1 The sport in thirty seconds

> "Quick picture of how the sport is set up, because it explains a brand's options.
>
> Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company: the calendar, the broadcasts, the races. The FIA writes the rules. And then eleven teams, including two American ones, Haas and Cadillac, each an independent business with its own partners.
>
> So a brand can enter at three levels: the sport, a team, or a driver personally. For Sonata the team route is where the pattern lives, and the sizes range from entry seats in the low single-digit millions up to the big tiers. The brief decides the level."

### 4.2 What a team partnership includes

> "What do you get as a partner? Five parts, quickly.
>
> **Branding:** your name on the car, the suits, the garage, around 24 weekends a year, 1.8 billion total audience last season, 76 million average per race.
>
> **The official title:** Official Digital Transformation Partner of a team, for example, usable in all your marketing and every client conversation. For an IT services firm, that title does work in RFPs that's hard to buy any other way.
>
> **Hospitality:** paddock access and the Paddock Club, where 66% of guests are senior executives. One detail people miss: passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend.
>
> **Content:** access to the team's channels and drivers. The number one sport in the world for social engagement, 2.2 billion engagements, ahead of the NBA.
>
> **People:** driver appearances, filming days, hot laps for your top clients.
>
> And around it all, the business platform: 25-plus partners per team, more than 350 companies across the grid. That room is an account list wearing passes."

### 4.3 The audience

*[B2B first for Sonata: the buyers, then the global stage.]*

> "Who you reach. For Sonata I'd be precise.
>
> **The buyers.** Formula 1 has the highest concentration of business decision makers in sport: fans index 160 for management and strategy roles against 100 for the general population. In the Paddock Club, two thirds are senior executives, and for a technology brand that means the CIOs, CTOs and transformation leaders who shortlist firms like yours.
>
> **The proof-hungry.** This sport is a technology showcase: every team is a data business operating under a cost cap. Buyers here are used to judging technology partners by what they visibly do, which suits a firm that wins on capability over size.
>
> **The world.** 827 million fans across five continents, with the US, where you're growing, as the sport's fastest-growing market: three races, two American teams, Apple broadcasting. One platform covers your home market's passion for the sport and your growth markets' buyers at the same time."

### 4.4 Why this could fit Sonata

> "Why we thought this was worth a call. Three reasons, and you tell me if they hold.
>
> **One, the pattern is proven for your industry.** IT and technology services brands have used this sport for years, Cognizant and Tata Communications among the long-standing examples, precisely because it solves the challenger's problem: when your name sits on a Formula 1 car, enterprise buyers stop asking if you're big enough. You skip years of brand-building with the exact audience that signs your contracts.
>
> **Two, the room is your pipeline.** The partner ecosystem, more than 350 companies, is heavy with the retailers, travel companies, manufacturers and financial services firms you sell to, and their technology leadership sits in the Paddock Club every race weekend. One good season of hosting the right CIOs can outperform years of conference sponsorships.
>
> **Three, a working story if you want one.** Every team is a modernization story: data platforms, cloud, AI under hard cost limits. Working-supplier shapes exist in this sport, where the brand visibly does a job for the team; CommScope's RUCKUS brand at Haas runs the team's operations with a showcase in the garage. For a services firm, nothing sells capability like a team that runs on it.
>
> One honest note on size. These seats are scarce: eleven teams, one seat per category, multi-year deals, and that keeps prices high. We'd point you at the entries where a firm your size gets real value against its brief, and if the honest answer is 'not yet', we'll say it."

### 4.5 Three early sketches (ideas, not proposals)

*[Offer as sketches; their reaction feeds the brief.]*

> "To make it concrete, three sketches, all sized sensibly.
>
> **One, the modernization story.** A content series with a team: how a race team modernizes its own technology under a hard cost cap, told through Sonata's lens. The cleanest capability pitch an IT services firm can make, and it fills your sales teams' hands with material.
>

### Q1. "What are comparable brands doing in Formula 1, and why are they using it?"

> "This is the strongest part of the story for you, because your industry wrote the playbook.
>
> IT and B2B technology names sit all over this sport: Cognizant and Tata Communications are long-standing examples of services firms here, with ServiceNow and NetApp in the wider set. And the software giants went even bigger: Atlassian's title is on Williams, Oracle's on Red Bull, HP's on Ferrari, Microsoft reportedly with Mercedes since January, AWS the sport's cloud provider since 2018.
>
> Why they do it: stature, because a name on this grid reads as world-class to every enterprise buyer; the room, because the paddock hosts the exact executives they sell to; and proof, through working roles. CommScope's RUCKUS brand powers the Haas team's operations with a live garage showcase: capability you can demo.
>
> And when the brief is right, it shows in revenue. Airwallex at McLaren grew revenue 50% year on year across EMEA and the Americas during the partnership, with UK revenue up 57% from new customers. That's the pattern working."

### Q2. "How do F1 teams drive introductions in business?"

> "Very concretely. It's a managed process, not a cocktail party.
>
> Every team runs a family of 25-plus partners, and the team's partnership department actively connects them: compliant introductions, partner summits, business forums on race weekends, private dinners. On top sit your own hospitality rights, where you choose the guest list: your target accounts, hosted at the race, with your leadership in the room.
>
> For Sonata, those partner rooms are full of enterprises in the middle of modernizing. That's a pipeline, not a billboard."

### Q3. "How is ROI measured in Formula 1?"

> "Four ways, agreed upfront.
>
> **Media value:** independent firms like Nielsen track every second your brand appears, across TV and digital, and price it against your fee.
>
> **Brand tracking:** awareness and trust, measured before and after, in your buying audience: enterprise technology leadership in your growth markets.
>
> **Business pipeline:** introductions, meetings, RFP wins and deals closed through the platform. That's how Airwallex could point at 50% revenue growth during their partnership. In your business, one large transformation contract can repay a season on its own.
>
> **Content performance:** reach and engagement on everything built.
>
> We'd weight the framework to your funnel answer from today, and for a listed company we'd make sure the reporting stands up internally too."

### Q4. "Which entry would fit us best?"

> "Today I don't know yet, and I wouldn't respect you by guessing.
>
> That's what the grid mapping is for: the realistic entries team by team and at series level, what the IT services category allows, because it's a busy one, and the honest price of each route. Give us the brief today, and the recommendation comes back with names, levels and reasons, sized for Sonata."

### Q5. "What does a partnership cost?"

> "The price follows the rights. Rule of thumb: entry-level team positions start in the low single-digit millions a year, official partner tiers sit above that, and driver, content and pure hospitality programs sit below, some well below.
>
> Why expensive? Eleven teams for the whole world, one seat per category, three-to-five-year deals. Scarcity.
>
> For Sonata, the realistic conversation runs from the hospitality-and-content end up to an entry or official seat, and the brief decides where on that ladder the value is. Our advice is free either way: the rights holders pay our fee."

### Q6. "Why work with SPORTFIVE? Could we not go to a team directly?"

> "You could. Two reasons brands use us anyway.
>
> One, information. A team sells you its own story; we put all eleven next to each other: inventory, pricing, appetite, and what each would mean for Sonata specifically, including where the IT services category is blocked and where it's open. For more than twelve years we worked exclusively with Mercedes and Ferrari; we gave that up deliberately to work brand-first across the whole grid. The neutral seat is the product.
>
> Two, process. We build the business case, benchmark the price and negotiate on your behalf, because we do it every week.
>
> And since the rights holders pay our fee, it costs you nothing to use us. There's no version where you pay more."

### Q7. "Are any of our competitors already in Formula 1?"

> "Some of the biggest names in your industry have been in this sport for years, which is exactly why I won't wing the detail from memory. The category is busy, it moves, and you deserve the precise picture.
>
> Mapping IT services and systems integration across all eleven teams and the series is part of the analysis after this call. You'd see who's in, who's out, what's blocked, and where a position is still open for a firm like Sonata."

### Q8. "We are a fraction of the size of the giants in our industry. Is F1 not their game?"

> "It's the right question, and the answer is the opposite of what you'd expect.
>
> The giants don't need this sport to look world-class; they already do. The brands that get the most out of this platform are the challengers, because it collapses the stature gap overnight. A buyer looking at a shortlist doesn't see headcount; they see a name they recognize from the biggest stage in motorsport, hosting them at a race, with a working story to tell. Airwallex wasn't a giant when they went to McLaren; the growth came after.
>
> And the entry prices make it possible: the right-sized seats cost a fraction of what the big tiers do. So the honest framing isn't 'is F1 too big for us'. It's 'which seat pays for itself against our brief'. That's a numbers question, and the mapping answers it. If the numbers say not yet, that's what we'll tell you."

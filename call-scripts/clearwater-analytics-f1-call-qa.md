# Clearwater Analytics x SPORTFIVE: F1 Call, Questions and Answers

**Call:** intro call with [Name, Job title] at Clearwater Analytics. From SPORTFIVE: Rahul, Bjorn and George.

---

## The intro

> "Hi [Name], thanks for making the time. I'm Rahul. I look after brand partnerships in SPORTFIVE's Formula 1 business, and Bjorn and George are with me.
>
> A bit about how we work, because it's different from what most people expect. We don't sell for one team. We look at all eleven, work out what you're trying to achieve, and then see whether any of it fits. The teams pay us, not you. And if it doesn't fit, we'll tell you."

### "So why did you reach out to us?"

> "A couple of things stood out. The first is who you sell to. Your platform keeps the investment records for a lot of insurers, asset managers and pension funds, more than ten trillion dollars of assets by your count. And the firms around those clients have moved into F1 this year. Nuveen and BNY both signed with Williams in January, Barclays did its first ever F1 deal there in February, and Standard Chartered signed with the sport itself. So a lot of the people you sell to now spend time in the paddock.
>
> The second is a trend on the money side of the teams. Teams have had a spending cap since 2021, so the finance department matters more than it used to. SAP helps Mercedes forecast its costs against the cap. Red Bull is moving its finance onto Oracle's business software. And last month McLaren named Airwallex its AI Finance Partner from 2027, and the person quoted on the announcement was McLaren's chief financial officer.
>
> As far as we can tell, nobody who does what you do has done it yet. You've also just gone private, and your CEO said that makes it easier to build the AI side of the business. So it felt worth a conversation, mainly to understand where you're heading."

## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who watches F1? Is it our audience?"

> "More of it than you'd think. F1 fans index at 160 for business decision makers, where 100 is the general population. That's the highest of any sport. In plain terms, the person who signs off big business decisions is much more likely to follow F1 than the average person.
>
> The US, your home market, matters too. There are three US races now, and purchase consideration for brands in F1 is up 32% among American fans. So for an American CFO, F1 isn't a foreign thing anymore."

### "Our buyers are CFOs and CIOs at insurers and asset managers, not fans. Who's in the paddock?"

> "Mostly business people. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. And there are more than 350 partner companies across the grid, and every one of them brings guests.
>
> A growing number of those companies are in finance. BNY, Barclays and Nuveen at Williams, UBS and Nasdaq at Mercedes, and Standard Chartered with the sport itself. When the banks explain why they're there, they all talk about the same thing: their clients. So the people standing next to your guests are often people you'd want to meet anyway."

### "What are companies like us doing in F1?"

> "The finance firms are mostly there for their clients, and the software firms are there to do a real job inside the team. A few examples."

- **BNY at Williams.** It signed in January as the team's institutional banking partner, with its name on the car's halo and chassis. Its plan for the season is hosting clients in financial centres around the world. What it tells you: the firms that look after institutional money use F1 to spend time with their clients.
- **Nuveen at Williams.** The asset manager signed in January, with its name above the drivers' steering wheels and on the rear wing. Its CEO said both are built on performance, with a long history and an eye on the future. What it tells you: asset managers, the people you sell to, now put their own names on cars.
- **UBS at Mercedes.** It's been with the team since 2011 and renewed in 2024. When it renewed, one of its wealth management bosses talked about experiences for clients. What it tells you: the banks that stay longest are the ones using it for clients.
- **Standard Chartered with F1.** It signed with the sport, not a team, as its wealth management and investment banking partner from this season. That includes F1 Academy, the all-women series, and trackside experiences for its clients. What it tells you: you don't have to pick a team to start.
- **SAP at Mercedes.** Its finance software helps Mercedes plan its spending, and its AI forecasts costs so the team can see where it will land against the cap. What it tells you: finance software can have a real job inside a team.
- **Oracle at Red Bull.** Title partner since 2021, extended in February. Red Bull is moving its finance, HR and marketing onto Oracle's business software, and the two are building an AI agent to help race engineers with strategy. What it tells you: even the biggest deals now reach into the back office.
- **Airwallex at McLaren.** It has supported McLaren's finances for three seasons, and from 2027 it becomes the team's AI Finance Partner. McLaren's CFO was the one quoted on the announcement. What it tells you: a real job leads to a bigger role, and AI for finance is something teams are happy to talk about.

### "What do brands like us do, beyond a logo?"

> "The good ones do a mix. A few concrete examples.
>
> Client weekends are the biggest part for financial firms. BNY is hosting clients in financial centres through the season, and Standard Chartered gives its clients trackside experiences. A Paddock Club pass works all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests over one weekend.
>
> Some do something useful with the team. Mercedes gets the use of Nasdaq's MarketSite, where companies ring the opening bell, and the two make content and merchandise together. Airwallex is providing the payments for McLaren's online merchandise shop for partners.
>
> Some pick races that match their markets. There are more than twenty race weekends on five continents, including three in the US and Silverstone in the UK. You could put your UK and European clients at one race and your American clients at another.
>
> And you have an obvious extra. Connect brings your clients to Boise every September. A team engineer on stage there, talking about how a team makes decisions from live data during a race, would probably be the session people remember."

### "Content is really important for us. How would a team help us make it?"

> "Teams are good at it now, and for a business like yours it's often where most of the value is.
>
> Most deals come with a set number of days with the drivers and the team's people each year, and the team's content people help make it. The best partner content usually isn't about the product. CrowdStrike's series with Mercedes had the team's specialists explaining the halo, the helmet and the fire extinguisher, and Mercedes says it got 6.2 million views. 1Password runs a podcast with Red Bull, hosted by a former Red Bull mechanic.
>
> One thing to know: the race footage is controlled by Formula 1 itself, not the teams. So partner content is filmed at the factory, in the garage, or on filming days.
>
> For you, I'd think about your research and Connect. A team's strategy people talking about making calls from live data, while staying under a spending cap, is a story your clients would recognise. That could be a chapter in one of your reports, a podcast series, or a session at Connect. And it gives your sales team something better to send a CFO than another product email.
>
> If content matters to you, we'd spell it out in the deal: how many days, who from the team, what goes on the team's channels, and how long you can use it."

### "Why are these brands in F1?"

> "Two different reasons, depending on the company.
>
> The banks and asset managers are there for their clients. BNY, UBS and Standard Chartered all talk about it the same way. A race weekend is three days with the people who matter most to your business, somewhere they really want to be.
>
> The software companies are there to prove something. A team makes its calls on a race weekend from live data, and it has to stay under a spending cap all year. If your software works there, people believe it will work for them. That's how SAP, Oracle and Airwallex use it.
>
> For Clearwater, I'd guess it's a bit of both. You talk about one record, the same facts across every asset, every day. An F1 team runs on the same idea."

### "Who are our competitors in F1, and which teams are available?"

> "I don't want to promise you anything on that yet, because right now it would be a guess. There are a few teams that already have a partner close to what you do. SAP at Mercedes and Oracle at Red Bull both provide finance software to their teams, and Airwallex becomes McLaren's AI Finance Partner from 2027. Some teams also have banks with broad financial deals, like BNY and Barclays at Williams, and UBS at Mercedes. And those deals usually come with exclusivity.
>
> So the first thing we do is check where there's a conflict, and rule those teams out.
>
> The second thing is understanding what you want out of it. If it's mainly about meeting insurers in the UK and Europe, that can point you to different teams than if it's about American asset managers, or about showing your AI working. Then we come back with the teams where it fits.
>
> One thing worth knowing: keeping the investment records for an insurer isn't the same as running a team's own accounts, and these contracts are often written quite narrowly. So a team with a finance software partner isn't automatically off the table.
>
> To give you an idea of what a partner like you does once they're in, look at SAP at Mercedes. Mercedes uses SAP's finance software, and the job is very specific. The team can only spend so much each season, so SAP's AI forecasts costs and predicts where the budget will end up. SAP also helps get car parts to race weekends on time. Toto Wolff said he couldn't think of a better partner to help the team be more efficient. So SAP gets to say that a team working under a hard spending limit trusts it with its numbers. That's a much stronger story than a sticker on the car."

### "It's very crowded. Won't we get lost?"

> "It's a real risk. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and a lot of them are just logos. The ones that get noticed do one of two things.
>
> Some give their product a real job. Airwallex at McLaren is a good example. They're next to Mastercard and Google, so they'd never win on logo size. Instead, they took on a job with McLaren's finance team. Three seasons later McLaren has made them its AI Finance Partner, and its CFO is the one talking about them.
>
> Others make something people want to read. For you, the obvious version is your research. Your AI survey found that 99% of institutional investors say unreliable data is the biggest thing stopping them trusting AI. A team has the same problem every race: a strategy call is only as good as the data behind it. Research built around that would get read by the people you sell to."

### "Why would a team want a partner like us?"

> "Teams aren't short of technology partners. Tech companies now spend more on F1 teams than any other kind of business, and a Reuters report in May counted eight new AI deals in six months.
>
> What's harder to find is a partner that brings something extra. Your clients are insurers, asset managers and pension funds. That's a lot of senior finance people a team would be glad to have at a race.
>
> Teams also care about their own numbers more than ever, because of the spending cap. A company whose whole business is getting financial records right has a story a team can relate to.
>
> Whether there's room for that at a given team is what we'd check first."

### "What would a first deal look like for a company our size?"

> "You're a big company, so you have more options than most. A typical first deal for a business that sells to other businesses is one team, one clear role, some branding, several race weekends with clients, and some content, over two or three years. BNY and Nuveen at Williams both look like that: a clear title, their names on the car, and clients at races.
>
> Others start smaller, with hospitality only. A few race weekends hosting clients, say Silverstone for your UK and European clients and one of the US races for American ones, to see whether it works before going further. Some sign a driver or a content deal instead of a team, which also costs less. And Standard Chartered shows you can go with the sport itself rather than one team."

### "What does it cost?"

> "It depends a lot on the team and what's included. A supplier or entry-level partner deal starts in the low single-digit millions a year. Official partner deals sit above that. Hosting clients at a few races, a driver deal or a content deal costs a lot less. I'd rather understand what you want first, then show you options at different levels."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

> "You can go to a team directly. They'll tell you about their own team, which is fair enough. We worked only with Mercedes and Ferrari for over twelve years, and then stopped, so we could look at all eleven teams for the brand. The teams pay our fee, so having us compare them costs you nothing. And with SAP, Oracle and several banks already on the grid, someone has to check each team for conflicts before you spend time on any of them."

### "Which team would fit us?"

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. It depends on what you want out of it, where your clients are, and where there's no conflict. That's what we'd come back to you with."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "We'd agree that before anything's signed. For a company that sells to insurers and asset managers, the real measure is business: which clients and prospects you spent time with, and what came of it. New clients, renewals, bigger contracts. TV exposure matters a lot less for you than it would for a consumer brand.
>
> It can show up in the numbers. Airwallex says its revenue grew 50% year on year in its Europe and Americas regions during its McLaren partnership, and 57% in the UK from new customers."

### "We've just gone private. Our owners will look hard at every dollar. Is this the right time?"

> "It might not be. Owners like yours want to see what they get back, so anything here has to be tied to business, not just visibility. Your CEO said going private makes it easier to build the AI side of Clearwater. If F1 helps you tell that story to insurers and asset managers, that's easier to justify than a logo.
>
> So we'd start with what you're trying to do in the next two or three years and work back from that. If the answer is a few race weekends with clients to begin with, that's fine. If it's 'not yet', we'll say so."

### "Our clients are conservative. Won't F1 look flashy to an insurer?"

> "Some of the most careful names in finance are on the grid. UBS has been with Mercedes since 2011. BNY, Barclays and Nuveen all signed with Williams this year. If they're comfortable putting their names on a car, an invitation from you won't look strange.
>
> And it doesn't have to be loud. A lot of what financial firms do there is quiet: a small group of clients, a walk through the garage, a dinner. Nobody outside sees it. What your clients remember is that you got them there."

# OpenText x SPORTFIVE: F1 Discovery Call Script

**Call type:** First call / discovery. Goal: introduce yourself and SPORTFIVE, understand where OpenText stands after Formula E, fill out the brief, explain what Formula 1 adds, agree next steps.

**How to use this script:** Every spoken block is written the way you would say it out loud. Do not read it word for word. Glance at the bold anchors, then talk. Short sentences, slow pace, warm tone. Keep it simple and concrete for a non-native English listener. Let them talk more than you.

## Part 4: How Formula 1 partnerships work (8 to 10 minutes)

*[They know motorsport, so compress the basics and go deeper on what is different about F1: the audience, the B2B room, the money. Invite questions as you go: "Stop me anytime."]*

### 4.1 The sport in thirty seconds

> "Let me give you a quick picture of how Formula 1 is set up, because it is structured differently from Formula E.
>
> Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company. They own the commercial side: the calendar, the broadcast deals, the races.
>
> The FIA is the regulator, same as in Formula E.
>
> And then there are eleven teams. From this coming season it is eleven, including two American teams, Haas and Cadillac. Each team is an independent business with its own owners and its own partnerships. Some belong to car makers, like Ferrari, Mercedes and Audi. Some to consumer giants, like Red Bull. Some to investors, like Williams, owned by a US investment firm.
>
> So a brand can partner at three levels: with the sport itself, with a team, or with a driver personally. Most brands enter through a team, and I can explain why when we get there."

### 4.2 What a team partnership includes

> "The rights package will feel familiar from Formula E, but everything is bigger. Think of it in five parts.
>
> **One, branding.** Your logo on the car, on the drivers' race suits, on the garage. Around 24 race weekends a year, in front of a global TV audience. Last season the total audience was 1.8 billion, and the average race reached 76 million people.
>
> **Two, the official title.** You become, for example, the Official Information Management Partner of a team. You can use the team's name, logo and imagery in your own marketing. Your website, your sales decks, your keynotes at OpenText World.
>
> **Three, hospitality.** This is where Formula 1 stands alone in sport. Paddock access, the Paddock Club, garage tours, time with the drivers. To give you a feel: 66% of Paddock Club guests are senior executives, and the top performing segments are CIOs, CTOs and CISOs. Your exact buyers.
>
> And one detail people miss. A Grand Prix runs over three days, and your passes work every day. So seven passes does not mean seven guests. You bring different people on Friday, on Saturday, and on Sunday. Seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend, and hundreds across a season. Very few platforms multiply your hosting like that.
>
> **Four, content and digital.** Access to the team's channels, the drivers, behind-the-scenes content made with you. Formula 1 is the number one sport in the world for social engagement, 2.2 billion engagements, ahead of the NBA.
>
> **Five, people.** Driver appearances, team bosses at your events, hot laps for your top clients, filming days for your campaigns. Imagine an F1 driver opening OpenText World.
>
> And around all of this sits the business platform, which for OpenText might be the most valuable part. Every team carries at least 25 partners. Across the grid, that is more than 350 companies. These companies want to do business with each other, and the teams make the introductions. I can go deeper on that whenever you like."

### 4.3 The audience

> "A quick picture of who watches, because this is where F1 really separates itself for a B2B company.
>
> Formula 1 fans have the highest concentration of business decision makers in any major sport. For management and strategy roles, they index at 160, where the average person is 100. This is an audience of people who run companies.
>
> The scale is 827 million fans, growing 63% since 2018, and the profile is young and digital now: 43% under 35, 42% women.
>
> The commercial side reflects it. Technology, media and telecom is the biggest sponsor sector in Formula 1, around 700 million dollars this year. Your industry did the maths already.
>
> And one detail close to home: the Canadian Grand Prix in Montreal. A home race for OpenText, with everything that gives you for Canadian clients, employees and media."

### 4.4 Why this could fit OpenText

> "Now let me tell you why I find this opportunity interesting for OpenText. Three reasons.
>
> **First, the paddock is your customer list.** Microsoft partners with Mercedes. Oracle has its name on Red Bull. IBM works with Ferrari, Cognizant and ServiceNow with Aston Martin, Dell with McLaren, Salesforce and AWS with Formula 1 itself. Enterprise technology is the biggest spender in this sport. And beyond tech, the grid carries banks, manufacturers and telecoms, the industries you serve. With 120,000 enterprise customers, chances are many companies in that paddock already run on OpenText. We can map that precisely.
>
> **Second, the step-up.** Formula E proved your model: real technology, embedded in a real team. Formula 1 runs the same play at a completely different scale of audience, media and hospitality. And from this season the technology stories converge: the new F1 cars carry a much bigger share of electric power and run on 100% sustainable fuel. The story that took you into Formula E now exists in F1, in front of the whole world.
>
> **Third, the access.** What really drives the value of these partnerships is not the logo. It is the paddock, the hospitality, the room with 350 companies in it. Nobody does business hospitality like Formula 1. That is what partners are buying, and that is what opens doors.
>
> One last thing on value. All of this sits on very small supply. Formula 1 has eleven teams, for the entire world, and inside each team your category has exactly one seat. Small supply, growing demand. That is why these partnerships are not cheap, and why they hold their value."

>

### Q2. "What is the difference between partnering with F1 itself, a team, or a driver?"

> "Three doors into the sport.
>
> **The series, Formula 1 itself.** You partner with the sport, not with one team. Trackside branding at every race, race title rights, global reach, and you stay neutral, you never lose on a Sunday. But these are fewer, bigger deals at premium prices, and you are not part of a story. You are everywhere, but you belong to no one.
>
> **A team.** This is where the emotion lives. The car, the drivers, the wins, the setbacks. You get season-long visibility, content, hospitality, and the team's whole business ecosystem. There are many more entry points and price levels than at series level. It is also the model you already know from Jaguar TCS Racing. For most brands, this is the right door.
>
> **A driver personally.** The smallest and simplest option. Space on a cap or helmet, the driver's personal social channels, some appearance days. Cheaper, yes. But you get no team rights, no car, no paddock hospitality, and the driver's team has to approve everything.
>
> The clear trend in the sport is that fans follow teams and drivers even more than the sport itself. Story beats logo. That is why our conversations usually start at team level, and the brief will tell us which team."

### Q3. "You have so many partners per team. How does one brand get the visibility and attention it needs?"

> "Fair question. Thirty logos on a car sounds crowded. Three answers.
>
> First, a partnership is a structure, not a crowd. Every team organises its partners in clear tiers: title partner, principal partners, official partners. Your tier defines exactly what you own: the size and position of your branding on the car, your driver days, your content days, your hospitality allocation. It is all in the contract. Nothing is first come, first served.
>
> Second, exclusivity. Within your category, you are alone. The team cannot sign your competitor. So inside your world, you are not one of thirty. You are the only one.
>
> Third, attention is won through activation, not through logo size. The partners people remember are the ones with the best stories: real technology doing real work inside the team. That is exactly the model you built with Jaguar in Formula E, and it is where OpenText can stand out against much bigger logos.
>
> And keep one thing in mind. Visibility is only half the value. The access, the hospitality, the introductions, those are private rights. They do not get diluted because other partners exist. If anything, more partners in the room means more companies for you to meet."

>

They make compliant introductions between partners, they host partner summits, business forums on race weekends, private dinners and roundtables. And it goes beyond the calendar, at events like CES, Davos and Web Summit.

### Q6. "How is ROI measured in Formula 1?"

> "Four ways, usually, and good teams report on all of them. It will feel familiar from Formula E, with bigger numbers.
>
> **Media value.** Independent measurement firms, Nielsen for example, track every second your branding is on screen, across TV and digital, and give it a money value. You compare that number to your fee.
>
> **Brand tracking.** Awareness and consideration among fans, measured before and after. And F1 fans reward sponsors: in the US they are 32% more likely to consider a sponsor's product.
>
> **Business pipeline.** For B2B, you count introductions made, meetings held, and deals closed through the platform. That is how Airwallex could point to 50% revenue growth during their partnership.
>
> **Hospitality outcomes.** Which clients and prospects you hosted, and what those relationships produced afterwards.
>
> You agree the framework upfront, and you review it with the team every quarter. The good teams manage a partnership like a business, not like a sticker on a car."

### Q7. "Who owns Formula 1? How does the money work?"

> "Three layers, very simple.
>
> Liberty Media, an American media company, owns Formula 1 itself. They bought it in 2017. They control the commercial rights: races, broadcast deals, the calendar.
>
> The FIA is the independent regulator. Rules and safety.
>
> The eleven teams are independent businesses. Ferrari, Mercedes and Audi belong to car makers. Red Bull to the drinks company. Williams to a US investment firm. Aston Martin to a consortium led by Lawrence Stroll. Each team earns money in two ways: a share of the sport's revenues, and its own sponsorships.
>
> One thing worth knowing: since 2021 the sport has a cost cap. Teams cannot outspend each other without limit anymore. The result is that teams became profitable, stable businesses, and their values have grown sharply. Team sponsorship has doubled since 2020, from about one billion to 2.2 billion dollars a year, across 335 deals. So you would be entering a healthy, growing market. You can see it in the calibre of brands arriving: Microsoft joined Mercedes just this year."

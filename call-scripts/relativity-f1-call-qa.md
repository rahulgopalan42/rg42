# Relativity x SPORTFIVE: F1 Call, Questions and Answers

**Call:** intro call with [Name, Job title] at Relativity. From SPORTFIVE: Rahul, Bjorn and George.

---

## The intro

> "Hi [Name], thanks for making the time. I'm Rahul. I look after brand partnerships in SPORTFIVE's Formula 1 business, and Bjorn and George are with me.
>
> A bit about how we work, because it's different from what most people expect. We don't sell for one team. We look at all eleven, work out what you're trying to achieve, and then see whether any of it fits. The teams pay us, not you. And if it doesn't fit, we'll tell you."

### "So why did you reach out to us?"

> "Two simple reasons. The first is who you serve. General counsel at big companies, and partners at the big law firms. Those companies are already in F1, and so are their lawyers. One of your own customers, Microsoft, signed a big deal with Mercedes in January. And one of the firms trying out claiR has been a team's official legal partner since 2019.
>
> The second is what you do. You help lawyers find what matters in a mountain of data, fast, and show the evidence for it. An F1 team does the same after every session. The car sends back a flood of data, and the engineers have to find the few things that matter before the next one. And every team now answers to the cost cap rules, so every team has to show what it spent.
>
> And you've just put AI at the front of the brand, with the new name. So it felt like a good time to talk. But I'd like to hear from you first. Where are things heading?"

## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who watches F1? Is it our audience?"

> "More of it than you'd think. F1 fans index at 160 for business decision makers, where 100 is the general population. That's the highest of any sport. In plain terms, the general counsel who signs off a big contract with you is much more likely than average to follow F1.
>
> Your home market matters too. There are three US races now, and Apple broadcasts the sport in the US. Purchase consideration for brands in F1 is up 32% among American fans.
>
> And the sport goes where your users are. There are more than twenty race weekends on five continents, including Montreal, which moved to May this year, Silverstone, Melbourne and São Paulo."

### "Our buyers are general counsel and law firm partners, not fans. Who's in the paddock?"

> "Mostly business people. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. There are more than 350 partner companies across the grid, and every one of them brings guests.
>
> What I'd point out for you is how many of them have big legal teams. Microsoft is at Mercedes. Barclays signed with Williams this year. Shell and Aon are at Ferrari. Cognizant is at Aston Martin. Their general counsel are your buyers.
>
> Then there are the advisers. Deloitte is at McLaren, PwC is with the sport itself, and Ashurst, a law firm, is McLaren's official legal partner. Teams also introduce their partners to each other. So the people your guests meet are often people they'd want to meet anyway."

### "How are similar brands activating, and what are they getting back?"

> "As far as we can tell, no legal technology company has a deal with an F1 team yet. So here are the closest ones: a law firm, two of the Big Four, and the tech companies doing real jobs inside teams."

**Ashurst at McLaren**

> "Start with the closest to you. Ashurst has worked with McLaren's road car business since 2013. In 2019 it became the official legal services partner for the whole McLaren Group, racing included. When it signed, McLaren's group legal director talked about having 'a partner who understands our different businesses.'
>
> Through the partnership, Ashurst works alongside McLaren's own lawyers. It has advised on some of the group's biggest deals.
>
> No return is published, and law firms rarely publish one. The signal is that it's lasted. Ashurst extended the deal around its tenth year with McLaren, and it's still on McLaren's partner pages this season. And you know the firm. After this year's merger it's Ashurst Perkins Coie, and in September it joined your claiR early access programme."

**Deloitte at McLaren**

> "Deloitte started working with McLaren in 2017, when McLaren was a client, on things like race simulators and HR systems. Over time that turned into a sponsorship. In January it became Official Transformation Partner across McLaren's whole racing business: F1, IndyCar and, from next year, endurance racing. Its US marketing chief, Scott Mager, gave the reason simply: 'Why not double down on it?'
>
> Through the partnership, Deloitte hosts senior clients at races for a few days behind the scenes, with a look at the work it does for the team. It put the McLaren car on its stand at CES in January, and it planned ads near the US races.
>
> It hasn't published figures. But Mager told Marketing Brew the value often outstrips the cost, 'pretty significantly'. And he put the logic in one line: 'Nobody buys multiyear transformation services out of an e-commerce cart.' Your contracts are the same. People buy them after they trust you."

**PwC with Formula 1**

> "PwC has been Formula 1's official consulting partner since the Miami race last year. It's with the sport, not a team. Its US senior partner, Paul Griggs, said F1 is 'the kind of high-performance environment where strategy, speed and innovation converge.'
>
> Through the deal, PwC advises F1 on parts of its business, like operations and logistics. In return it gets signs at some races and the chance to host clients. And it launched its first new brand look in more than a decade at the same time.
>
> No return is published, and PwC wouldn't share the cost. What it shows is a professional services firm using F1 to launch a new brand. You've just refreshed yours and renamed the platform, so that one's worth noting."

**Cognizant at Aston Martin**

> "Cognizant has been with Aston Martin since 2021, when the team came back to F1. It started as title partner, and at the time it said the aim was global brand awareness. After three seasons, the team said those goals had been met, and the name came off the title. But Cognizant stayed, in a technology services role. And in May it became the team's Global AI Services Partner. Its CEO, Ravi Kumar, said 'Formula 1 represents the ultimate test bed for applied AI.'
>
> Through the deal, Cognizant works on the team's IT, software, data and AI across the factory at Silverstone.
>
> No business return is published. The signal is six years and a role that keeps getting deeper. It moved from a name on the car to a job inside the team."

**Microsoft at Mercedes**

> "Microsoft signed a multi-year deal with Mercedes in January. Sky News reported it at around sixty million dollars a season, though neither side confirmed that. The reason, from Microsoft's commercial chief, Judson Althoff: F1 is where 'milliseconds matter and data determines outcomes.'
>
> Through the deal, Mercedes runs more of its simulation, race strategy and analysis on Microsoft's cloud and AI. The two companies say each car carries more than 400 sensors, sending over a million data points a second. The cloud lets the team add computing power when it needs it, while staying inside strict financial rules.
>
> It's too new for results. Two things matter for you. It shows how much money is now behind the AI deals in F1. And Microsoft is your customer. Its litigation team uses aiR, and your own case study says aiR's calls matched its human reviewers 92% of the time."

**Oracle at Red Bull**

> "Oracle has been Red Bull's title partner since February 2022, and it extended and expanded the deal this February. The team boss, Laurent Mekies, said Red Bull relies on Oracle 'to help us understand and optimise countless variables.'
>
> Through the partnership, Red Bull runs billions of race strategy simulations on Oracle's cloud around each race weekend. It's moving finance, HR and marketing onto Oracle's business software. And this season it added an AI strategy agent that works with the race engineers on the big calls.
>
> Oracle doesn't publish a business return. The signal is the renewal, and what the team points to since Oracle arrived: three drivers' titles and two constructors' titles."

**What that adds up to**

> "So the honest picture is this. As far as we can tell, no legal technology company has a deal with an F1 team. The closest is Ashurst at McLaren, a law firm, and it's been there for years. The professional services firms and tech companies rarely publish numbers. What they show is that they stay, and their role gets deeper: Deloitte doubling down, Cognizant moving from the title into AI work, Oracle renewing. Deloitte is the one that's said out loud that it pays.
>
> For you, I'd take three things from that. First, give it a real job people can see, the way Oracle and Microsoft do, rather than just a logo. For you, that would be the legal and compliance side of a team, if a team wants help there. Second, host the people who sign your contracts: general counsel, law firm partners and the Big Four, the way Deloitte and PwC do. Third, tie it to the new brand. PwC used F1 to launch its new look, and you've just launched Relativity aiR."

### "Content is really important for us. How would a team help us make it?"

> "It's one of the most useful parts of a deal. And for a company whose clients can't be named, it may matter more than the logo.
>
> Most deals come with a set number of filming days each year with the drivers and the team's people, and the team's content people help you make it. The team's own channels matter too. F1 is the number one sport on social media, ahead of the NBA.
>
> One thing to know: the race footage is controlled by Formula 1 itself, not the teams. So partner content is filmed around the race: at the factory, in the garage, or on filming days.
>
> The best partner content usually isn't about the product. CrowdStrike made a series with Mercedes where the team's specialists explain the safety kit on the car, from the halo to the helmet, and Mercedes says it got 6.2 million views.
>
> For you, I'd start with the hours after a practice session. The car has just sent back a flood of data, and the engineers have until the next session to find what matters. Film one of them walking through it: what they look at first, what they ignore, and how they check they're right before they act on it. Then put one of your experts next to them, with a case of a million documents and a court deadline. It's the same problem. You can't read everything, so how do you find the one thing that matters, and how do you prove it? No client data, and no team secrets. Just two people who find needles in haystacks for a living.
>
> A second idea, for general counsel: the cost cap. Every year each team hands the FIA a full set of financial records, and the FIA goes through them. Last year's review took seven months. Someone from a team explaining how they get ready for that, if a team is willing, is a story every general counsel would recognise.
>
> If content matters to you, we'd spell it out in the deal: how many days, which drivers and team people, what the team posts on its own channels, and how long and where you can use it."

### "Why are these brands in F1?"

> "Two reasons, mostly.
>
> The professional services firms are there for their clients. Deloitte, PwC and Ashurst all use races to spend time with the people who hire them. A race weekend is three days with the people who matter most to your business, somewhere they really want to be.
>
> The tech companies are there to prove something. A team makes decisions from huge amounts of data, under a spending cap, and every big call gets picked apart afterwards. If your software works there, people believe it will work for them. That's why Oracle and Microsoft are there.
>
> For Relativity, I'd guess it's both. Your buyers are the people these firms host. And your promise, finding the facts in the data and showing where each one came from, is what a team does after every session."

### "Who are our competitors in F1, and which teams are available?"

> "I don't want to promise you anything on that yet, because right now it would be a guess. As far as we can tell, no legal technology company has a deal with an F1 team. Harvey, Legora and Clio have gone into other sports, but not F1.
>
> But nearly every team now has an AI partner, and some are close to what you do. Williams has Anthropic's Claude as its 'Official Thinking Partner'. Aston Martin has Cognition, Cohere and Cognizant. Mercedes has Microsoft and Meta AI. McLaren has Google's Gemini, and Ashurst for legal services. Red Bull has Oracle, and Ferrari has IBM. Cadillac's AI partner, TWG AI, is exclusive. You already work with two of those: you connect to Claude, and your AI runs on Microsoft's cloud. Deals like these usually come with exclusivity.
>
> So the first thing we do is check where there's a conflict, and rule those teams out.
>
> The second thing is understanding what you want out of it. If it's mainly about American general counsel, that can point you to different teams than if it's law firms in London, or the Big Four. Then we come back with the teams where it fits.
>
> One thing worth knowing: legal AI isn't the same job as a general AI deal, and these contracts are often written quite narrowly. That's why my note said 'Official Legal AI Partner'. But we'd check each team before saying any more.
>
> To give you an idea of what a partner like you does once they're in, look at Oracle at Red Bull. The team runs billions of strategy simulations on Oracle's cloud around each race weekend. It's moving its finance, HR and marketing onto Oracle's software. And this season it built an AI agent with Oracle that works with the race engineers on strategy calls. So Oracle gets to say a championship-winning team runs its strategy and its books on Oracle. That's a much stronger story than a sticker on the car."

### "It's very crowded. Won't we get lost?"

> "It's a real risk. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and Reuters counted eight new AI deals in six months. If you're one more AI logo, you'll get lost.
>
> The ones that get noticed do one of two things. Some take on a real job, like Oracle at Red Bull, or Cognizant at Aston Martin. Others make something people want to watch.
>
> You have something different to say. The AI partners on the grid mostly talk about engineering, strategy or fans. As far as we can tell, none of them talks about evidence: finding the facts, and showing where they came from. That's your story, and you've been telling it for 25 years. It just needs a team that wants to tell it with you, and that's what we'd look for."

### "Why would a team want a partner like us?"

> "Teams aren't short of technology partners. Tech companies now spend more on F1 teams than any other kind of business, and a Reuters report in May counted eight new AI deals in six months.
>
> What's rarer is a partner that knows the legal side. Every team signs contracts with drivers, sponsors and suppliers. Every team has to show the FIA what it spent each year. And Reuters reported in May that teams already use AI to help read the sporting and technical rules. A company whose whole job is finding the facts in documents, and showing where they came from, is easy for a team to understand.
>
> You'd also bring people a team would like to meet. More than 300,000 legal professionals use your platform, in more than 30 countries. Your customers include big law firms, the Big Four and large companies, and some of them are already on the grid.
>
> Whether there's room for that at a given team is what we'd check first."

### "What would a first deal look like for a company our size?"

> "You're one of the biggest companies in legal technology, with Silver Lake behind you, so money isn't the main limit. The conflict check is, and the timing of your IPO. A typical first deal for a company that sells to other businesses is one team, one clear role, some branding, a few race weekends with customers, and some content, over two or three years.
>
> Others start with hosting, a few race weekends to see whether it works. Passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend. For you, Miami, Austin or Las Vegas for American general counsel, Montreal in May for Canada, Silverstone in July for the London firms, and Melbourne in March for Australia.
>
> And PwC shows you can go with the sport itself rather than one team, though that sits at a much higher level."

### "What does it cost?"

> "It depends a lot on the team and what's included. A supplier or entry-level partner deal starts in the low single-digit millions a year. Official partner deals sit above that. Hosting customers at a few races, a driver deal or a content deal costs a lot less. I'd rather understand what you want first, then show you options at different levels."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

> "You can go to a team directly. They'll tell you about their own team, which is fair enough. We worked only with Mercedes and Ferrari for over twelve years, and then stopped, so we could look at all eleven teams for the brand. The teams pay our fee, so having us compare them costs you nothing. And with an AI partner at nearly every team, someone has to check each one for conflicts before you spend time on any of them."

### "Which team would fit us?"

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. It depends on what you want out of it, whether your buyers are mainly in the US, the UK or Australia, and where there's no conflict. Microsoft being your customer is a door at Mercedes, for example. But Mercedes already has Microsoft and Meta AI, so we'd check that first. That's what we'd come back to you with."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "We'd agree that before anything's signed. For you I'd look at three things. Business: which general counsel, law firm partners and Big Four leaders you spent time with, and the contracts that came from it. Brand: whether people outside e-discovery start to know the name Relativity aiR. And content: how far the films travelled, which we can measure.
>
> It can show up in the numbers. Airwallex says its revenue grew 50% year on year in its Europe and Americas regions during its McLaren partnership, and 57% in the UK from new customers."

### "Our buyers are lawyers. Aren't they too conservative for F1?"

> "Less than you'd think. A law firm is already on the grid: Ashurst has been McLaren's official legal services partner since 2019. Deloitte and PwC are there. So are careful banks like Barclays, which signed with Williams this year.
>
> And legal technology has moved into sport fast. The ABA Journal wrote about it in October. Harvey is with the Golden State Warriors, the Chicago Cubs, Fulham and the US Open. Legora is with the Yankees. Clio is on the Vancouver Canucks' away jerseys. None of them is in F1.
>
> And it doesn't have to be loud. Most of what firms do at a race is quiet: a small group, a walk through the garage, a dinner. Nobody outside sees it. One thing to plan for: lawyers and government clients often have strict rules on accepting hospitality. We'd build around that from the start."

### "Our clients' matters are confidential. We can't do case studies. What's the story?"

> "You don't need your clients' stories. The story is the job itself.
>
> F1 teams are secretive too. Nobody shows you their data. What they do show is how they work: how they find the one thing that matters, fast, and check it before they act. That's your story as well, without a single client document.
>
> And there's a public story in F1 that sounds a lot like your world: the cost cap. Every year, each team hands the FIA a full set of financial records, and the FIA goes through them. Last year's review took seven months. In 2022, the FIA found Red Bull had overspent in 2021. It was a small overspend, but the team was fined seven million dollars and lost some wind tunnel time. So getting the records right matters.
>
> And where clients do agree, you already publish. Microsoft let you say its litigation team expects to save $1.6 million a year with aiR."

### "AI partners are everywhere in F1. How do we stand out?"

> "By doing a different job. Look at what the AI partners do now. Claude helps Williams with strategy and car development. Cohere and Cognition work on Aston Martin's engineering and software. Google puts Gemini at the centre of McLaren's work. They're about the car and the race.
>
> As far as we can tell, nobody is talking about the other side of a team: the contracts, the rules and the cost cap. That work is real. Every team has to show what it spent, and Reuters reported in May that teams already use AI to help read the sporting and technical rules. That's your ground: finding the facts in a pile of documents, and showing where each one came from.
>
> So lead with a real job, not a logo. 'Legal AI' is narrower than what the other AI partners hold, which helps when we check for conflicts. Whether a team wants help there, and whether its current AI deal already covers it, is what we'd check team by team.
>
> And keep the people in it. Your pitch is that the lawyer stays in charge, and the AI shows its working. Williams said much the same about Claude: it's there to support the team's experts, not replace them."

### "We filed for an IPO in March. Is this the right time?"

> "It might not be, and I'd rather say so. Once you're public, investors look hard at every big spend. So anything here has to be tied to business, not just visibility.
>
> There's also timing. Your lawyers will have views on what you announce, and when, while the IPO is pending. We'd work around that.
>
> What helps is that you can start small and quiet: a few race weekends with customers, and no announcement. Then, when you're ready to tell a bigger story about Relativity aiR, a team deal can help you tell it to a much wider audience. But we'd start from your plan for the next two or three years and work back. If the answer is 'not yet', we'll say so."

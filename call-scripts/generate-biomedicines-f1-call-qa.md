## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who watches F1? Is it our audience?"

> "Partly. The part that matters to you is narrow, but it's there.
>
> F1 fans index at 160 for business decision makers, where 100 is the general population. That's the highest of any sport. In plain terms, the head of business development at a big drug company is more likely than average to follow it.
>
> The other number I'd look at is age. 43% of fans are under 35. You compete with tech companies for machine learning people, and that's who they are.
>
> And the sport goes where your partners are. There are more than twenty race weekends on five continents. Three are in the US: Miami, Austin and Las Vegas. Montreal is a short flight from Boston. And Monza is a few hours from Novartis in Basel."

### "Our partners are drug companies and investors, not fans. Who's in the paddock?"

> "Mostly business people. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. There are more than 350 partner companies across the grid, and every one of them brings guests.
>
> What I'd point out for you is who's arrived lately. AbbVie joined McLaren in October as its official pharmaceutical partner. Viatris was announced this month as Cadillac's, from next season. Sanofi has worked with McLaren since 2022. On the money side, Nasdaq, where you listed, and UBS are both at Mercedes. Barclays, BNY and Nuveen are all at Williams. And Anthropic, which your scientists worked with this autumn, is on the Williams car. Teams also introduce their partners to each other.
>
> One thing to plan for. The US drug industry's code says companies shouldn't give doctors tickets to sporting events, and Europe's codes are similar. So the guest list is partners, investors and people you want to hire. Not doctors, and not the people running your trials."

### "How are similar brands activating, and what are they getting back?"

> "As far as we can tell, no biotech has a deal with an F1 team. So here are the closest ones: two drug companies that have just arrived, one that went in to learn, your AI research partner, a team that runs billions of simulations, and the exchange you listed on."

**AbbVie at McLaren**

> "Start with the one in your world. AbbVie has been with McLaren since October, as its official pharmaceutical partner. It started at the race in Austin. Tracie Haas, who runs corporate affairs at AbbVie, said the two 'may operate in different industries, but both are driven by a mindset of continuous improvement.'
>
> Through the deal, AbbVie runs a campaign called Race to Innovate. It tells stories about the people and the thinking behind progress, in medicine and in racing. And for every ten points McLaren scores in the constructors' championship, AbbVie gives $10,000 to patient groups for people with immune diseases. Notice what's missing: no medicine is named anywhere.
>
> It's too early for results, and none are published. The signal is the pattern. AbbVie's baseball work started with one team, the Chicago Cubs, last year. This year it covers the whole league. It also signed the PGA TOUR in September, each time with a donation tied to the scoring. A company that keeps adding sports that way is telling you it likes what it gets back."

**Viatris at Cadillac**

> "Viatris was announced this month as Cadillac's official global pharmaceutical partner, starting next season. It makes generic and branded medicines, and has delivered them in more than 165 countries. Andrew Enrietti, one of its senior executives, said: 'What attracted us to the Cadillac Formula 1 Team is the ambition and ingenuity behind what they're building.'
>
> The announcement names no medicines and no programmes yet. It ties the deal to precision, reliability and performance, which matter in both worlds.
>
> There are no results, because it hasn't started. The signal is the timing. Two drug companies signed with F1 teams within a month of each other. And a new American team chose one of them."

**Sanofi at McLaren**

> "Sanofi has worked with McLaren since 2022, and this one isn't about a logo. Its CEO, Paul Hudson, said: 'We want to run our lines with the speed, precision and efficiency of an F1 racing team.'
>
> Through the partnership, McLaren's people work with Sanofi's factory teams. They look at where production time is lost. And they model and simulate how a line switches from one product to the next, the way a team plans a pit stop.
>
> This is one of the few with published numbers. In February last year, Sanofi said changeover times were down by up to 40%, and downtime by up to 50%. That's the latest update we can find, so we'd check where it stands now."

**Anthropic at Williams**

> "You know Anthropic from your own lab. It has been with Williams since February, with Claude as the team's 'Official Thinking Partner'. When it signed, Mike Krieger from Anthropic said: 'Formula 1 is ultimately about the pairing of human endeavour and technical excellence.'
>
> Through the deal, the team says Claude works alongside its engineers and strategists, on race strategy, car development and running the team. Its name is on the car, the drivers and the kit.
>
> No results are published yet. The signal for you is closer to home. In October, six of your scientists used Claude to redesign part of a CAR T cell. So a company on the Williams car is already part of your story."

**Oracle at Red Bull**

> "Oracle has been Red Bull's title partner since February 2022. Red Bull's principal strategy engineer, Hannah Schmitz, put it simply: 'Every race-winning strategy that we've made since 2021 has been made on Oracle.'
>
> Through the deal, Red Bull runs its race strategy simulations on Oracle's cloud. Before and during each race, it plays out what could happen next, then picks one call.
>
> This one has published numbers. Oracle says the team ran more than 200 billion race strategy simulations last season. And the team has said it will carry on with Oracle into F1's new era. It's the closest thing on the grid to how you work: try a huge number of options on a computer, then commit to one in the real world."

**Nasdaq at Mercedes**

> "Nasdaq has been with Mercedes since May last year. Its CEO, Adena Friedman, said the deal 'embodies our shared passion for technology and performance.'
>
> Through the deal, Nasdaq's name is on the Mercedes pit wall and the engineers' station, at every race. The team gets to use Nasdaq's MarketSite in Times Square, where companies ring the bell. And Mercedes says about a third of its partners already list on Nasdaq.
>
> No return is published. The signal is who it wanted to stand next to: the companies it lists, and the investors who follow them. Mike rang that bell in February."

**What that adds up to**

> "So the honest picture is this. Drug companies have only just arrived. AbbVie came in October, and Viatris starts next season. Neither has named a medicine. As far as we can tell, no biotech is there yet. The ones with numbers are the ones with a real job: Sanofi learning from McLaren how to run its factories, and Oracle running Red Bull's simulations. Most others don't publish. What they show is that they stay and do more, like Oracle carrying on into the new era, or AbbVie going from one baseball team to three sports in a year.
>
> For you, I'd take three things from that. First, talk about how you work, not what you're developing. The design loop and the people are the story, and that keeps you inside the rules. Second, host the short list: the drug companies you want to partner with, your investors, and the scientists you want to hire. Third, start small, in the US where the three races are, and give it one real job before anything bigger."

### "Content is really important for us. How would a team help us make it?"

> "It's one of the most useful parts of a deal. For a company that can't talk about its medicines, it may be the most useful part.
>
> Most deals come with a set number of filming days each year with the drivers and the team's people, and the team's content people help you make it. The team's own channels matter too. F1 is the number one sport on social media, ahead of the NBA.
>
> One thing to know: the race footage is controlled by Formula 1 itself, not the teams. So partner content is filmed around the race: at the factory, in the garage, or on filming days.
>
> Two examples. CrowdStrike made a series with Mercedes where the team's specialists explain the safety kit on the car, from the halo to the helmet, and Mercedes says it got 6.2 million views. And this season, doctors from UChicago Medicine are studying the Haas team's own people: their sleep, stress and travel across a season. Haas has put it forward as a talk for SXSW next year. That's science content with no product in it.
>
> For you, I'd build it around the loop. Put one of a team's design engineers next to one of your protein scientists. Each brings one thing they had to get right. For the engineer, a part on the car. For your scientist, a protein that has to fit one target and nothing else. Then each walks through the loop: design it on a computer, build it, test it, learn, go again. How many versions did it take? What did the test show that the computer missed? When did they throw a design away?
>
> Both sides know what a test costs. F1 teams have limits on how much computer and wind tunnel time they can use for aerodynamics, so every test has to count. You've made and tested 42,000 proteins, so you know what a failed one teaches you. No stunt, and no medicine. Just two people who design things for a living.
>
> Your regulatory team would check every film, and we'd keep drug names and health claims out completely.
>
> If content matters to you, we'd spell it out in the deal: how many days, which drivers and team people, what the team posts on its own channels, and how long and where you can use it."

### "Why are these brands in F1?"

> "Two reasons, mostly.
>
> The first is learning, and being seen to learn. A team designs, builds and tests at a speed most companies can't match, and it does it under a spending cap. Sanofi's CEO said he wanted his lines to run 'with the speed, precision and efficiency of an F1 racing team.' AbbVie talks about the same 'mindset of continuous improvement.'
>
> The second is people. The partners they want to host, the investors they want to see, and the people they want to hire. Barclays went into Williams this year to spend time with its clients.
>
> For Generate, I'd guess it's mostly the second, with the first as the story. You already work the way a team does. What you need is time with a short list of people, and a way to explain what you do without talking about medicines."

### "Who are our competitors in F1, and which teams are available?"

> "I don't want to promise you anything on that yet, because right now it would be a guess. As far as we can tell, no AI biotech has a deal with an F1 team.
>
> But some teams have partners close to you. AbbVie is McLaren's official pharmaceutical partner. Viatris will be Cadillac's from next season. Sanofi, which sells one of the big asthma and COPD medicines, has worked with McLaren since 2022. Google is at McLaren too, and its parent company owns Isomorphic Labs, which designs drugs with AI. And Anthropic, your research partner, is at Williams. That can open a door, or it can need care. Deals like these usually come with exclusivity.
>
> So the first thing we do is check where there's a conflict, and rule those teams out. I'd expect McLaren and Cadillac to be hard, and we'd confirm that.
>
> The second thing is understanding what you want out of it. If it's time with drug companies, that can point to different teams than if it's investors, or hiring. Then we come back with the teams where it fits.
>
> One thing worth knowing: these contracts are often written quite narrowly. So something like 'Official Generative Biology Partner' would be narrower than 'Official Pharmaceutical Partner'. But we'd check each team before saying any more.
>
> To give you an idea of what a partner like you does once they're in, look at Sanofi at McLaren. It didn't just buy a logo. McLaren's people worked with its factories on how a line switches from one product to the next, the way a team plans a pit stop. Sanofi says changeover times fell by up to 40%. That's a much stronger story than a sticker on the car."

### "It's very crowded. Won't we get lost?"

> "It's a real risk. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and a lot of them are just logos.
>
> The ones that get noticed do one of two things. Some give their product a real job, like Oracle running Red Bull's simulations. Others make something people want to watch.
>
> You have two advantages. First, as far as we can tell, you'd be the first biotech on the grid. Second, you don't need everyone to notice you. You need a few dozen drug companies, your investors and the people you want to hire to remember the weekend. That's a much smaller job than selling to fans."

### "Why would a team want a partner like us?"

> "Teams aren't short of technology partners. Tech companies now spend more on F1 teams than any other kind of business, and a Reuters report in May counted eight new AI deals in six months.
>
> What's rarer is a company that runs the same loop in biology. You design proteins on a computer, make them, test them and learn, the way a team does with parts. Your Chroma model was published in Nature. And Mike says you went from no drug programmes to Phase 3 in under five years. That's a conversation a team's engineers would enjoy.
>
> You'd also bring a different story. F1 is full of cars, banks and software. Medicine and science are new, and AbbVie and Viatris have only just arrived.
>
> And you'd bring people a team would like to meet: Amgen, Novartis and Zealand as your partners, and the investors who now follow you on Nasdaq. Whether a team wants any of that, and whether a current partner already covers it, is what we'd check first."

### "What would a first deal look like for a company our size?"

> "I'll be straight with you: a full team partnership is a big step while you're paying for two Phase 3 trials, and I wouldn't start there. There are smaller ways in.
>
> The first is hosting. A few US race weekends with the drug companies you want to partner with, and your investors, to see whether it works. Passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend. Miami, Austin and Las Vegas are the obvious ones, and Montreal is the closest race to Boston.
>
> The second is content. A deal built around a few filming days and the design loop idea, with the team posting it on its own channels.
>
> The third is a driver, or F1 Academy, the women's series, which runs on F1 race weekends. Both cost less than a team. F1 Academy could fit if hiring more women into science and engineering matters to you. With a driver, the rules on medicines apply to everything they say about you.
>
> If one of those works, a bigger deal can come later. AbbVie's baseball work started with one team and became the whole league a year later."

### "What does it cost?"

> "It depends a lot on the team and what's included. A supplier or entry-level partner deal starts in the low single-digit millions a year. Official partner deals sit above that. Hosting at a few races, a driver deal or a content deal costs a lot less. For where you are right now, I'd start at the lower end and show you options there first."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

> "You can go to a team directly. They'll tell you about their own team, which is fair enough. We worked only with Mercedes and Ferrari for over twelve years, and then stopped, so we could look at all eleven teams for the brand. The teams pay our fee, so having us compare them costs you nothing. We also work across a lot of sports, so we can tell you plainly when F1 isn't the best fit. And with AbbVie at McLaren and Viatris coming to Cadillac, someone has to check each team quietly before your name goes anywhere."

### "Which team would fit us?"

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. It depends on what you want out of it, whether that's partners, investors or hiring. It also depends on which races matter most, and where there's no conflict with the drug companies already on the grid. That's what we'd come back to you with."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "We'd agree that before anything's signed. For you I'd look at three things. Partners: which drug companies you spent time with, and what followed. Deals take a long time, so we'd track the meetings and next steps, not just signatures. People: how many good scientists and engineers applied, and where they heard of you. And the name: how well known Generate is with investors and scientists, measured before and after, plus how far the films travelled.
>
> It can show up in the numbers. Airwallex says its revenue grew 50% year on year in its Europe and Americas regions during its McLaren partnership, and 57% in the UK from new customers."

### "We're a clinical-stage biotech. We can't advertise our medicines. What would we even say?"

> "You wouldn't talk about medicines at all. And you'd be in good company. AbbVie and Viatris have both just signed with F1 teams, and neither named a single medicine. AbbVie's deal is about a charity, and stories about how progress happens.
>
> What you'd say is how you work. You design a protein on a computer, make it, test it, learn and go again. A team does the same with every part on the car. That's a story engineers and fans understand, and it doesn't need a drug name.
>
> You can also talk about people. Your scientists and engineers, and why someone would leave a tech company to design proteins. And you can talk about the name. Most people have never heard of Generate. You want the right people to know it.
>
> What you can't do is say anything about what your drugs do. That's the line we'd build everything around."

### "Are there rules on what we can do through F1?"

> "Yes, and most of them are yours, not F1's. As far as we can tell, neither F1 nor the teams publish rules for drug companies. AbbVie and Viatris show it can be done.
>
> The bigger rules come from your side. In plain terms, I'd expect four limits.
>
> First, the audience is worldwide. Outside the US and New Zealand, most countries don't allow prescription medicines to be advertised to the public at all. F1 is shown around the world, so anything on a car or in a film has to work everywhere.
>
> Second, in the US, the FDA's Office of Prescription Drug Promotion checks drug ads, and it has been much stricter since September last year. And a drug still in trials can't be promoted as safe or effective at all. So the brand is Generate and the science, never GB-0895.
>
> Third, hospitality. The US industry code says no sporting event tickets for doctors, and Europe's codes are similar. So no doctors, and nobody running your trials, on the guest list.
>
> Fourth, you're listed. What you say to investors at a race follows the same disclosure rules as any investor meeting. No news that could move the share price, shared in private.
>
> I'm not your lawyer, so please check all of this with your regulatory and legal team. We'd build their review time into the plan."

### "Our share price has had a hard year, and investors watch every dollar. Is this the right time?"

> "It might not be. You listed at $16 in February, and the shares are below that now. You're paying for two Phase 3 trials, and you've said your cash lasts into the first half of 2028. Investors will want anything new tied to the business, not to being seen.
>
> So I wouldn't propose a big team deal now. I'd look at something small that does one job. Mike has said he expects one or two more big partnerships in the next twelve months. If a few race weekends help with those conversations, that's easy to explain. For scale, the Zealand deal alone brought in $30 million up front. So the test is simple: does it help with the next one?
>
> We'd start from your plan for the next two years and work back. If the answer is a couple of US race weekends first, that's fine. If it's 'not yet', we'll tell you."

### "Our buyers are a few dozen pharma business development teams. Why a global sport?"

> "Fair question. You wouldn't be buying the TV audience. You'd be buying three days with people who are hard to get time with.
>
> A business development lead at a big drug company gets invited to a lot of dinners. A race weekend is different. It's three days, somewhere they want to be, with time to talk between sessions. Passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend. Over a season, that covers a lot of your list.
>
> The global part helps in a different way. Your partners are in California, Basel and Copenhagen, and F1 races in America and across Europe, close to all three. And teams introduce their partners to each other. AbbVie is on the grid now, and so are the banks.
>
> If what you need is one meeting with one company, F1 is too big a tool. If you want the right few hundred people to know Generate, it can work."

### "Could F1 help us hire scientists and engineers?"

> "It can, if it's built in from the start. 43% of F1 fans are under 35. And the people you want, machine learning engineers and data scientists, are the same kind of people F1 teams hire to run their simulations.
>
> The deals that do this well make hiring part of the job. Komatsu runs an engineering academy at Williams that brings ten students a year into the team, found through Formula Student, the university race car competition. And content helps. An engineer and a scientist comparing how they work is the kind of film a good candidate watches.
>
> But I'd be careful. You have about 300 people, and you hire for specific roles. F1 won't fill those on its own. What it can do is make Generate a name that people outside biology have heard of."

### "What's the biggest risk?"

> "Signing a logo with no job. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and the ones that get lost put a name on the car and stop there. A deal needs a real job, a plan for the people you'll host, and someone on your side who owns it.
>
> The second risk is a conflict you didn't see. AbbVie is at McLaren, Viatris is coming to Cadillac, and contracts often cover more than the title says. That's why we check every team before your name goes anywhere.
>
> The third is picking the wrong option for the job. If what you need is time with a few drug companies in Europe, tennis or golf might do it better. If it's hiring, a university programme might come first. We'd rather tell you that up front than sell you the wrong thing.
>
> And for you there's a fourth: the rules on medicines. One wrong line in a film, or the wrong guest at a race, would cost more than the deal is worth. That's why your regulatory team should be in from day one."

# Vast x SPORTFIVE: F1 Discovery Call Script

**Call type:** First call / discovery with an insider. The contact has worked in Formula 1, so there is nothing to sell about the sport's size. The goal: a lighthearted, human opening, then an honest working session on whether there is a real fit between what Vast does and what Formula 1 offers as a platform. Share how we work, learn the brand, explore entry points, agree next steps.

**How to use this script:** Every spoken block is written the way you would say it out loud, contractions and all. Do not read it word for word. Glance at the bold anchors, then talk. This person knows the sport, so skip anything that smells like F1 education; respect for their knowledge is the fastest way to their trust. Lighthearted does not mean casual on facts: the claims discipline below still applies to every word.

## Call Navigator (keep this section open)

**The goal of this call:** find out together whether Formula 1 and Vast belong in the same plan. They should leave feeling this was a peer conversation, not a pitch, and that our advice costs them nothing.

**The flow:**

1. **Opening: hope all is well, the LinkedIn line, no selling needed, propose the structure.** 1 min
2. **SPORTFIVE in a few lines, then play back what we know about Vast.** 3 min
3. **Discovery: learn the brand, fill the brief.** 15 min

**Three messages that must land:**

- **No selling, just fit.** They know how big F1 is. The only question on the table is whether there is an actual fit between what Vast does and what this platform offers. Say it early; it sets the tone for everything.
- **The neutral seat.** Twelve-plus years exclusive with Mercedes and Ferrari, given up deliberately to work brand-first across all eleven teams. The rights holders pay our fee, so the advice is free. For someone who knows the paddock, this is the part that is rare.
- **Honest sizing, honest answer.** We map entry points with real numbers against their brief, and if the right answer is "not yet", we say it.

## Prep notes (not spoken)

### Who Vast is (verified 20 October 2026; re-verify on call day)
- American space company, Long Beach, California, founded 2021. Roughly a thousand people, with hundreds of open roles reported; they are hiring hard.
- Founded and first funded by Jed McCaleb, the billionaire who co-founded Ripple and Stellar. CEO since 2023 is Max Haot, who founded Launcher (acquired by Vast) and before that Livestream.
- What they are building, in plain words: **Haven-1**, intended to be the world's first commercial space station. A single module, launched on a SpaceX Falcon 9, with crews visiting on SpaceX Dragon capsules. Launch timing has moved: the target through late 2025 was May 2026; the latest reporting (Payload Space) puts it no earlier than the first quarter of 2027, with crewed visits to follow once the station is proven safe. Treat the date carefully and attribute it; never state a launch date as fact.
- Behind it: **Haven-2**, their proposed successor to the International Space Station, competing in NASA's Commercial LEO Destinations program. The ISS is due to retire around 2030, and NASA was expected to pick Phase 2 winners in 2026. Check status before the call.
- Funding: McCaleb funded the company from his own pocket first. In March 2026 Vast raised $500 million ($300M equity, $200M debt), led by Balerion Space Ventures, with In-Q-Tel and Qatar Investment Authority among the participants.
- Customers, current and future: NASA, sovereign space agencies without their own stations, research organizations and pharma, and private astronauts. Trust and credibility are the product; nobody books a mission with a company they half believe.

### Flags
- **Capacity caution, moderate:** well funded (billionaire founder, a $500M raise, sovereign backers), but pre-revenue, with every dollar pointed at hardware and a launch that has already slipped. Marketing money will exist only if it clearly serves credibility, talent or customers. Surface budget in the first half, size every idea with honest numbers, and never assume big-team money.
- **Insider audience:** the contact has F1 history. Do not recite fan-growth stats at them, do not explain Liberty Media, do not use the sport-in-thirty-seconds speech. Numbers only when they sharpen a point.
- **Category check:** claim nothing open. Space and aerospace names have appeared around this sport in various forms, and the exact team-by-team and series picture is verified at the recommendations stage, fresh.
- **Claims discipline:** never say or imply any F1 team or the series has asked for a space station partner; Q1 gives the straight answer for your outreach email line. Haven-1 "first commercial space station" is Vast's stated goal, so attribute it ("what they intend to be the first"). Launch dates are attributed to reports. Never claim what any team is or is not willing to do.
- **Names check:** SPORTFIVE citations in this script (Mercedes and Ferrari exclusivity history, RUCKUS at Haas, Airwallex at McLaren) were verified in September 2026 checks; Vast facts were checked on 11 October 2026 and refreshed on 20 October 2026; no NASA Phase 2 award for the ISS successor was found as of that date. Re-verify on call day.
- **Pipeline check:** confirm no live deal in space, aerospace, defense-adjacent or launch categories, and check the register for earlier Vast outreach.

### Numbers cheat sheet (sparingly; this audience knows the sport)
- Business side, if useful: 350+ partner companies across the grid, 25+ per team; team sponsorship has doubled since 2020 to $2.2bn.
- The room: F1 fans index 160 for business decision makers, the highest in sport; 66% of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. The paddock at Monaco, Miami or Abu Dhabi gathers sovereign guests and serious wealth like almost nowhere else.
- Reach, only if asked: 827M fans, 43% under 35; 1.83bn TV audience in 2025; around 24 race weekends on five continents; three US races, two American teams, Apple broadcasting in the US.
- Talent angle: the sport is an engineering magnet, and 43% of fans are under 35.
- Pricing logic: eleven teams, one seat per category, three to five year deals. Entry positions start in the low single-digit millions; driver and content deals sit below that.

## Part 3: Discovery, learn the brand (12 to 15 minutes)

*[Conversation starters, not a form. Ask one, stay quiet, follow what they give you. Their answers become the brief.]*

**Open the story**
- "Tell me where Vast is right now, in your own words. What's going well, and what's the next hill to climb?"
- "What are the key focus areas for the next twelve months, beyond the obvious one of getting Haven-1 flying?"

**What the brand needs to be known for**
- "Here's the question I find most useful for a company like yours: what does Vast need to be famous for, and with whom, over the next three years? Governments, researchers, future crew members, engineers you want to hire, the public?"
- "And is the job right now being known at all, or being believed? Those are different problems, and they have different answers."

**Customers and buyers**
- "Who really buys what Vast sells, today and in five years? Space agencies, research organizations, pharma, private individuals?"
- "Who are the people you most need in a room, and struggle to get there?"

**Talent**
- "You're hiring at scale in a brutal market for engineers. How much of your brand effort is really a talent story?"

**Markets**
- "Is this a US story first, or global from day one? Your future customers include countries, which makes the question more interesting than usual."

**Evaluation and success**
- "If Vast did something in this sport and it worked, what would be different a year later? And who inside Vast judges that?"
- "And the other side: what would make it a failure in your eyes?"

**Marketing today**
- "What does your marketing and comms effort focus on today, and what's working?"
- "Is the bigger job top of the funnel or bottom of the funnel: being discovered by the world, or converting the handful of serious conversations you're already in?"

**Budget and timing**
- "Now the unromantic question: is there a budget range in mind for something like this? I ask early because it decides which doors we even look at, and it saves us both time."
- "And timing. You have a launch coming. Is this a now conversation, a launch-year conversation, or a Haven-2 era conversation? All three are fine; they just change what we'd bring back."

**Decision process**
- "Apart from you, who'd be part of this decision? Max, Jed, the board?"

**Hospitality closer**
- "Last one, my favorite. If I handed you a paddock weekend tomorrow, who would you bring, and why those people? Given who your future customers are, I suspect your answer is more interesting than most."

*[If they hesitate on budget:]*

> "No problem, you don't need a number today. If we get to recommendations, you'll see options at different levels, including the smallest sensible ones, with what each level buys."

## Part 4: Entry points, and why Vast could fit (8 to 10 minutes)

*[No F1 education here; they know the sport. Go straight to the doors, then the fit, then the sketches.]*

*[New to F1 (only with the alternative opening): give the thirty-second picture first.]*

> "Quick picture of how the sport is set up, because it explains the options. Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company: the calendar, the broadcasts, the races. The FIA writes the rules. And then there are eleven teams, including two American ones, Haas and Cadillac, each an independent business with its own partners. Around 24 race weekends a year, a total TV audience of 1.8 billion last season, and in the Paddock Club two thirds of the guests are senior executives."

### 4.1 The entry points, quickly

> "You know the sport, so let me skip the tour and just map the doors as we see them from the brand side.
>
> **The series itself.** Global, neutral, premium-priced. Formula 1 as a platform, every race.
>
> **A team.** Where the story and the garage live. Everything from title deals down to entry-level partner seats, and the entry seats are where most sensible first steps happen: low single-digit millions, not headline money.
>
> **A driver.** The smallest door. Personal channels, appearances, campaigns. Underrated for storytelling.
>
> **And the doors people forget:** race-by-race hospitality without any branding at all, and content partnerships built with a team but sized like a campaign, not a sponsorship.
>
> For Vast, my instinct says the right first door is small and sharp, and I'll tell you why."

### 4.2 Why Vast and this sport could fit

> "Three reasons we thought this was worth a call, and you tell me if they hold.
>
> **One, the story is a gift in both directions.** Formula 1 is the biggest engineering show on earth. Vast is building the most ambitious machine of this decade. The people building the first commercial space station meeting the people building the fastest cars on earth: that content writes itself, and nobody owns that crossover today. For a company whose product is the future, there's no better-fitting stage.
>
> **Two, the paddock is quietly your customer list.** Your buyers are governments, research leaders and a small number of very wealthy individuals. Walk the Paddock Club at Monaco, Miami or Abu Dhabi and that's exactly who's standing there. Two thirds of that room are senior executives, and the sovereign guest lists at certain races are unlike anything else in sport. You don't need 800 million fans. You need forty specific people a year, properly hosted.
>
> **Three, belief, borrowed fast.** Vast sells something people have to believe in before they can buy it. A serious engineering platform transfers that belief faster than any press release, to customers, to regulators, and very much to the engineers you're trying to hire. And the same weekend that hosts a space agency also recruits a thermal engineer. Few platforms do both jobs at once.
>
> One honest note. You have a launch to fund and a date that has already moved, and we respect that completely. So whatever we'd recommend will be small, sharp and pointed at those forty people and that story, not a big logo for its own sake. And if the honest answer is that this waits until Haven-1 flies, we'll say exactly that."

### 4.3 Three sketches (ideas, not proposals)

*[Offer as sketches; their reaction feeds the brief. All deliberately right-sized.]*

> "To make it concrete, three sketches. Early thoughts, nothing more.
>
> **One, the two machines.** A content series pairing your engineers with a team's engineers: life support meets aerodynamics, orbital docking meets pit stops, two teams building machines where failure isn't an option. Engineering fans devour exactly this, and it humanizes Vast to the people you want to hire.
>
> **Two, the orbit room.** A small, surgical hospitality program at three races, say Miami, Monaco and Abu Dhabi: your target agencies, research partners and future customers hosted properly, with your leadership in the room and the race as the excuse. Seven passes can host twenty guests across one weekend. No logo required at all.
>
> **Three, the first flag in orbit.** The headline idea, if the brief ever earns it: a right-sized team partnership where Vast's name rides the car and the team's crest flies on Haven-1's first mission. The first partnership connecting the grid and orbit. That's a global story for the price of an entry-level seat, and the kind of first this sport loves."

## Part 5: Questions they may ask. Prepared answers.

*[Quick index. Q1 is the one your outreach email invited; know it cold.]*

- Q1. Your email said teams are looking for a space station partner. Which teams
- Q2. Which entry point would fit Vast
- Q3. What does it cost
- Q4. I know the teams myself, why go through you
- Q5. How would we measure it
- Q6. We are pre-revenue and every dollar goes into hardware
- Q7. Are competitors or other space brands already in
- Q8. Is this not a distraction until Haven-1 flies

### Q1. "Your email said teams are looking for a space station partner. Which teams?"

> "Fair question, and I'll give you the straight version.
>
> Teams don't publish shopping lists saying 'wanted: space station partner'. What they do tell us, constantly, because we run grid analyses all year, is that they want brands that redefine what innovation means next to their name. Not the thirtieth logo. The partner that makes the team look like the future.
>
> So when we did our research and saw what Vast is building, the first commercial space station, it was obvious. That's why I wrote to you. That's the honest origin of that line.
>
> Which teams specifically? That's exactly what the mapping after this call answers. We take your brief, go team by team and to the series, and come back with names and reasons. And if the answer turns out to be that none of them are right for Vast right now, I'll tell you that too. You know this sport well enough to smell a bluff, so you won't get one from me."

### Q2. "Which entry point would fit Vast best?"

> "Today, my instinct, not a recommendation: small and sharp. Hospitality pointed at the forty people who matter to you, content built on the engineering story, and only then, if it earns itself, an entry-level team seat with a first attached to it.
>
> But that's instinct. The mapping turns it into names, numbers and a sequence. Give us the brief today and we'll come back with exactly that."

### Q3. "What does a partnership cost?"

> "You probably remember the logic: eleven teams, one seat per category, multi-year deals, so scarcity keeps prices high. Rule of thumb today: entry-level team positions start in the low single-digit millions a year, official partner tiers sit above that, and driver, content and pure hospitality programs sit below, some well below.
>
> The honest point for Vast: the ideas I'd explore first mostly live at the bottom of that ladder. And because the rights holders pay our fee, finding the exact numbers against your brief costs you nothing."

### Q4. "I know this sport. I could call the teams myself. Why go through you?"

> "You could, and with your background you'd get the meetings. Two reasons brands with insiders still use us.
>
> One, each team will sell you their story, and they'll sell it well. We put all eleven next to each other: inventory, pricing, appetite, and what each one would mean for Vast specifically. The neutral seat is the product.
>
> Two, we carry the process: business case, negotiation, the benchmarks from doing this every week. You stay the smart buyer; we do the legwork.
>
> And since the teams pay our fee, you lose nothing by letting us run it. If you ever felt we added no value, you could still pick up the phone yourself."

### Q5. "How would we measure something like this?"

> "For Vast I'd keep it brutally simple and agree it upfront.
>
> If the job is the room: did the right agencies, researchers and future customers show up, and what happened in the ninety days after each weekend. Meetings, pilots, relationships you can name.
>
> If the job is the story: reach and engagement on the content, and brand tracking in the audiences you care about, including engineering candidates. Recruiting is measurable: applications and offer-acceptance move when a brand gets hot.
>
> And if there's branding involved, the standard media-value tracking on top. We'd weight it to whatever job you hire the platform for."

### Q6. "We are pre-revenue. Every dollar goes into hardware. Why would we spend on sport?"

> "Maybe you shouldn't. I mean that, and this call is how we find out.
>
> But here's the other side. Your hardest problems are belief problems: customers who must trust you with crews, regulators and agencies who must take you seriously, and a thousand engineers you still need to hire. Those are exactly the problems this platform is unusually good at, and at the sizes I'd start with, this costs campaign money, not sponsorship money.
>
> So the real question isn't hardware versus marketing. It's whether a few hundred thousand to low millions, pointed at belief, buys you more than the same money anywhere else. That's a numbers conversation, and we'll bring the numbers. If they don't clear the bar, we'll say so."

### Q7. "Are other space companies already in Formula 1?"

> "Space and aerospace names have shown up around this sport in different forms over the years, so I won't answer from memory; the category moves and you deserve the exact picture.
>
> Mapping the space, aerospace and adjacent categories across all eleven teams and the series is part of what we'd do after this call. You'd see who's in, who's out, and where a first-mover position is still open. That last part is the interesting one for Vast."

### Q8. "Shouldn't this all wait until Haven-1 has flown?"

> "It might. If the brief says the money and attention belong entirely on the launch, that's a legitimate answer and we'll give it to you straight.
>
> But consider the other timeline. The months around your launch are when the world decides what Vast means. Stories get written, customers pick sides, engineers choose where to work. Platforms like this take time to set up, and the scarce seats don't wait for anyone's launch calendar. So even if activation waits for the right moment, knowing your options now costs nothing and protects the moment when you'll want them.
>
> That's the case for doing the homework today, not for signing anything today."

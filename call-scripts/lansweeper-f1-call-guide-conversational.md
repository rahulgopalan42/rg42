## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who actually watches Formula 1? Is it relevant to our audience?"

> "Yeah, I think there's actually quite a strong alignment, particularly for a company that sells to other businesses. Formula 1 fans index at 160 for business decision-makers, with 100 being the general population. That's the highest of any sport.
>
> So, in practical terms, the people who decide how a company manages and secures its technology are considerably more likely than average to follow Formula 1.
>
> What's particularly interesting for a software business is how those fans buy. They index at 126 when it comes to researching products online before making a purchase, which is also the highest across sports. Given that, as I understand it, people can start with Lansweeper on a free plan before they ever speak to sales, I think that behaviour is quite relevant.
>
> And then there's the scale and the geography. Formula 1 has around 827 million fans globally, with more than 20 race weekends across five continents. From a US perspective, you've got three races, two American teams and Apple now broadcasting the sport there. And in Europe, next year's calendar covers Belgium, the UK and Spain, which are all markets where you've got people on the ground."

### "We sell to IT and security leaders, not fans. Who's actually in the paddock?"

> "Yeah, and I think that's actually one of the strongest reasons for a B2B brand to consider Formula 1. Around two-thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. And across the grid, you've got more than 350 partner companies, with most teams having upwards of 25, all of which are bringing their own clients and leadership teams to races.
>
> When you look at who those partners are, there's quite a bit of overlap with the sectors you sell into. On the manufacturing side, you've got Komatsu with Williams and Stanley Black & Decker with McLaren. In financial services, UBS is with Mercedes and Barclays is with Williams. And in healthcare, UChicago Medicine is with Haas.
>
> What's also interesting is that two of the customers you name publicly already have a presence in the sport. Nestlé's KitKat is a partner of Formula 1 itself, and Sephora became a partner of F1 Academy this year. I'm obviously not suggesting the others are your customers. You'd know that far better than I would.
>
> Teams also actively connect their partners with each other. TeamViewer has spoken about Mercedes hosting a technology forum on the Friday before a race, bringing together the senior IT decision-makers that its different technology partners had invited.
>
> And then there's the time you get with people. A typical sales meeting with a CIO or a CISO might be thirty minutes. A race weekend runs across three days."

### "How are other technology brands using Formula 1, and what are they getting out of it?"

> "Yeah, absolutely. We've actually spent quite a bit of time looking at this, because IT operations and security is already quite an established area within Formula 1. What's particularly useful is that most of these companies have a genuine job inside the team, so we can look at exactly what they do and what they've said about it."

**NinjaOne at Audi**

> "NinjaOne is probably the most relevant example, because they're competing for the attention of the same IT teams you are. They joined Audi in January this year, on a multi-year agreement, as the team's official endpoint management, mobile device management and SaaS backup partner. That was before Audi had even raced in Formula 1.
>
> What's interesting is how both sides positioned it. NinjaOne's CEO, Sal Sferlazza, spoke about supporting Audi's IT team with the speed and confidence it needs to operate at its best. Audi's chief commercial officer, Stefano Battiston, talked about choosing partners that share the team's approach to performance.
>
> In practical terms, Audi is deploying NinjaOne's platform to manage and monitor its devices and systems across the factory and trackside, from a single console.
>
> They haven't published any commercial results, and it's only their first season, so I wouldn't want to speculate. But it's a multi-year commitment, and it tells you that a direct competitor looked at Formula 1 this year and decided it was worth the investment."

**TeamViewer at Mercedes**

> "TeamViewer is probably the most established example in IT operations. They've been with Mercedes since 2021, initially on a five-year agreement, and they've since extended beyond that.
>
> Their head of sports partnerships, Faith Wheller, explained the rationale quite clearly to BlackBook Motorsport in May. She said Formula 1 fans fit their ideal customer profile, which is enterprise IT decision-makers.
>
> What's particularly interesting is that Mercedes was already a TeamViewer customer before the sponsorship. In April, the team moved onto TeamViewer's newest platform, which TeamViewer says gives it visibility and control across thousands of IT and operational devices, from the factory to the trackside garage.
>
> From an activation perspective, they use it very much as a hosting platform. In Miami this year, they brought Mercedes' IT director to a dinner and Q&A with their customers. And Wheller made the point that without genuine stories about how the team uses the product, it's simply branding on a car.
>
> They haven't disclosed specific figures. But they extended, and Wheller said one of the reasons was that Mercedes is helping their business grow. I think that's quite a meaningful signal."

**ServiceNow at Aston Martin**

> "ServiceNow is another one that's close to your world, and I know it's also a platform you integrate with. They've been with Aston Martin since 2023, and early last year the two renewed on what the team called an elevated, multi-year basis.
>
> ServiceNow's chief marketing officer, Colin Fleming, said at the time that Formula 1 and ServiceNow share the same DNA, around precision, speed and innovation.
>
> According to Aston Martin, the platform automates IT and facilities requests across its technology campus and at the track. With the renewal, that extended into HR and finance, and ServiceNow's branding moved onto the drivers' race suits for the first time.
>
> Again, there aren't any publicly available commercial results. But they renewed, the role inside the team got bigger and the branding grew with it. So, it's a good example of a partnership that started with a real job and expanded from there."

**CrowdStrike at Mercedes**

> "On the security side, CrowdStrike is the longest-running example. They've been with Mercedes since 2019. Their CEO, George Kurtz, described Formula 1 at the time as a highly data-intensive sport, where protecting that data creates a competitive advantage.
>
> In practical terms, Mercedes uses CrowdStrike's platform at the factory in Brackley and at the track. But what people tend to remember is the content. Two years ago, they produced a 13-part series called Safe and Secure, where members of the team explained the safety technology on the car, from the halo to the helmets.
>
> According to Mercedes, that series generated around 6.2 million video views and 8.3 million impressions. And then last year, George Kurtz personally acquired a stake in the team and became its technology adviser.
>
> So, we can't put a revenue figure on it. But it's a relationship that's now in its eighth season, and it has deepened quite significantly."

**Keeper Security at Williams**

> "Keeper Security is an interesting one because of how quickly it progressed. They're a password and privileged access management company, and they joined Williams two years ago on a multi-year agreement. Just twelve months later, on the eve of last year's Miami Grand Prix, the two announced an early renewal on a new long-term basis.
>
> James Vowles, the team principal, described that early renewal as a strong endorsement of the relationships the team builds with its partners.
>
> According to Keeper, Williams uses its platform at the track and at its headquarters in Grove to protect passwords and privileged accounts. And this year, Keeper launched a global campaign featuring Alex Albon, including three TV commercials filmed during pre-season testing.
>
> They haven't published any commercial results. But an early renewal, followed by a bigger campaign in the third season, is a positive indication. It's also a good example of starting with one clear role and building from there."

**Cisco at McLaren**

> "Cisco has been with McLaren since 2021. It started with Webex, their collaboration product, and it has expanded several times since.
>
> In July, they extended again. The partnership now covers the team's networking, network security and observability, with Splunk's existing relationship with McLaren being brought under Cisco.
>
> What's particularly relevant for you is how they use it. Just before Silverstone, Cisco hosted an executive event at the McLaren Technology Centre. It brought Cisco's own customers and partners together with McLaren's leadership and other McLaren partners, including Mastercard and Deloitte.
>
> Cisco's Bryan Jones made the point that when a championship-winning team builds on the full Cisco portfolio, it validates their approach in front of customers. Cisco has also pointed out that in a sport where spending is capped, a team choosing to invest in your technology says something about how critical it is.
>
> No commercial return has been published. The signal is five years, and a role that keeps expanding."

**Salesforce with Formula 1**

> "And then, slightly outside IT operations, Salesforce is one of the few examples where we've actually seen published numbers. They've been a partner of Formula 1 itself since 2022, and in March they extended through 2030.
>
> Formula 1's own fan service runs on Salesforce, including AI agents that handle routine issues like logins and streaming problems.
>
> Salesforce says chat handling times have come down by 30%, average response times by 80%, and click-through rates have increased by 22%.
>
> Obviously, those are operational metrics rather than sales figures. But I think that's the interesting point for Lansweeper. The strongest technology partnerships tend to produce a number that demonstrates what the product does, and that becomes the story you take to customers."

**What that adds up to**

> "I think there are three things I'd take away from all of that.
>
> Firstly, IT operations is already an established area in Formula 1. NinjaOne, TeamViewer and ServiceNow are all there, and McLaren also works with Freshworks on IT service management. But when you look at the partnerships that have lasted, the common denominator is that there's a genuine job behind them. In several cases, the team was a customer first.
>
> Secondly, very few of them publish commercial results. What they publish is the work, the renewal and the expansion. So, I'd be cautious of anyone promising you a specific return, and I'd build the measurement around your own pipeline instead.
>
> And thirdly, I haven't come across a partner on the grid that leads with asset visibility specifically, with knowing what you've actually got before you try to manage or protect it. I certainly wouldn't describe that as an available category, because several of those companies cover parts of it and we'd need to check their rights. But from a storytelling perspective, I think it's a distinct position.
>
> That's why I'd probably start with the elements that work regardless of which team it is: hosting the right IT and security leaders, and content that explains what you do through the sport."

### "Content is really important for us. How could a Formula 1 team support us with that?"

> "Yeah, absolutely. And I actually think content could be one of the strongest elements of a partnership for Lansweeper. Asset intelligence is quite a difficult thing to explain in the abstract, and Formula 1 gives you a very visual way of showing it.
>
> Typically, as part of a team partnership, you'd negotiate a certain number of content and filming days with the drivers and other members of the team. You'd also have access to the team's content production capabilities and, depending on the rights you've negotiated, their own social media channels.
>
> Formula 1 is currently the number one sport in terms of social media engagement, ahead of the NBA, with around 2.2 billion engagements. Around 61% of fans engage with Formula 1 content on a daily basis.
>
> One thing to be mindful of is that Formula 1 itself owns the race footage. So, the content you'd create with a team would generally be around the race weekend, at the factory, in the garage or during dedicated filming days.
>
> There are already some good examples. CrowdStrike's series with Mercedes had members of the team explaining the safety technology on the car, and according to Mercedes, it generated around 6.2 million views. It wasn't really about the software at all.
>
> And closer to your world, McLaren published a piece in November about how its trackside IT operation works, built around its partner Freshworks. It explains that the team has two days to do what would normally be a six-month office move. IT lays around 3.5 kilometres of cable, and the data centre flies from race to race and is usually the last piece of IT to be installed.
>
> For Lansweeper, I think there's a really natural idea in there. McLaren says it moves six sets of garage equipment around the world, and everything has to be accounted for and working before the cars go out. Imagine a series where a team's IT lead walks through everything that gets connected at a race weekend, device by device, and what happens if one of them isn't where it should be.
>
> You could then connect that back to the question your customers deal with every day. A post on your own blog in June made the point that organizations generally have 25 to 40% more devices than they think they have. That's a very easy story to tell through a Formula 1 garage.
>
> There's also an AI angle. Your October event was about bringing AI out of the shadows, and Reuters reported in May that Ampere had counted eight new AI partnerships across the teams in six months. A conversation with a team's technology leadership about how they keep track of what's running, and where, would be relevant to pretty much every CIO right now.
>
> Of course, we wouldn't want to imply that a team runs on Lansweeper unless that's genuinely part of the partnership. It would simply be about using the team's environment to explain the problem you solve.
>
> The important thing commercially would be making sure those rights are properly negotiated upfront: how many filming days you get, which drivers and team personnel you can access, what the team publishes on its channels, and where and for how long you can use that content."

### "Why are so many technology companies investing in Formula 1?"

> "I think it primarily comes down to two things.
>
> The first is credibility under pressure. A Formula 1 team is split between a factory and a garage that moves to a different country every couple of weeks, and everything has to work first time. And since 2021, teams have operated under a cost cap. Mercedes' IT director made the point this year that every euro under the cost cap has to earn its place. So, if a team chooses to use your technology, that's a fairly strong endorsement to take to a customer.
>
> The second is access to the people who buy. You've got senior executives, customers and partners attending races all over the world, and for a B2B company, three days with a CIO is very difficult to get anywhere else.
>
> It's also where the money has been going. Reuters reported in May that technology is now the biggest category of team sponsorship spending.
>
> And security is a very real topic within the sport itself. Two years ago, the FIA, the sport's governing body, disclosed that phishing attacks had given someone access to two of its email accounts. Last year, it confirmed that security researchers had got into its driver categorisation website, and said it took immediate steps to secure the data.
>
> For Lansweeper, I think there's potentially a third reason, which is explaining what you do. Asset intelligence isn't a term every executive is familiar with. But everyone understands that a team can't run a car if it doesn't know exactly what's on it. Formula 1 gives you a very simple way of making that point."

### "Who are our competitors in Formula 1, and which teams are currently available?"

> "Yeah, so we've done some initial mapping. I wouldn't want to give you any assurances around availability at this stage because we'd need to have those conversations with the teams directly. But we do have a reasonably good understanding of who's currently where.
>
> The closest to you is NinjaOne at Audi, which holds endpoint management, mobile device management and SaaS backup. At Mercedes, you've got TeamViewer, whose platform now covers endpoint management across IT and operational devices, alongside CrowdStrike and Microsoft.
>
> Aston Martin has ServiceNow, which sells its own discovery and asset management products, as well as Zscaler. And McLaren works with Freshworks on IT service management. Freshworks also owns Device42, which does asset discovery and dependency mapping, so that's quite close to your core. McLaren also has Cisco, Okta and Rubrik.
>
> Williams is interesting because its title partner is Atlassian, which you already integrate with, and it also has Keeper Security. Racing Bulls has ThreatLocker and Dynatrace, Red Bull has 1Password and Oracle, and Alpine has Arctic Wolf and Cato Networks.
>
> Haas has RUCKUS on networking and Exein on connected-device security. Cadillac's technology partner is IFS. And from what we've seen in their announcements, Ferrari has Bitdefender and IBM.
>
> The important thing to be mindful of is that category exclusivity can be quite broad. Sometimes a partnership title doesn't tell you the full extent of the contractual rights. So, I wouldn't describe any team as available until we've checked.
>
> So, the first thing we'd do is conduct a proper conflict check. I'd initially expect Audi to be very challenging, and Mercedes, Aston Martin and McLaren to need careful review. We'd assess the remaining teams individually.
>
> The second part is understanding what you're actually trying to achieve. If the priority is hosting CIOs and CISOs in the US, that might lead us in one direction. If it's a genuine technical role that you can build a case study around, that could lead us somewhere else. And if it's being recognized as a cybersecurity brand, the security partners already at each team become more relevant.
>
> We initially had something along the lines of Official IT Asset Intelligence Partner in mind. But given how you now describe yourselves, the wording should probably follow your own language around technology or cyber asset intelligence. And how that title is defined matters, because it determines which existing partners it sits alongside.
>
> To give you a sense of what the end result could look like, I think Cisco at McLaren is a good reference point. The team uses the technology, Cisco brings its customers to the factory before a race, and it has a story it can tell in every sales conversation. We'd just need to establish what the equivalent looks like for Lansweeper."

### "NinjaOne, ServiceNow and TeamViewer are already there. Are we too late?"

> "Yeah, it's a fair question, and I don't think so. That's three teams out of eleven. But I think it's important to be realistic about what it means.
>
> It means you wouldn't be the first IT operations brand in the sport, and those three teams are likely to be difficult. NinjaOne in particular is a direct competitor, and they made their move this year.
>
> But when you look at what each of them actually does, they're managing devices, running service workflows or providing remote access. None of them is really positioned around discovery, around knowing every asset across IT, OT, cloud and now AI before you try to manage it. From a positioning perspective, I think that's a different story.
>
> There's also an advantage to entering an area that's already established. You can see what has lasted. TeamViewer extended beyond its first five years, ServiceNow renewed with a bigger role and Keeper renewed early at Williams. In each case, there was a genuine job behind the partnership.
>
> And the picture isn't fixed. Zoom, for example, was a partner of both Formula 1 and Red Bull a few years ago and no longer appears on either partner list.
>
> So, I wouldn't necessarily frame it as being too late. I'd frame it as making sure that if you do come in, you come in with a story that's clearly your own."

### "Formula 1 is quite crowded commercially. How do we avoid getting lost?"

> "Yeah, absolutely, and I think that's something every brand considering Formula 1 needs to be mindful of. Most teams have upwards of 25 commercial partners, and there are more than 350 partner companies across the grid. A lot of those are technology companies. So, if the strategy is simply to put another software logo on the car, I think there's a genuine risk of getting lost.
>
> The brands that stand out tend to do one of two things. Some have a real job inside the team that they can talk about, like TeamViewer at Mercedes or Cisco at McLaren. Others create content that people genuinely want to watch, like the CrowdStrike series.
>
> And I'd argue that for a B2B business like yours, you don't actually need the wider audience to notice you. You need a relatively small number of IT and security leaders to spend time with you and understand what you do. That's much more about who you host and what you show them than the size of the logo.
>
> The other advantage you have is that your message is simple. You can't manage or protect what you can't see. Most technology partners have to explain something quite abstract. Yours can be explained by standing in a garage."

### "Why would a Formula 1 team be interested in partnering with Lansweeper?"

> "Yeah, I wouldn't necessarily want to speak on behalf of any of the teams, particularly given that several already have partners close to what you do. And I certainly wouldn't tell you that a team needs Lansweeper. But I do think there are a few things that make you an interesting commercial proposition.
>
> The first is your customer base. You've said publicly that more than 30,000 environments use Lansweeper, including companies like Nestlé, Sephora and CMA CGM. Teams are generally interested in partners that bring a network of other businesses with them.
>
> The second is that you sit alongside technology that teams already work with. You integrate with platforms like ServiceNow, Atlassian and Microsoft, which are all present on the grid. So, in principle, you complement an existing set-up rather than asking anyone to replace something.
>
> The third is your own story. You've got a new majority investor, a clear growth plan and a positioning around cybersecurity and AI, which are two areas where teams have been signing new technology partners this year.
>
> Of course, we'd need to establish whether those elements are genuinely attractive to the individual teams and whether an existing partner already covers that ground. But I think there are some good reasons for a team to have that conversation."

### "Would a team actually use our product?"

> "Potentially, but I wouldn't want to promise that. Ultimately, that's a decision for the team's IT department, and it's not something we or the team's commercial people can commit to on their behalf.
>
> What I can tell you is how it has worked for others. TeamViewer's head of sports partnerships has said quite openly that Mercedes has to see value in the technology, because the team pays for it and it sits within the cost cap. And Toto Wolff said this year that every vendor has to earn their place.
>
> So, a team won't use a product simply because the company is a sponsor. In several of the examples we've looked at, the team was a customer first and the sponsorship followed.
>
> There's also the question of what's already in place. A team will have existing tools for managing and securing its devices, and some of those come from partners with contractual rights. So, we'd need to understand where discovery sits in that environment before suggesting there's a role.
>
> What I'd suggest is that we treat it as two separate questions. One is the commercial partnership: hosting, content, association and introductions. The other is whether the team's IT department wants to evaluate the product, on its own merits, in the normal way.
>
> If both come together, that's the strongest outcome, and it gives you a case study. If the product isn't adopted, the partnership needs to stand on its own. And if having the team use Lansweeper is a must-have for you, we'd establish whether that's realistic before going any further."

### "What rights would we actually get, and which ones would be most valuable for a B2B brand like ours?"

> "Yeah, so this is where I think it becomes quite interesting for Lansweeper. A Formula 1 partnership is essentially a combination of different commercial rights. But the importance of those rights varies quite significantly depending on the type of business. For a B2B brand like yours, I would probably look at six key areas, and I'd rank them quite differently from how a consumer brand would.
>
> The first, and the one I'd prioritize, is a genuine role inside the team and the right to talk about it. That means the team's IT function uses the product in some capacity, and you have the contractual right to say so. In practice, that's a case study, a named spokesperson from the team's IT leadership and the ability to reference it in your sales materials.
>
> That's ultimately what TeamViewer, Cisco and ServiceNow have. As we discussed, it isn't something that can be guaranteed, because the technical decision sits with the team. But where it's possible, it's the most valuable right for a business like yours.
>
> The second is hospitality and hosting. That would typically include an allocation of Paddock Club passes across selected races, garage tours and potentially appearances from team personnel at your own customer events. What's quite useful is that passes are generally valid across the three days, so with seven passes, you could host around twenty different guests over a weekend.
>
> A good example is TeamViewer bringing Mercedes' IT director to a customer dinner in Miami. For an audience of IT leaders, an hour with the person who runs a team's technology is probably more valuable than meeting a driver.
>
> The third is partner introductions. Teams actively connect their partners with one another, through partner events, technology forums and direct introductions. Given that the grid includes manufacturers, banks and healthcare organizations, that network could be commercially relevant in its own right.
>
> I'd want us to specify that in the agreement, though, rather than leave it as a general intention. For example, a certain number of facilitated introductions, or a place at the team's partner events.
>
> The fourth is content and thought leadership. That covers filming days, access to the team's technical people and agreed content published through the team's own channels. For Lansweeper, I'd lean towards the engineers and IT leadership rather than the drivers. I'd also lean towards formats your buyers would actually use, like a webinar, a keynote at Lansweeper Connect or a piece of research.
>
> The fifth is the designation and the use of the team's marks. That's the right to call yourselves an official partner, with an agreed title, and to use the team's name and logo across your website, campaigns, event stands and sales presentations. It sounds quite basic, but for a demand generation team, it's something you'd leverage every day.
>
> And the sixth is traditional branding, on the car, the team kit or within the garage. For a business selling to a relatively defined group of IT and security leaders, I'd put that last. It helps with recognition, but it's rarely what drives a B2B result.
>
> Then there are two additional commercial considerations that can significantly influence the investment. One is category exclusivity, in other words how broadly your category is defined and who else the team can work with. The other is territory, whether you're acquiring global rights or rights limited to specific markets.
>
> For Lansweeper, the definition of the category is particularly important, because you sit so close to endpoint management, service management and security. A narrower definition is likely to be easier to agree and less expensive, but it gives you less protection.
>
> So, if I were to prioritize those rights specifically for Lansweeper, I'd put a genuine role and the right to talk about it first, followed by hosting, partner introductions and content. The designation and traditional branding would come after those. But obviously, that's something we'd want to establish together based on how you would actually activate the partnership.
>
> And we'd make sure everything is clearly quantified within the agreement, whether that's the number of passes, filming days, introductions, territories or the duration of those rights."

**Call type:** First call / discovery. Goal: introduce yourself and SPORTFIVE, understand Moncler's objectives, fill out the brief, show how F1 fits a luxury house, agree next steps.

## Part 3: The brief. Key discovery questions (12 to 15 minutes)

*[Work through these as a conversation, not a checklist. Follow their energy. The bold labels are for you; the questions are for them. Capture answers, they become the brief.]*

**Objectives**

- "Who is the customer you most want to win next: younger, more female, more American, more Asian?"

**Markets**

**Product and integration**
- "Would you want Moncler visible on people, not just on boards? Think off-track wardrobe, travel wear, the paddock arrivals that get photographed every weekend. Is product integration like that interesting, or is this more about experiences and clients?"

**Budget and timing**

**Decision process**
- "Apart from you, who shapes this decision? Mr Ruffini's office, the board?"

**Marketing context**
- "How would this sit next to Genius, the shows, the campaigns? Would F1 be a Genius chapter or its own pillar?"
- "Do you have the team to activate at this scale, or would you want support there too?"

**Clients and hospitality**

## Part 4: How Formula 1 partnerships work (8 to 10 minutes)

*[Calibrate to their answer from Part 2. If they know the sport, compress. Invite questions as you go: "Stop me anytime."]*

### 4.1 The sport in thirty seconds

> "Let me give you a simple picture of how the sport is set up, because it explains the options you have.
>
> Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company. They own the commercial side: the calendar, the broadcast deals, the races.
>
> The FIA is the regulator. They write the rules.
>
> And then there are eleven teams. From this coming season it is eleven, including two American teams, Haas and Cadillac. Each team is an independent business with its own owners and its own partnerships. Some belong to car makers, like Ferrari, Mercedes and Audi. Some to consumer giants, like Red Bull. Some to investors.
>
> So a brand can partner at three levels: with the sport itself, with a team, or with a driver personally. Most brands enter through a team, and I can explain why when we get there."

### 4.2 What a team partnership includes

> "What do you get as a partner? Think of it in five parts.
>
> **One, branding.** Your logo on the car, on the drivers' race suits, on the garage. Around 24 race weekends a year, in front of a global TV audience. Last season the total audience was 1.8 billion, and the average race reached 76 million people.
>
> **Two, the official title.** You become, for example, the Official Luxury Apparel Partner of a team. You can use the team's name, logo and imagery in your own campaigns, your boutiques, your shows.
>
> **Three, hospitality.** This is where Formula 1 stands alone. Paddock access, the Paddock Club, garage tours, time with the drivers. Monaco, Las Vegas, Silverstone: rooms your clients cannot buy their way into.
>
> And one detail people miss. A Grand Prix runs over three days, and your passes work every day. So seven passes does not mean seven guests. You bring different clients on Friday, on Saturday, and on Sunday. Seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend, and hundreds across a season. For clienteling, very few platforms multiply like that.
>
> **Four, content and digital.** Access to the team's channels, the drivers, content made with you. Formula 1 is the number one sport in the world for social engagement, 2.2 billion engagements, ahead of the NBA.
>
> **Five, people.** The drivers themselves. Appearances at your shows and store openings, campaign shoots, and the paddock walk every single race weekend, which has become a fashion moment of its own.
>
> And around all of this sits a business platform: every team carries 25 or more partners, more than 350 companies across the grid, hosted together all season."

### 4.3 The audience

*[This is the B2C section. For Moncler, the audience is the argument.]*

> "Now the part that matters most for a consumer brand: who is watching.
>
> Formula 1 has 827 million fans, and the base has grown 63% since 2018. But the story is who the new fans are.
>
> 42% of fans are women, up from 37% in 2018, and in the sport's own survey, three out of four new fans are women. 43% of all fans are under 35, and the average age keeps falling. This is not the old motorsport crowd. This is young, digital, and increasingly female.
>
> And they behave like luxury customers. F1 fans index higher than any other sport's fans for buying premium versions of products. They are joint first for high income. And they are the most brand-loyal fans in sport, twice the loyalty of the average consumer.
>
> Sponsorship moves them too: in the US, fans are 32% more likely to consider a sponsor's product. Among women it is 41%, among Gen Z 39%.
>
> In short: the customer luxury is chasing for the next decade is already sitting in these grandstands."

### 4.4 Why this could fit Moncler

> "Let me tell you why I find this interesting for Moncler. Three reasons.
>
> **First, luxury has already made its move.** The biggest luxury group in the world put Louis Vuitton on Monaco, TAG Heuer on Madrid, and Moet on the podium. At team level, Brioni dresses Alpine, Tommy Hilfiger is with Cadillac, Hugo Boss with Racing Bulls. Fashion is not testing Formula 1 anymore. It is settling in. The question is which houses get the remaining seats.
>
> **Second, the white space fits you.** Every team's technical racewear is held by sportswear brands: Puma, adidas, Castore. That is not your fight. The space next to it, what the team wears off track, how the drivers arrive, what the paddock looks like on camera, that is a luxury space, and it is exactly where Moncler lives. The paddock walk is photographed every weekend of the season. It is a runway that happens 24 times a year.
>
> **Third, the stages themselves.** Monaco. Las Vegas at night, in the desert cold, where half the paddock already wears puffer jackets. Miami. Monza, an hour from your Milan headquarters. These are the most glamorous nights in sport, and a partner owns a piece of them.
>
> One last thing on value. All of this sits on very small supply. Eleven teams for the entire world, one seat per category, deals of three to five years. Small supply, growing demand. That is why these partnerships hold their value."

### 4.5 Three activation ideas (examples, not proposals)

*[Offer these as sketches to make it concrete. Let them react; their reaction feeds the brief.]*

> "To make this concrete, let me sketch three ways a house like Moncler could show up. Early thoughts, nothing more.
>
> **One, the capsule.** A limited Moncler collection with a team, in the team's colors, launched in race week and sold through your boutiques and the team's store. You have run this play through Genius many times, and Project Mondo G shows how far it can go. Formula 1 gives it 24 global stages a year.
>
> **Two, the wardrobe.** Moncler dresses the team off track: the travel wardrobe, the arrivals, the cold races. Brioni does the formal version with Alpine. Nobody owns the luxury outerwear version yet, and the paddock in Las Vegas at midnight is the most natural Moncler picture in world sport.
>
> **Three, the clients.** A Moncler hospitality program: your top clients hosted across the season, Monaco, Monza, Las Vegas, with the paddock, the garage and the drivers as the experience. Twenty guests per weekend from seven passes. Money cannot buy the access. A Moncler client card can."

### Q1. "How are fashion and luxury brands doing this today?"

> "Three models, all live right now.
>
> **The series model.** Louis Vuitton holds the title of the Monaco Grand Prix, TAG Heuer holds Madrid, and Moet & Chandon owns the podium moment. That is luxury buying the sport's biggest stages directly.
>
> **The team wardrobe model.** Brioni dresses the Alpine team off track, a bespoke partnership built around craft, not logos. Tommy Hilfiger is doing the fashion play with Cadillac, the new American team. Hugo Boss with Racing Bulls.
>
> **The performance model.** Puma, adidas and Castore hold the technical racewear across the grid. That is volume sportswear, a different business.
>
> The reading for Moncler: the luxury seats are being taken one by one, each house choosing the model that fits its codes. The capsule, the wardrobe, the stage, or a mix. That choice is exactly what we would work through in the brief."

### Q2. "What is the difference between partnering with F1 itself, a team, or a driver?"

> "Three doors into the sport.
>
> **The series, Formula 1 itself.** You partner with the sport, not with one team. Race title rights, branding at every race, global reach, and you stay neutral. That is the Louis Vuitton route. Fewer, bigger deals at premium prices.
>
> **A team.** This is where the story lives. The car, the drivers, the wins and setbacks, the content, the hospitality. Many more entry points and price levels. The Brioni route.
>
> **A driver personally.** The face, not the team. Campaign rights, personal channels, appearance days. Fashion houses use drivers as ambassadors all the time. Simpler and cheaper, but no team rights, no car, no paddock hospitality.
>
> For a house like Moncler, the honest answer is that the right route depends on the objective: a statement, a wardrobe, or a client platform. The brief decides."

### Q3. "Teams have thirty partners. How does one brand stand out?"

> "Fair question. Three answers.
>
> First, structure. Partners sit in clear tiers, and your tier defines exactly what you own: positions, sizes, days, hospitality. It is all contractual. Nothing is first come, first served.
>
> Second, exclusivity. Within your category you are alone. No other luxury outerwear brand can sit at your team. In your world, you are not one of thirty. You are the only one.
>
> Third, and this is where fashion wins: attention follows story, not logo size. A capsule drop, a wardrobe moment, a paddock look that gets photographed, these outshine bigger logos every weekend. Moncler's whole model is built on exactly that.
>
> And remember: the hospitality and the access are private rights. They never get diluted by other partners."

### Q4. "Is Formula 1 the right image for a luxury house?"

> "Ten years ago, a fair doubt. Today the picture has changed.
>
> Monaco is the most glamorous sporting event on earth, and Louis Vuitton chose to put its name on it. The podium champagne is Moet. The paddock is photographed like a fashion week street. The drivers front campaigns for luxury houses.
>
> There is also a craft parallel. These cars are the most engineered objects in sport, built by hand, obsessed with material and performance. That is a language Moncler speaks, from the Alps to Grenoble.
>
> And the sport is cleaning its footprint: a net zero commitment for 2030, and from this season the cars run on 100% sustainable fuel.
>
> So the image question has been answered by the market. The real question is fit: which team, which model, which moments. That is ours to solve together."

### Q5. "Which team would fit Moncler?"

> "Today I do not know yet, and I would not respect you by guessing.
>
> This is exactly what the grid mapping is for. We look at each team's brand codes, its audience profile, its existing fashion relationships, its hospitality quality, and what your objectives need. Heritage, design-led, challenger energy: each team carries a different story, and the fit depends on which Moncler story you want amplified.
>
> Give us the brief today, and the recommendation comes back with names and reasons."

### Q6. "How is ROI measured in Formula 1?"

> "Four ways, usually.
>
> **Media value.** Independent firms like Nielsen track every second your branding appears, across TV and digital, and give it a money value against your fee.
>
> **Brand tracking.** Awareness, consideration and image shift among fans, measured before and after. And this audience responds: in the US, fans are 32% more likely to consider a sponsor's product, and among women it is 41%.
>
> **Commercial signals.** For a house like Moncler: capsule sell-through, traffic in race-week boutiques, growth in the markets you activate.
>
> **Client outcomes.** Who you hosted, and what those relationships produced: retention and spend of top clients after a race weekend.
>
> You agree the framework upfront and review it with the team every quarter. The good teams manage a partnership like a business."

### Q7. "Who owns Formula 1? How does the money work?"

> "Three layers, very simple.
>
> Liberty Media, an American media company, owns Formula 1 itself. They bought it in 2017. They control the commercial rights: races, broadcast deals, the calendar.
>
> The FIA is the independent regulator.
>
> The eleven teams are independent businesses: Ferrari, Mercedes and Audi belong to car makers, Red Bull to the drinks company, others to investors. Each team earns a share of the sport's revenues plus its own sponsorships.
>
> Since 2021 the sport has a cost cap, so teams became profitable, stable businesses. Team sponsorship has doubled since 2020, to 2.2 billion dollars a year. You would be entering a healthy, growing market."

### Q8. "What does a partnership cost, and why is Formula 1 so expensive?"

*[Only if they ask. Stay qualitative, ranges not quotes.]*

> "Let me explain how the pricing works, because Formula 1 has its own logic.
>
> The price follows the rights inside the deal: your tier, your branding positions, driver time, content days, hospitality volume, and the category you lock. As a rule of thumb: entry-level positions start in the low single-digit millions per year. Meaningful official partner positions sit in the mid single-digit millions. Title partnerships are a different world, well above fifty million a year.
>
> Why more expensive than other sport? Four reasons.
>
> First, supply. Football has hundreds of clubs. Formula 1 has eleven teams for the entire world, one seat per category, locked for three to five years. At most eleven doors on earth.
>
> Second, demand. Team sponsorship has doubled since 2020, and new money keeps arriving, from tech to luxury.
>
> Third, one deal covers the world: around 24 races across five continents in one contract, including every market Moncler cares about.
>
> And fourth, access. The paddock and the hospitality are what carry the price, because nobody else can offer them.
>
> This is why we start with the brief. Our job is to match the level to your goals, then negotiate it hard on your side. And our advice costs you nothing."

### Q9. "Why work with SPORTFIVE? Could we not go to a team directly?"

> "You could, and some brands do. Brands use us for two reasons.
>
> First, information. A team will sell you their own story. We know the whole grid: every team's inventory, pricing, appetite and roadmap. You see all your options next to each other.

### Q10. "Is the audience not mostly men who love cars?"

> "It was, years ago. Not anymore, and this is the whole point for Moncler.
>
> Today 42% of F1 fans are women, up from 37% in 2018. In the sport's own survey, three out of four new fans are women, and half of the Gen Z respondents are women. The average age keeps falling.
>
> This is now one of the youngest, most female, most digital audiences in major sport. That shift is exactly why fashion and beauty brands are arriving."

### Q11. "Are any of our competitors already in Formula 1?"

> "Some luxury and fashion names are in, and I named the big ones. But I will not answer the category question from memory, because this market moves weekly and you deserve an exact answer.
>
> Mapping luxury apparel and outerwear across all eleven teams is part of the grid analysis we do after this call. When we come back with recommendations, you will see precisely who is in, who is not, and where the open positions are."

### Q12. "If we create product together, how do rights, licensing and merchandise work?"

> "Very good question, and this is where fashion deals differ from a normal sponsorship.
>
> A standard partnership gives you marketing rights: the team's name, logo and imagery for your campaigns. When you create product together, we add a license on top. The license defines what you can make, the team approves the designs, and both sides agree how the revenue is shared. Co-branded pieces usually carry a royalty back to the team, or a revenue share. The exact model is negotiated deal by deal.
>
> Distribution is also agreed upfront: your boutiques and your site, the team's store and race retail, or a limited drop across both. For a house like Moncler, limited is often better. Scarcity suits you.
>
> And one thing that matters to every luxury house: you keep control of the aesthetic. The best fashion deals in this sport are design-led. Brioni with Alpine is bespoke tailoring, not logo placement. We negotiate creative control into the contract from day one.
>
> So the mechanics exist, they are standard practice in this sport, and they are exactly the details we handle for you in the negotiation."

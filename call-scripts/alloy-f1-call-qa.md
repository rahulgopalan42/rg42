# Alloy x SPORTFIVE: F1 Call, Questions and Answers

**Call:** intro call with [Name, Job title] at Alloy. From SPORTFIVE: Rahul, Bjorn and George.

---

## The intro

> "Hi [Name], thanks for making the time. I'm Rahul. I look after brand partnerships in SPORTFIVE's Formula 1 business, and Bjorn and George are with me.
>
> A bit about how we work, because it's different from what most people expect. We don't sell for one team. We look at all eleven, work out what you're trying to achieve, and then see whether any of it fits. The teams pay us, not you. And if it doesn't fit, we'll tell you."

### "So why did you reach out to us?"

> "A couple of things stood out. The first is who you sell to. Banks and payment companies are some of the biggest names in F1 right now. Mastercard is McLaren's naming partner, Visa backs both Red Bull teams, and Williams signed Barclays and BNY this year. So your customers already spend a lot of time in the paddock.
>
> The second is a trend. Over the last two years, security and identity companies have been signing with teams. Okta with McLaren, 1Password with Red Bull, Zscaler with Aston Martin this summer, ThreatLocker with Racing Bulls last month. And none of them are there for TV exposure. They're there because a team runs on data and access, and it's a very public place to show your product works.
>
> Fraud is the part of that story nobody's told yet. As far as we can tell, no fraud prevention company has done it. So it felt worth a conversation, mainly to understand where you're heading."

## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who are our competitors in F1, and which teams are available?"

> "I don't want to promise you anything on that yet, because right now it would be a guess. There are a few teams that already have a partner close to what you do. Okta at McLaren, 1Password at Red Bull, Keeper at Williams, CrowdStrike at Mercedes. And those deals usually come with exclusivity.
>
> So the first thing we do is check where there's a conflict, and rule those teams out.
>
> The second thing is understanding what you want out of it. If it's mainly about meeting banks, that can point you to different teams than if it's about showing the product working. Then we come back with the teams where it fits.
>
> One thing worth knowing: fraud prevention isn't the same as cybersecurity, and these contracts are often written quite narrowly. So a team with a security partner isn't automatically off the table.
>
> To give you an idea of what a partner like you does once they're in, look at Okta at McLaren. It's not just a logo. McLaren launched a new fan club this year, and every fan who logs in to the app, the website or the shop goes through Okta. On a race weekend that's a lot of people logging in at once, and part of Okta's job is protecting them from identity fraud. Okta says more than fifty of McLaren's sponsors use the same system to book their hospitality. So Okta gets to say that the team that won the last two championships trusts it with its fans and its partners. That's a much stronger story than a sticker on the car."

### "Why are these brands in F1?"

> "Two different reasons, depending on the company.
>
> The security companies are there to prove something. An F1 team has its car designs, race strategy and live data moving between the factory and a different country most race weekends. If that's safe with you, a bank's data probably is too. CrowdStrike's CEO put it simply: it's a very data-heavy sport.
>
> The banks and payment companies are there for their customers. Barclays, Visa and Standard Chartered all use the same word when they talk about it: clients. A race weekend is three days with your most important customers, somewhere they really want to be.
>
> For a company like Alloy, I'd guess it's a bit of both."

### "What are companies like us doing in F1?"

> "Most of them have a real job inside the team, which is what makes them worth talking about. A few examples."

- **1Password at Red Bull.** When a Red Bull engineer signs in to live car data at the track or the factory, it goes through 1Password. It's their first F1 deal, and they put the logo on the drivers' steering wheel screens. What it tells you: one clear job can be enough.
- **Keeper Security at Williams.** When someone at Williams needs extra access on their laptop, Keeper gives it and then takes it away again. It started in 2024, renewed early, and this year ran a campaign with Alex Albon. What it tells you: you can start at a midfield team and grow.
- **CrowdStrike at Mercedes.** It protects thousands of the team's computers, and it's been there since 2019. What people remember is a video series where Mercedes engineers explained the halo, the helmet and the fire extinguisher. What it tells you: the sport is the story, not the software.
- **Bitdefender at Ferrari.** It feeds threat data straight into Ferrari's security team, and it hosts its own customers at private events with Ferrari drivers. In May it published research on scams aimed at F1 fans. What it tells you: the fan fraud angle has started to get attention.
- **Airwallex at McLaren.** Its payments run inside McLaren's finance team, and McLaren has just given it a bigger role from 2027. What it tells you: a real job leads to a longer relationship.
- **Mastercard at McLaren.** It takes small groups of cardholders inside the McLaren garage at five races. What it tells you: the big payment companies use F1 for their customers, and their customers are often banks.

### "It's very crowded. Won't we get lost?"

> "It's a real risk. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and a lot of them are just logos. The ones that get noticed do one of two things.
>
> Some give their product a real job. Airwallex at McLaren is a good example. They're next to Mastercard and Goldman Sachs, so they'd never win on logo size. Instead, their payments run inside McLaren's finance team. That gave them something true to say, and McLaren has just given them a bigger role.
>
> Others make something people want to watch. CrowdStrike at Mercedes did a video series where the team's engineers explained the halo, the helmet and the fire extinguisher, and how each one keeps a driver safe. It wasn't about software at all, and people watched it.
>
> For you, the obvious version is fraud. F1's own ticket site warns fans about fake tickets. Teams have fan clubs, shops and ticket ballots now, and keeping those real is a problem you solve."

### "Why would a team want a partner like us?"

> "Teams aren't short of technology partners. Tech is now the biggest sponsorship category in F1, and Reuters counted eight new AI deals in six months earlier this year.
>
> What's harder to find is a partner that solves a problem the team has. Teams run proper consumer businesses now: online shops, fan clubs, ticket ballots. Keeping fake accounts and fraud out of those is a real problem, and it isn't what the cybersecurity partners do. They protect the team's own systems.
>
> Whether there's room for that at a given team is what we'd check first."

### "What would a first deal look like for a company our size?"

> "Usually smaller than people think. Most companies your size start with one team, one clear role, some branding, a few race weekends with guests, and a bit of content, over two or three years. Keeper started like that at Williams and renewed early.
>
> Some start even smaller, with two or three race weekends hosting customers, to see whether it works for them before going further."

### "What does it cost?"

> "It depends a lot on the team and what's included. A supplier or entry-level partner deal starts in the low single-digit millions a year. Hosting customers at a few races, without a team deal, costs a lot less. I'd rather understand what you want first, then show you options at different levels."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

> "You can go to a team directly. They'll tell you about their own team, which is fair enough. We worked only with Mercedes and Ferrari for over twelve years, and then stopped, so we could look at all eleven teams for the brand. The teams pay our fee, so having us compare them costs you nothing."

### "Which team would fit us?"

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. It depends on what you want out of it, where your customers are, and where there's no conflict. That's what we'd come back to you with."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "We'd agree that before anything's signed. For a company that sells to banks, the real measure is usually business: which customers you spent time with, and what came of it. TV exposure matters a lot less for you than it would for a consumer brand."

### "Mastercard's our partner, and it's at McLaren. Does that help?"

> "Possibly. It could help with an introduction at McLaren. But Mastercard sells fraud tools too, so its contract might already cover your area. We'd check that before saying anything to anyone."

### "We sell to banks, not fans. Isn't F1 a consumer thing?"

> "The fans are consumers, but the paddock is mostly business. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives, and the banks on the grid are there for their clients, not for fans. If it doesn't make sense for a company your size, I'll tell you."

## The intro

> "Hi [Name], thanks for making the time. I'm Rahul. I look after brand partnerships in SPORTFIVE's Formula 1 business, and Bjorn and George are with me.
>
> A bit about how we work, because it's different from what most people expect. We don't sell for one team. We look at all eleven, work out what you're trying to achieve, and then see whether any of it fits. The teams pay us, not you. And if it doesn't fit, we'll tell you."

### "So why did you reach out to us?"

> "Two simple reasons. The first is who your members are. People with good incomes who want more than stocks. Many of them are already F1 fans. F1 fans are joint first of any sport for high income. And in the US, fans are about a third more likely to consider buying from a brand in the sport. Carlyle, one of your own fund partners, signed with Red Bull last year to reach the same kind of investor.
>
> The second is what you do. You help people spread their money across real estate, private credit and private equity, so no single bet decides everything. An F1 team thinks the same way. Before every race, the strategists plan for rain, a safety car and worn tyres. Then they make the call without knowing what happens next.
>
> And with the new name, the new funds and two senior hires this year, it felt like a good time to talk. But I'd like to hear from you first. Where are things heading?"

## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who watches F1? Is it our audience?"

> "More of it than you'd think. Most of what you offer is for accredited investors: people earning over $200,000 a year, or worth over a million dollars outside their home. F1 fans index at 131 for high income, where 100 is the general population. That's joint first of any sport. They also rank first for buying the premium version of things, and first for researching online before they buy. That's how people choose where to invest.
>
> The US is where it's growing. Apple broadcasts the sport there, there are three American races and two American teams. And US fans are 32% more likely to consider buying from a brand that's in the sport.
>
> It's also young. 43% of fans are under 35. Those are people building wealth now, and many of them want more than stocks."

### "Some of the people who matter to us are fund partners and advisers, not fans. Who's in the paddock?"

> "Mostly business people. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. There are more than 350 partner companies across the grid, and every one of them brings guests.
>
> What I'd point out for you is how many of them manage money. Carlyle is at Red Bull, Goldman Sachs at McLaren and UBS at Mercedes. Nuveen, BNY and Barclays all signed with Williams this year. And they bring financial advisers with them, the way Carlyle does at Red Bull. Teams also introduce their partners to each other, so the people your guests meet are often people they'd want to meet anyway.
>
> One thing to plan for. If your guests are advisers, their firms will have rules on gifts and entertainment. We'd build around that from the start."

### "How are similar brands activating, and what are they getting back?"

> "Let me give you six. I'll start with two of your own fund partners, then the investing apps, then the money managers who arrived this year."

**Carlyle at Red Bull**

> "Carlyle has been with Red Bull since September last year, as its exclusive investment management partner. It's the first big private markets firm to partner with an F1 team. Its CEO, Harvey Schwartz, pointed to 'greater access to private markets and growing interest from a new generation of investors.' In June he put it more simply: 'F1 was a natural fit.'
>
> Through the deal, its name is on the cars, the drivers' kit and the pit wall. And it uses the team to meet the people who sell its funds. Bloomberg reported that its head of wealth took a group of financial advisers to the 2026 car launch in Detroit, to meet Max Verstappen.
>
> No return is published. But Schwartz was still talking it up at Monaco in June. And Carlyle is the closest thing to you on the grid. Its private credit fund is on your platform."

**Goldman Sachs at McLaren**

> "Goldman Sachs has been with McLaren since 2022. Its marketing chief, Fiona Carter, said the two 'share an ambition to accelerate progress for the fans, our clients and in our communities.'
>
> Through the deal, its name went on the cars from that year's British Grand Prix, and it took on a role in McLaren's net zero plans.
>
> No return is published. The signal is time: it's still on McLaren's partner list in its fifth season. And its real estate income fund is one of the three you added in December."

**Public at Aston Martin**

> "Public, the US investing app, has been with Aston Martin since April last year, as the team's official US investment platform. Its co-founder, Leif Abraham, said 'Public is designed for those who take investing seriously.' He called F1 'the perfect arena to showcase what it means to operate at the highest level.'
>
> Through the deal, its name went on the car only at the three US races: Miami, Austin and Las Vegas. Inside the Public app, it hid three Aston Martin cars. Users who found all three were entered to win a VIP weekend in Las Vegas. And the team took a stake in Public.
>
> No return is published. But Public is still on the team's partner list this season. And it's a deal built around the US races and the app, which is very close to what you'd want."

**eToro at Alpine**

> "eToro, the trading and investing app, has been with Alpine since January, as the team's exclusive trading and investment partner. It was eToro's first step into F1. Its CEO, Yoni Assia, said 'Formula One is driven by innovation and a relentless commitment to improvement.' The plan was 'inspiring content and experiences for fans worldwide.'
>
> Through the deal, it makes content and fan experiences. In August, it also signed Pierre Gasly, one of Alpine's drivers, for live events, social media and podcast appearances. Gasly's line: 'Success in Formula One is about preparation, continuous learning and staying committed to your goals.'
>
> It's too early for results, and none are published. The signal is that within seven months, eToro added the driver. A partner that grows its role that fast is usually happy with it."

**Kraken at Williams**

> "Kraken, the crypto app, has been with Williams since 2023. In January it renewed for a fourth year and moved to the front wing. The team principal, James Vowles, said Kraken 'has been instrumental in helping Williams bring our passionate fans closer to the team.'
>
> Through the deal, Kraken presents the team's Fan Zones around the world, where fans meet drivers and try simulators. Every year, fans help design Kraken's branding on the rear wing. And registered fans get perks through a digital pass.
>
> No return is published. The signals are the renewal and the better spot on the car. And on Kraken's side, the renewal was announced by its chief growth and marketing officer. So it's a growth team that keeps paying for it."

**Nuveen, BNY and Barclays at Williams**

> "All three signed with Williams this winter. Nuveen, a big manager of real estate and private credit among other things, joined in January as official asset management partner. BNY joined the same month. Barclays came in February, its first time in F1, and said the deal 'will deepen our client engagement and elevate our brand across our key markets.'
>
> Through the deals, all three are on the car. Barclays runs programmes for its private banking and wealth clients. BNY's plan is to bring financial audiences together in 'key financial hubs around the world.'
>
> It's too early for results, and none are published. But three big money names choosing one team in a single winter tells you how much they value time with clients at a race."

**What that adds up to**

> "So the honest picture is this. None of these brands publishes numbers from F1. What they show is that they stay and they grow: Goldman Sachs in its fifth season, Kraken renewed for a fourth, eToro signing a driver within months. And more money managers keep arriving.
>
> For you, I'd take three things from that. First, start with the US, the way Public did. The three American races, plus something your members can do in the app, which your team can track. Second, use content to explain how you think, the way eToro does with Gasly, inside your compliance rules. Third, use hosting for the people who bring you business: advisers, fund partners and your best members, the way Carlyle does."

### "Content is really important for us. How would a team help us make it?"

> "It's one of the most useful parts of a deal. For a brand that's just changed its name, it may matter more than the logo.
>
> Most deals come with a set number of filming days each year with the drivers and the team's people, and the team's content people help you make it. The team's own channels matter too. F1 is the number one sport on social media, ahead of the NBA, and 61% of fans engage with its content every day.
>
> One thing to know: the race footage is controlled by Formula 1 itself, not the teams. So partner content is filmed around the race: at the factory, in the garage, or on filming days.
>
> The best partner content usually isn't about the product. CrowdStrike made a series with Mercedes where the team's specialists explain the safety kit on the car, from the halo to the helmet, and Mercedes says it got 6.2 million views. eToro now has Pierre Gasly doing podcasts and live events.
>
> For you, I'd build it around decisions under uncertainty. Before a race, a team strategist shows the plans they've prepared: what they'll do if it rains, if there's a safety car, if the tyres wear faster. After the race, they explain which plan they used and why. Sometimes a good call loses and a bad call wins. So they judge the decision, not just the result, and they never bet the race on one plan. That's the conversation your members have about their money. No product, no returns, and no driver recommending anything. It also cuts well into short clips, where fans pick the call before they see what the team did.
>
> Your compliance team would check every film. And if a driver is paid to talk about Willow, that counts as an endorsement, with the disclosures that needs.
>
> If content matters to you, we'd spell it out in the deal: how many days, which drivers and team people, what the team posts on its own channels, and how long and where you can use it."

### "Why are these brands in F1?"

> "Two reasons, mostly.
>
> The first is reaching a new kind of investor. Private markets used to be for pension funds and the very rich. Now the big managers want well-off individuals too, and F1's fans are young and earn well. Carlyle's CEO talked about 'a new generation of investors.' Your CEO said something very close in December: 'Private markets are undergoing a shift in how they're accessed and who can access them.'
>
> The second is time with the people who bring them business. For Carlyle and Barclays, that's advisers and clients. A race weekend is three days with them, somewhere they really want to be.
>
> For Willow, I'd guess it's mostly the first, with some of the second. You want the new name known by the right people. And your fund partners and advisers are the kind of guests the paddock is built for."

### "Who are our competitors in F1, and which teams are available?"

> "I don't want to promise you anything on that yet, because right now it would be a guess. As far as we can tell, no platform like yours has a deal with a team.
>
> But a lot of teams have partners close to what you do. Carlyle is Red Bull's exclusive investment management partner. eToro is Alpine's exclusive trading and investment partner. Public is Aston Martin's official US investment platform. Goldman Sachs is at McLaren, UBS at Mercedes, and Revolut, the banking app, is Audi's title partner. Williams has Nuveen, BNY, Barclays and Kraken. These deals usually come with exclusivity, and some are written broadly.
>
> So the first thing we do is check where there's a conflict, and rule those teams out. Red Bull, for one, is very likely out because of Carlyle.
>
> The second thing is understanding what you want out of it. If it's mainly new members through the app, that can point you to different options than if it's meeting advisers and fund partners. Then we come back with what fits.
>
> To give you an idea of what a partner like you does once they're in, look at Public at Aston Martin. It didn't buy the whole season. Its name went on the car at the three US races, where its customers are. It hid the team's cars inside its own app, and users who found them all could win a weekend in Las Vegas. So the deal gave people a reason to open the app during race season. That's more useful than a logo on its own."

### "It's very crowded. Won't we get lost?"

> "It's a real risk. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and money brands are some of the busiest on the grid. You'd never win on logo size next to Goldman Sachs or Barclays.
>
> But you don't need to. Public didn't try. It focused on three US races and its own app. You could do the same. Concentrate on Miami, Austin and Las Vegas, where your members are. Then give them a reason to take part, not just to look.
>
> And F1 fans stick with brands. They index at 200 for brand loyalty, first of any sport. So the work is getting noticed by the right people once."

### "Why would a team want a partner like us?"

> "Teams aren't short of finance partners, so it has to be something else.
>
> You bring more than 500,000 members in the US, the market every team wants to grow in. They're mostly well-off people who invest for the long term. That's an audience a team would like to reach.
>
> You also bring people who know how to grow a consumer finance brand. Mitch ran E-Trade, and he and Larry built Telebanc, one of the first online banks in the US. A team's commercial people would enjoy that conversation.
>
> And there's a story the team can relate to. Every race is a set of decisions made without knowing what happens next. So is investing.
>
> Whether a team has room, and the appetite, is what we'd check first."

### "What would a first deal look like for a company our size?"

> "I'll be straight with you: a full team partnership is a big step for a company your size, and I wouldn't start there. There are smaller ways in.
>
> The first is hosting. A few US race weekends with your best members, advisers and fund partners, to see whether it works. Passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend. Miami, Austin and Las Vegas are the obvious ones.
>
> The second is a US-only deal, like Public's at Aston Martin: branding at the three American races, and something for members in the app.
>
> The third is a driver deal or a content deal, like eToro's with Gasly, which costs less than a team. And there's F1 Academy, the women's series, which Standard Chartered backs. It runs on F1 race weekends and costs less too.
>
> If one of those works, a bigger team deal can come later."

### "What does it cost?"

> "It depends a lot on the team and what's included. A supplier or entry-level partner deal starts in the low single-digit millions a year. Official partner deals sit above that. Hosting at a few races, a driver deal or a content deal costs a lot less. For a company your size, I'd start at the lower end and show you options there first."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

> "You can go to a team directly. They'll tell you about their own team, which is fair enough. We worked only with Mercedes and Ferrari for over twelve years, and then stopped, so we could look at all eleven teams for the brand. The teams pay our fee, so having us compare them costs you nothing. And for you, someone has to check each team's partners and appetite quietly, before your name goes anywhere. That's better done by us than by a round of calls from you."

### "Which team would fit us?"

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. It depends on what you want out of it, whether that's members, advisers or getting the new name known. It also depends on where there's no conflict, and which teams are comfortable. That's what we'd come back to you with."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "We'd agree that before anything's signed. For you I'd look at three things. Members: new sign-ups and funded accounts from F1 campaigns around the US races, tracked through links and codes. The name: how many more people know Willow Wealth and search for it, measured before and after. And people: which advisers and fund partners you hosted, and what came of it.
>
> It can show up in the numbers. Airwallex says its revenue grew 50% year on year across Europe, the Middle East, Africa and the Americas during its McLaren partnership, and 57% in the UK from new customers."

### "We've had a difficult few years as Yieldstreet. Will a team even work with us?"

> "Fair question, and I'd rather be straight with you. Teams run background checks on every partner, and the history will come up: the losses on some older deals, the SEC settlement and the investor lawsuits. A team will ask about it, and it may ask its other partners too. Some teams will say no. And when a deal is announced, reporters may bring the history up again, so we'd plan for that.
>
> What helps is what's changed. There's a new name and a new direction, with funds from big managers like Carlyle, Goldman Sachs and StepStone. You have new leaders who've run big financial businesses before: Mitch at E-Trade, Larry with Telebanc, and Tom from Morgan Stanley. And two of your fund partners are on the grid themselves.
>
> So we'd check team appetite quietly first, before your name goes to anyone. I won't promise anything. Where a team isn't comfortable, we'll tell you, rather than waste your time."

### "We sell investments. Are there rules on what we can say through F1?"

> "Yes, but most of them are yours, not F1's. As far as we can tell, neither F1 nor any team publishes rules on investment sponsors. Each team does its own checks. And a few countries have their own advertising rules. Teams covered up crypto logos at the French Grand Prix a few years ago.
>
> The bigger rules come from your side. Your adviser is registered with the SEC, so the SEC's marketing rule covers your ads. Your broker-dealer is a FINRA member, so FINRA's rules on communications apply. In plain terms, I'd expect three limits. First, the brand and the stories are one thing, but a specific offering has limits on who you can advertise it to. Second, no promises about returns, and nothing that suggests a fast car means a good investment. Third, if a driver is paid to talk about Willow, that's an endorsement. It needs clear disclosure, a written agreement, and your team checking what's said. FINRA fined M1 Finance $850,000 over paid influencers' posts it hadn't checked.
>
> Two more things. You only serve US investors, but F1 content goes around the world. And if your guests are advisers, their firms have rules on hospitality. I'm not your lawyer, so please check all of this with your compliance team. We'd build their review time into the plan."

### "Our marketing is performance-led. How do we measure a brand deal?"

> "I'll be straight: a team deal won't behave like paid social. Most of the value comes more slowly. People hear the name, see it next to a team they like, and trust it more when your ads find them later.
>
> But you can build pieces you can measure into it from day one. Public's hunt inside the app is one: everyone who took part is someone you can count. Race-weekend landing pages and codes are another. You can compare sign-ups around Miami, Austin and Las Vegas with cities that didn't get the push. And you can track searches for 'Willow Wealth' before and after.
>
> F1 fans help here. They rank first of any sport for researching online before they buy. So the deal makes people curious, and your performance team catches them when they search. We'd agree the targets with you before anything's signed."

### "We're watching every dollar. Is this the right time?"

> "It might not be. Your last raise was in July last year, and your investors will want anything new tied to growth, not just to being seen. If F1 brings in funded members in the US and gets the new name known, that's easier to justify than a logo.
>
> So we'd start from your plan for the next two years and work back. If the answer is a couple of US race weekends first, that's fine. If it's 'not yet', we'll tell you."

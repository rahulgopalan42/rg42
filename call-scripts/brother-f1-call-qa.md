From what I can see, the group is run from Nagoya, Brother International Corporation in New Jersey looks after the Americas, and the European head office is in Manchester. 2. "Your medium-term plan, CS B2027, says the industrial side should reach 40% of sales. It was 32% last year. The plan names machine tools, industrial printing and commercial and industrial labelling as the growth businesses, and says office and home printing is there to protect profits. 3. "Printing and Solutions was about 62% of first-half revenue. Personal and Home was about 6%. But most people know the name from home printers, label makers and sewing machines. 4. 5. "Where does the brand most need help in the next two or three years? Your results show the Americas as your biggest region in printing, and Europe as weaker. Machine tools sell mostly in Asia. Your plan lists China, India and Japan as priority regions for them, and has added Europe and Southeast Asia."
6. "How much of your business goes through partners: resellers, retailers and dealers? One of this year's business inkjet models is sold only through your Gold Authorized Partners. Do you run events or reward schemes for them now? Would you host them at a race?"

8. "Which name would lead? You have Brother, Domino and now MUTOH. Would a partnership carry the Brother name only, or would Domino and MUTOH use it with their own customers?"
9. "Brother has a long history in sport: Manchester City from 1987 to 1999, Formula 1 around the turn of the century, cycling in the UK, and the Asian Games in Nagoya this autumn. What did the business take from those? Would you do anything differently?"
10. "If you had a team partnership, which parts would you use most? Hosting customers and dealers at races? A real job inside the team that you can talk about? Content? Promotions through your retailers? Or your name on the car?"
11. "How do you judge marketing now? On the print side I'd guess awareness, and sales through your partners. On the industrial side, leads and orders. You were at NRF in New York in January. What does a good event or campaign look like in numbers?"
12. "What's the biggest risk for you in doing something like this? What would make it go wrong? Your results talk about dearer parts and materials, tariffs and a print market that is shrinking overall."

## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who watches F1? Is it our audience?"

> "More of it than you'd expect, and for you it works at two levels.
>
> For your business buyers: F1 fans index at 160 for business decision makers, where 100 is the general population. That's the highest of any sport. In plain terms, the person who chooses a coding line, a label system or a fleet of printers is much more likely than average to follow F1.
>
> For small offices and homes: fans index at 126 for researching online before they buy, and at 200 for brand loyalty. Both are the highest in sport. Loyalty matters for a business that lives on ink, toner and labels. Your own results show consumables at more than half of printing revenue.
>
> And it reaches your markets. There are 827 million fans, and 43% are under 35. There are more than 20 race weekends on five continents. The US has three races and two American teams, and Apple shows the sport there."

[Rahul: the consumables figure is from Brother's results slides. The consumable ratio in Communications and Printing Equipment was 58% in the April to June quarter and 54% in July to September.]

### "We sell to businesses and through dealers, not fans. Who's in the paddock?"

> "Mostly business people. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. There are more than 350 partner companies across the grid, 25 or more at most teams, and every one of them brings guests.
>
> What I'd point out for you is how many come from the industries you sell to. In food and drink, PepsiCo is with Mercedes, Heineken is with Red Bull and Barilla is with Racing Bulls. In logistics, DP World is with McLaren and CEVA Logistics is with Ferrari. In retail, M&S is with Williams and Mercado Libre is with Alpine. I'm not saying they are your customers. You'd know that better than I do.
>
> Then there are your dealers and resellers. Xerox sells through partners, as you do. Its marketing chief, Darren Cassidy, said this year: 'We also bring our channel partners.' And: 'We're an organisation that goes to market through partnerships and other organisations.'
>
> A sales meeting is half an hour. A race weekend is three days. And passes work on all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend."

**Xerox at Aston Martin**

> "Xerox has been with Aston Martin since the 2025 season, as an Official Global Partner on a multi-year deal.
>
> Its marketing chief, Darren Cassidy, gave the reason in May: 'Frankly, we're here for the audience.' He said younger people don't know the brand, and added: 'So, for us, this is about reaching that under-30 audience in a more relevant way.'
>

Cassidy said: 'We're doing four marquee events this season, in Melbourne, Montréal, Silverstone and Austin, with a major hospitality offering for our clients.' ### "Why are technology and industrial companies in F1?"

> "Two reasons, mostly.
>
> The first is proof. A team is a manufacturer that works to very short deadlines, and teams have had a cost cap since 2021. So when a team relies on a company's equipment, buyers believe it will work for them.
>
> The second is people: three days with the executives they want to sell to. That is what Xerox built its plan on.
>
> It's also where the money is going. Team sponsorship has doubled since 2020, to 2.2 billion dollars. Reuters reported in May that technology is now the biggest area of team sponsor spending.
>
> Machine tool builders have been there for years. DMG MORI is with Red Bull. Camozzi is with Audi. And the owner of Haas Automation runs his own team.
>
> For Brother, I'd add the reason Xerox gave. Younger people don't know the name as well as their parents did, and 43% of F1 fans are under 35."

### "What rights would we get, and which matter most for a company like ours?"

> "A partnership is a bundle of rights. You sell mostly to businesses, but you also make things people buy for the home, so I'd rank them like this.
>
> The first is hosting. Paddock Club passes at the races you pick, garage tours and factory visits, for industrial customers, dealers and resellers. Seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend. This is where Xerox says its return comes from.
>
> The second is a real job inside the team, and the right to talk about it: a case study, a named person from the team, a line in your sales material. It can't be promised, because the team's own people decide. Where it's possible, it's worth a lot.
>
> The third is introductions to the team's other partners. The grid has food and drink makers, logistics firms and retailers on it. I'd write a number into the contract: so many introductions, and a seat at the partner events.
>
> The fourth is the title, and the team's name and logo, in your own marketing: packaging, trade show stands, and the material your resellers and retailers use.
>
> The fifth is content, with engineers and operations people as much as drivers.
>
> The sixth is promotions and licensed products for consumers: prize draws through retailers, days out for dealers' best customers, and designs or a special edition. I'll explain licensing next.
>
> The seventh is branding on the car, the kit and the garage. For the industrial businesses I'd put that last. If the main job is making Brother better known to small offices and younger buyers, it moves up.
>
> Two more things set the price. One is exclusivity: how widely your field is defined, and who else the team may sign. The other is territory: the world, or a few markets. Given how Brother is organised, rights for one region may suit you better than global ones.
>
> Whatever you take goes into the contract with numbers: passes, days, introductions, markets and years."

### "Could we sell team or F1 branded products or designs? How does licensing work?"

> "In principle, yes, and you already know how.
>
> A sponsorship lets you link your name to a team in your marketing. A licence lets you make and sell something that carries the team's name and marks. You pay the team a share of sales, called a royalty, and usually a guaranteed minimum.
>
> You do this with Disney. Your Stellaire embroidery machine has 101 Disney designs built in, and your Disney ScanNCut has 182. You sell more Disney designs through your download store. Team logos and car designs as embroidery or cutting designs would be the same thing with a new rights owner.
>
> I can see three forms. One: designs, as I said. Two: a special edition of a product. HP sells a numbered Ferrari laptop, and DEWALT sold McLaren tools through one retailer. Yours could be a label maker, a sewing machine or a printer. Three: personalising things for fans, with your embroidery machines and garment printers.
>
> A licence sets five things: which products, which countries, how long, the royalty and minimum, and how designs get approved. The team signs off designs and packaging, so plan in months, not weeks.
>
> Three cautions. The team can only license its own name. The Formula 1 name is a separate deal with Formula 1, and a driver's name or face needs the driver. Second, clothing. adidas is Audi's official apparel partner and makes its teamwear and merchandise, and other teams have kit partners too. Anything stitched or printed onto clothes could cut across them. Third, a guaranteed minimum is owed even if the product sells badly, so I'd start small.
>
> And selling at the track is restricted. Formula 1 said in May 2023 that 'PUMA subsidiary stichd will have exclusive trackside vending rights to sell Formula 1 licensed products'. So a personalising stand inside a circuit is not something you can simply set up. It would work better in a dealer's shop in the host city, in the team's hospitality, or at your own events."

[Rahul: the Disney figures are from Brother USA's product pages for the Stellaire2 XE2 and the Disney ScanNCut DX SDX330D. I could not confirm who runs Formula 1's online store, so I left that channel out. I found no F1 team with a licensed sewing, embroidery, craft or label product.]

### "What are the different ways in, and what would a first deal look like for us?"

> "There are five ways in, and I'd start with one of the smaller ones.
>
> The first is hosting only. Pick a few races in your markets and build a programme for customers and dealers. Next year Suzuka is 9 to 11 April. Miami is 30 April to 2 May. Silverstone is 2 to 4 July. Austin is 22 to 24 October, and Las Vegas is 18 to 20 November.
>
> The second is content: a series with a team or a driver, with some hosting around it.
>
> The third is a supplier deal with one team: a title, a small job, some hosting and a little branding. That is roughly where Roland DG is.
>
> The fourth is a full team partnership, closer to what Xerox has.
>
> The fifth is a licence: one set of designs or one special edition, with one team, in one or two markets.
>
> A typical first team deal for a company that sells mostly to businesses is one team, one clear role, a few race weekends with customers, some content and some branding, over two or three years. But you don't have to begin there. One region could start with hosting or a licence, see what it returns, and build from that."

[Rahul: next year's calendar was published on 16 September 2026. Other dates near Brother sites: Melbourne 2 to 4 April, Shanghai 16 to 18 April, Montreal 21 to 23 May, Monza 3 to 5 September, Madrid 10 to 12 September, Singapore 8 to 10 October, Mexico City 29 to 31 October. Suzuka is a Sprint weekend.]

### "What does it cost?"

> "It depends a lot on the team and what's included. A supplier or entry-level partner deal starts in the low single-digit millions a year. Official partner deals sit above that. Hosting customers at a few races, a content deal or a driver deal costs a lot less. A licence works differently: a royalty on what you sell, and a guaranteed minimum. I'd rather understand what you want first, then show you options at different levels."

### "We're a Japanese company. Do Japan and Suzuka matter?"

> "They could, and more now than a few years ago.
>
> Honda came back this season as the works engine partner of Aston Martin. Toyota Gazoo Racing became the title partner of Haas, which now races as TGR Haas F1 Team. Komatsu renewed with Williams in September, at a signing in Komatsu City.
>
> Suzuka is close to home for you. Next year's race is 9 to 11 April, and it's a Sprint weekend. The circuit says the train from Nagoya to Shiroko takes about 50 minutes, and the bus from there about 20. For customers, dealers or your own board, that's an easy first race.
>
> I'd be careful with drivers. No Japanese driver has a full-time race seat this season. Yuki Tsunoda is Red Bull's reserve. Ryo Hirakawa is the reserve at Haas. I haven't seen a Japanese driver confirmed in a race seat for next year.
>
> And if the sign-off is in Nagoya, a Japanese link may help the talk inside the company. I wouldn't pick a team for that reason alone."

[Rahul: Honda and Toyota are from Honda's own page (10 February 2026) and the Haas release (4 December 2025). Tsunoda's reserve role is from Motorsport.com (4 December 2025). ### "We were in F1 before, with Jordan. What has changed?"

> "A great deal. Brother was with Tyrrell and then Williams at the end of the 1990s, and on the Jordan cars from 2000 to 2003. Then, a deal was built around the name on the car and the drivers' overalls.
>
> The audience is different now. There are 827 million fans, up 63% since 2018. 42% are women, up from 37% in 2018. And 43% are under 35.
>
> The US is a real market for the sport now: three races, two American teams, and Apple as the broadcaster.
>
> The teams are steadier businesses. They've had a cost cap since 2021, and team sponsorship has doubled since 2020.
>
> And the deals are different. The ones that last have a job, a hosting plan and content behind them.
>
> One small thing. The team that raced as Jordan is now Aston Martin, several owners later."

[Rahul: Formula 1's own "family tree" article (1 March 2026) says: "What is now Lawrence Stroll's team started life as Jordan in 1991, founded by former racing driver Eddie Jordan." Takuma Sato drove for Jordan in 2002, when Brother was on the car.]

### "Our decisions are split between Nagoya and the regions. Can a deal be regional?"

> "Yes, it can. Territory is one of the two things that set the price. The other is exclusivity.
>
> Rights for Europe only, or for the Americas only, cost less than rights for the world. Some teams have regional titles.
>
> There are limits. The car is seen everywhere, so branding on the car is hard to limit to one region. Where regional rights work is in using the team's name and marks, the content, the promotions and the hosting.
>
> So one region could start with rights for its own markets and a few races, with an option to widen it if other regions or Nagoya want to join. The one thing to settle at the start is who owns it inside Brother."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

> "You can go to a team directly. They'll tell you about their own team, which is fair enough. We look at all eleven teams and compare them for you, including how each one is priced, so you know what a fair price is. The teams pay our fee, so that costs you nothing. We also work across a lot of sports, so we can say plainly when F1 isn't the best fit.
>
> For you there's a second reason. HP, Xerox and Roland DG are at three teams, machine tool builders are at four, and licences are often handled apart from sponsorship. Someone has to check each team quietly before your name goes anywhere.
>
> We've done this for a technology supplier before. We ran a process across the grid for RUCKUS Networks, and it's now the official networking partner of Haas."

[Rahul: RUCKUS is from our stat bank: enterprise wireless, shown live in the garage, and we ran the process across the grid. Its logo is on the Haas partner page. Check how much of our part you can share.]

### "Which team would fit us?"

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. It depends on what the job is: hosting industrial customers and dealers, making Brother better known to small offices, a real role and a case study, or a product. It depends on which markets matter most, and on which business leads, because the partners already in place differ for office print, wide-format, labelling and machine tools. And it depends on where there's no conflict. Ferrari has HP, Aston Martin has Xerox and Alpine has Roland DG. It may also be that the first step isn't a team at all. That's the kind of thing we'd come back to you with."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "We'd agree that before anything's signed, and I'd use numbers you already track. For you I'd look at four things.
>
> The people you host: who came, what orders followed, and what you can fairly put down to the weekend. That is how Xerox gets to its figure. Darren Cassidy said: 'we monitor the people there, the prospects, the pipeline that we create, and the deals that we generate.'
>
> Sales through your partners: run a promotion with resellers or retailers, and compare those markets and weeks with ones that had none.
>
> How well you're known: awareness and consideration among small office and home buyers, above all younger ones, in the markets where you use the partnership.
>
> And a licensed product, if there is one: units sold, and designs downloaded.
>
> It can show up in the numbers. Airwallex says its revenue grew 50% year on year in its Europe and Americas regions during its McLaren partnership, and 57% in the UK from new customers. One caveat: the sponsorship alone didn't drive that. But it shows the kind of result a company tracks."

### "What does SPORTFIVE do outside Formula 1?"

> "A lot, and most of it isn't motorsport: football, tennis, hockey and more, from over 60 offices in more than 20 countries. In football, we've worked with Borussia Dortmund since 1999, and that was recently extended to 2034. We find their sponsors, look after them once they're signed, and sell the hospitality at the stadium. In tennis, we're the Australian Open's lead agency for partners in Europe. And our business intelligence team backs it up with research when a deal is planned, and again when it's reviewed."

[Rahul: check these examples with colleagues before the call.]

### "What's the biggest risk?"

> "Signing without knowing which part of Brother it is for. Most teams have 25 or more partners. The ones that get lost put a name on the car and stop there. A deal needs a clear plan for the people you'll host or the product you'll sell.
>
> The second risk is ownership. If one region or one business signs and the others don't use it, you pay for rights that sit unused. So settle who owns it first.
>
> The third is a conflict you didn't see. Your rivals are at Ferrari and Aston Martin, Roland DG is at Alpine, and four teams have machine tool builders. Contracts often cover more than the title says.
>
> The fourth is building the plan on a team using your products. The team's own people decide that, and no contract can promise it.
>
> The fifth is picking the wrong tool for the job. If the job is selling more home printers as cheaply as possible, shop promotions and online adverts probably do that better. If it's time with industrial buyers and dealers, and being known by younger buyers in Europe and North America, F1 is hard to beat. We work across a lot of sports, so we'd rather tell you that up front than sell you the wrong thing."

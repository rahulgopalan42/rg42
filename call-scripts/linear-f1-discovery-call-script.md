Goal: open warm and short, say why Linear caught our eye, make the goal plain (understand if it makes sense, not sell), let them talk, fill the brief with sharp questions, and show how this sport could fit a craft-obsessed software company. Agree next steps only if the conversation earns them.

**How to use this script:** Every spoken block is written the way you'd say it out loud, contractions and all. Don't read it word for word; glance at the bold anchors and talk. This audience built the most design-obsessed tool in software. They'll notice sloppy thinking instantly and they're allergic to marketing speak, so keep it precise, plain and short. Let them talk more than you.

Around $134M raised in primary funding overall; the tender was employee liquidity, not new capital.

### Flags

The exact team-by-team picture is verified at the recommendations stage.

- **Names check:** citations in this script (Atlassian title at Williams, Oracle title at Red Bull, HP title at Ferrari, Microsoft with Mercedes as reported in January 2026, AWS as the sport's official cloud provider since 2018, RUCKUS at Haas, Airwallex at McLaren) were verified in September 2026 checks; Linear facts were checked on 14 October 2026. Re-verify on call day.
- **Pipeline check:** confirm no live deal in project management, developer tools or product software, and check the register for earlier Linear outreach.

## Part 3: The smart questions (12 to 15 minutes)

*[Conversation starters, not a form. Ask one, stay quiet, follow what they give you. Their answers become the brief.]*

**Open the story**
- "Tell me where Linear is right now, in your own words. What's going well, and what's the next hill to climb?"
- "What are the key focus areas for the next twelve months? What comes up in every leadership conversation?"

**The direction, and where this could play a role**
- "How do you see something like Formula 1, or sport at all, maybe playing a role in where the brand is headed? Even a rough first instinct helps; there's no wrong answer."
- "And as a business, where could you imagine it adding value: enterprise credibility, the brand story, hiring, community?"

**The enterprise push**
- "From the outside it looks like the next chapter is enterprise: bigger customers, bigger contracts. Is that fair? And what's the hardest part of that sale today: being known by the buyer, or being trusted by them?"

**The end goal**
- "Zooming out: if everything goes right over the next three years, what does Linear look like?"
- "And what would make a partnership like this a failure in your eyes? That answer usually tells us more than the first one."

**Evaluation and success**
- "If you ever did something like this, on what basis would it be judged internally, and by whom?"
- "What would a partnership of this magnitude need to deliver for you to call it a success after year one?"

**Buyers and users**
- "Who chooses Linear, and who signs the big contracts? I'm guessing those are two different people, and both matter here."
- "Are there named accounts or segments you'd build a program around?"

**Markets**
- "Is the growth story US-first or global? The calendar runs five continents, and the US alone has three races."

**Brand and marketing today**
- "You've built one of the strongest brands in software without marketing. What do you do deliberately to keep that brand sharp, and what would you never do?"
- "Is the bigger job right now top of the funnel or bottom of the funnel: being known by people who've never heard of Linear, or converting the enterprises already in your pipeline?"

**Budget and timing**
- "The unromantic question: is there a budget range in mind for something like this? I ask early because it decides which doors we even look at, and it saves us both time."
- "And timing: is this a now conversation or a next-year conversation? Both are fine; they just change what we bring back."

**Decision process**
- "Apart from you, who'd be part of this decision? The founders, the board?"

**Hospitality**
- "Last one, my favorite. If I gave you a paddock weekend tomorrow, who would you bring, and why those people? That answer usually tells me what a partnership is really for."

*[If they hesitate on budget:]*

> "No problem, you don't need a number today. If we get to recommendations, you'll see options at different levels, including the smallest sensible ones, with what each level buys."

## Part 4: How Formula 1 works, and where it could fit (8 to 10 minutes)

*[Calibrate to their answer from Part 2. Keep it precise; this room hates fluff. Invite questions: "Stop me anytime."]*

### 4.1 The sport in thirty seconds

> "Quick picture of how the sport is set up, because it explains a brand's options.
>
> Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company: the calendar, the broadcasts, the races. The FIA writes the rules. And then eleven teams, including two American ones, Haas and Cadillac, each an independent business with its own partners.
>
> So a brand can enter at three levels: the sport, a team, or a driver personally. They come in very different sizes, and for Linear I'd be straight from the start: the interesting doors are the right-sized ones."

### 4.2 What a team partnership includes

> "What do you get as a partner? Five parts, quickly.
>
> **Branding:** your name on the car, the suits, the garage, around 24 weekends a year, 1.8 billion total audience last season.
>
> **The official title:** Official Something Partner of the team, usable in all your own marketing and every enterprise conversation.
>
> **Hospitality:** paddock access and the Paddock Club, where 66% of guests are senior executives. One detail people miss: passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in a weekend.
>
> **Content:** access to the team's channels and drivers. This is the number one sport in the world for social engagement, 2.2 billion engagements, ahead of the NBA.
>
> **People:** driver appearances, filming days, hot laps for your top customers.
>
> And around it all, the business platform: 25-plus partners per team, 350-plus across the grid. That room is a market in itself."

### 4.3 The audience

*[Two audiences for Linear: the builders who choose the tool, and the executives who sign.]*

> "Who you reach. For Linear it's really two groups, and this sport is unusual because it holds both.
>
> **The builders.** This is the most engineering-obsessed sport on earth, and its audience skews young and technical: 43% under 35. The people who choose tools like yours watch this sport because it's their own discipline at full speed. That's where the brand love comes from.
>
> **The signers.** Formula 1 has the highest concentration of business decision makers in sport, index 160 against 100 for the general population. In the Paddock Club, two thirds are senior executives, and for a software brand that means the CTOs and engineering leaders who approve enterprise contracts. That's where the revenue comes from.
>
> And the stage is already set for software: AWS is the sport's cloud provider, Oracle's name is on Red Bull, HP's on Ferrari, Microsoft reportedly with Mercedes, and Atlassian's title is on Williams. The room is used to taking tools seriously."

### 4.4 Why this could fit Linear

> "Why we thought this was worth a call. Three reasons, and you tell me if they hold.
>
> **One, this sport is your thesis with an engine.** Linear's argument is that speed comes from craft: the right tools, the right process, momentum instead of chaos. Formula 1 is the most public proof of that argument anywhere. Money is capped, computing is capped, and a team ships a new version of the car every two weeks to a deadline that cannot move. The most disciplined team wins. You wouldn't be borrowing a story; you'd be pointing at the loudest example of your own.
>
> **Two, both of your audiences in one place.** The builders who choose Linear and the executives who sign for it watch the same race. Very few platforms reach the bottom-up and the top-down of a product-led company at once.
>
> **Three, the enterprise moment.** You're moving upmarket. Enterprise buyers purchase trust as much as software, and the fastest way for a young brand to borrow trust is to stand confidently on a serious stage. Your competitor's name is already on a car; that fact alone makes this conversation worth an hour.
>
> One honest note. Everything about Linear says taste and restraint, so a wrong-shaped sponsorship would cost you more than it buys. Whatever we recommend will be small, sharp and craft-first, and if the honest answer is that nothing here passes your bar, we'll say exactly that."

### 4.5 Three early sketches (ideas, not proposals)

*[Offer as sketches; their reaction feeds the brief.]*

> "To make it concrete, three sketches, all sized and shaped for Linear on purpose.
>
> **One, how a race team ships.** A content series on the sport's two-week build cycle: how a team plans, prioritizes and ships under a hard deadline and a hard cap. The best process story in the world, told in Linear's voice and design language. Nobody owns it, and your audience would devour it.
>
> **Two, the buyers' room.** A small, sharp hospitality program at two or three races: the engineering and product leadership of your target enterprise accounts, hosted properly, with your founders in the room. Seven passes, twenty guests, no logo required at all.
>
> **Three, the craft seat.** If the brief earns it: an entry-level partnership with one team, activated your way: the team's build process as a story, Linear-designed artifacts fans keep, the title used in your enterprise motion. Low single-digit millions, not headline money, and only if it passes your taste bar."

### Q1. "What are comparable brands doing in Formula 1, and why are they using it?"

> "Good question, and there's a clear pattern.
>
> Software and tech brands have moved into this sport in a wave: Atlassian took the title at Williams, Oracle's name is on Red Bull, HP's on Ferrari, Microsoft reportedly signed with Mercedes in January, and AWS has been the sport's official cloud provider since 2018. B2B services names like Cognizant, ServiceNow, NetApp and Tata Communications sit across the grid too.
>
> Why do they do it? Three jobs, usually. Trust: putting their name next to serious engineering so enterprise buyers take them seriously. The room: hosting the exact executives they sell to, race after race. And proof: the working partnerships, where the brand visibly does a job for the team. CommScope's RUCKUS brand powers the Haas team's operations with a live showcase in the garage; that deal is marketing you can demo.
>
> And it shows up in revenue. Airwallex, a fintech at McLaren: during the partnership their revenue grew 50% year on year across EMEA and the Americas, and UK revenue grew 57% from new customers. That's what a working platform looks like when the brief is right."

### Q2. "How do F1 teams drive introductions in business?"

> "Very concretely. It's a managed process, not a cocktail party.
>
> Every team runs a family of 25-plus partners, and the team's partnership department actively connects them: compliant introductions, partner summits, business forums on race weekends, private dinners. Add the hospitality rights, and you choose who sits at your table: your own target accounts, hosted at the race.
>
> For Linear that room is full of enterprises mid-way through modernizing how they build software. That's a pipeline, not a billboard."

### Q3. "How is ROI measured in Formula 1?"

> "Four ways, and you agree the framework upfront.
>
> **Media value:** independent firms like Nielsen track every second your brand appears, across TV and digital, and price it against your fee.
>
> **Brand tracking:** awareness and trust, measured before and after, in the audience you care about: engineering and product leadership, and developers.
>
> **Business pipeline:** introductions, meetings and contracts closed through the platform. That's how Airwallex could point at 50% revenue growth during their McLaren years.
>
> **Content performance:** reach and engagement on everything you build.
>
> For Linear I'd weight it to the funnel answer you give me today: if the job is enterprise trust, we measure enterprise motion, not impressions."

### Q4. "Which entry would fit us best?"

> "Today, instinct only: craft-first content, the buyers' room, and possibly a right-sized team seat later. I wouldn't respect you by pretending that's a recommendation.
>
> The mapping after this call turns it into names, numbers and a sequence against your brief. And if the honest answer is that nothing fits who Linear is, that's what we'll bring back."

### Q5. "What does a partnership cost?"

> "The price follows the rights. Rule of thumb: entry-level team positions start in the low single-digit millions a year, official partner tiers sit above that, and driver, content and pure hospitality programs sit below, some well below.
>
> Why expensive? Eleven teams for the whole world, one seat per category, three-to-five-year deals. Scarcity.
>
> For Linear, the shapes I'd explore first mostly live at the small end. And our advice is free either way: the rights holders pay our fee."

### Q6. "Why work with SPORTFIVE? Could we not go to a team directly?"

> "You could. Two reasons brands use us anyway.
>
> One, information. A team sells you its own story; we put all eleven next to each other: inventory, pricing, appetite, and what each would mean for Linear specifically. For more than twelve years we worked exclusively with Mercedes and Ferrari; we gave that up deliberately to sit on the brand's side, across the whole grid. The neutral seat is the product.
>
> Two, process. We build the business case, benchmark the price and negotiate on your behalf, because we do it every week.
>
> And since the rights holders pay our fee, it costs you nothing to use us. There's no version where you pay more."

### Q7. "Are any of our competitors already in Formula 1?"

> "One of them loudly: Atlassian holds the title partnership at Williams. That's exactly why I won't wing the rest of the category answer from memory; this space moves and you deserve the precise picture.
>
> Mapping project management, developer tools and software across all eleven teams and the series is part of the analysis after this call. You'd see who's in, who's out, and where the open positions are, including what Atlassian's position does and doesn't block."

### Q8. "We grew to $100M ARR without marketing. Why would we start now, and why here?"

> "Fair, and maybe you shouldn't. That's a real possible outcome of this process, and if the numbers say so, we'll say so.
>
> But here's the case worth testing. What got you here was the builders choosing you. What the next chapter needs is enterprises trusting you, and that's a different muscle: the buyer isn't in your product every day, doesn't feel the craft, and often picks the name their board already knows. That's precisely the job this platform does: trust, the room, and a story your buyers already respect. Your competitor figured that out and put their name on a car.
>
> So the question isn't whether Linear should start marketing. It's whether this one platform does a job your playbook can't, at a price that makes sense. That's a numbers conversation, and the mapping gives us the numbers."

# Humanoid x SPORTFIVE: F1 Call, Questions and Answers

**Call:** intro call with [Name, Job title] at Humanoid. From SPORTFIVE: Rahul, Bjorn and George.

---

## The intro

> "Hi [Name], thanks for making the time. I'm Rahul. I look after brand partnerships in SPORTFIVE's Formula 1 business, and Bjorn and George are with me.
>
> A bit about how we work, because it's different from what most people expect. We don't sell for one team. We look at all eleven, work out what you're trying to achieve, and then see whether any of it fits. The teams pay us, not you. And if it doesn't fit, we'll tell you."

### "So why did you reach out to us?"

> "Two simple reasons. The first is who you sell to. Big factories, carmakers, and the companies that supply them. A lot of those people are already in F1, through the carmakers and the engineering firms around the teams. Siemens, one of your partners, has worked with Red Bull since the team started.
>
> The second is what you do. You build robots that do the same physical job, again and again, without getting it wrong. F1 cares about the same thing. A team is a small factory that rebuilds its car for every race, and every part has to be right. And most of the teams build their cars in England, not far from your office.
>
> And your first robots go to work in German factories this winter. So it felt like a good time to talk. But I'd like to hear from you first. Where are things heading?"

## Questions to ask Humanoid

1. "You raised your Series A in July, and the first robots go live at Schaeffler next month. What does 2027 look like: winning new customers, or showing it works at scale with the ones you have?"
2. "Who buys from you today: carmakers and their suppliers like Schaeffler, electronics makers like Siemens, or logistics companies? Which of those matters most over the next two years?"
3. "You rent the robots out as a service rather than selling them. At a customer, who signs that off: the plant manager, the head of operations, or the board?"
4. "Germany is clearly your first market. What comes next: the UK, the rest of Europe, or the US, where you've got teams in Boston and San Diego?"
5. "You're a London company, but your first big customers are German. Does being British matter in how you tell your story?"
6. "The two-legged robot is mainly for research today, with homes further out. When does that start to change how you talk about Humanoid in public?"
7. "You've got around 95 jobs open, most of them in London. Is finding the right engineers as hard as finding customers?"
8. "CES on Schaeffler's stand and GTC with NVIDIA both seem to have worked for you. Which brought in more business? And when you spend serious money on marketing, how do you judge it afterwards?"
9. "Has anyone at Humanoid been to a race? Silverstone isn't far from you. What did they make of it?"
10. "If something here made sense, who else would need to be involved? Your founder, your strategy and marketing team, your investors? And is there budget for this kind of thing, or would it need to be found?"

## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who watches F1? Is it our audience?"

> "More of it than you'd think. F1 fans index at 160 for business decision makers, where 100 is the general population. That's the highest of any sport. In plain terms, the person who decides whether a plant brings in robots is much more likely than average to follow F1.
>
> The other thing I'd look at is age. 43% of fans are under 35. You're hiring a lot of engineers, mostly in London, and that's who you want to reach. Komatsu's academy with Williams, for example, finds engineering students through Formula Student, the university race car competition.
>
> And the sport goes where you are. There are more than twenty race weekends. Silverstone is your home race, and there are races close to your German customers, in Austria, Belgium and Italy."

### "Our buyers run factories, not fan clubs. Who's in the paddock?"

> "Mostly business people. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. There are more than 350 partner companies across the grid, and every one of them brings guests.
>
> What I'd point out for you is how many of them make things. Carmakers are all over the grid: Mercedes, Ferrari, Audi, Ford, Honda, Toyota, and General Motors through Cadillac. Around them are industrial names like Siemens, Hexagon and DMG MORI at Red Bull, Komatsu at Williams, and 3M, which joined Cadillac in June. Siemens you know already. Teams also introduce their partners to each other. So the people your guests meet are often the people who run factories."

### "How are similar brands activating, and what are they getting back?"

> "As far as we can tell, no humanoid robot company has a deal with an F1 team yet. So here are the closest ones: companies that build machines, chips and factory software, plus one humanoid from outside F1."

**Siemens, Hexagon and DMG MORI at Red Bull**

> "Start with a name you know. Siemens has been with Red Bull since 2004, and calls itself the team's longest-standing technical partner. DMG MORI has supplied the team's machine tools since before its first race in 2005, and Hexagon has been there about as long. They're there because a team is a small, very demanding factory, and that's the hardest customer they could have. Hexagon says Red Bull makes around 30,000 design changes in a season.
>
> Through the partnerships, Siemens' design and simulation software was used to develop Red Bull's new engine with Ford. DMG MORI's machines cut parts from small fasteners to gearbox parts. And Hexagon's measuring kit checks how the car is set up and how the engine is built, right through race weekends.
>
> Hexagon is the one that's published a number: it says the team cut faults by half over two years using its kit. Siemens hasn't published a return. The signal is time: all three are still on Red Bull's partner list this year, through a big rule change, and Siemens has stayed for more than twenty years."

**Arm at Aston Martin**

> "Arm has been with Aston Martin since January 2025. It's a British company, from Cambridge, and the team is based at Silverstone. The team's CEO at the time said Arm's 'expertise in compute and AI technology will play a significant role in enhancing performance'. Arm's own reason, from its marketing chief, was to open 'new pathways for talent in STEM and women in motorsport'.
>
> Through the partnership, Arm works on the team's new pit-stop system: the wheel guns, the jacks and the lights that release the car. The team built it in about nine months, around Arm's chips, and calls it nearly fully autonomous. Arm also backs the team's F1 Academy car, and Jessica Hawkins, who runs that programme, is its ambassador.
>
> Arm hasn't published a return. The signal is the job itself: a team doesn't hand you its pit stop unless it trusts you. And Arm is still on the team's partner list this season, talking about the work in podcasts with the team's head of electronics."

**Komatsu at Williams**

> "Komatsu has worked with Williams on and off since the 1990s, when it made gear parts for the 1996 and 1997 championship cars. It came back as a partner in 2024. The reason, in its CEO's words: 'the greatest engineering advances come from tackling complex challenges.'
>
> Through the partnership, Komatsu's engineers help develop key parts for future Williams cars, built in Oxfordshire. It runs an engineering academy that brings ten students a year into the team, and it's adding a leadership programme for its own managers.
>
> No business numbers are published. But in September, after three seasons, Komatsu renewed with a bigger role. It's now the team's Official Hydraulic Innovation Partner, and from next year it's in F2, F3 and F1 Academy too. A company that grows its role like that is telling you it's working."

**Exein at Haas**

> "Exein has been with Haas since July, from the Belgian Grand Prix. It's an Italian company that keeps robots and connected machines safe from hackers, and its page on the Haas website names humanoid robots among the things it protects. Its founder said F1 is 'one of the world's greatest demonstrations of engineering excellence, where every detail matters.'
>
> Through the deal, which runs for several years, its name is on the rear wing of the Haas car, and the two have said they'll work together to get the message out about security. Haas's team principal said F1 runs on 'the confidence that every system performs exactly as intended.'
>
> It's too early for results, and none are published. What it shows is that physical AI has arrived in the paddock, and that a company can join halfway through a season with a clear title of its own: Official Physical AI Security Partner."

**Roboze at Racing Bulls**

> "Roboze, an Italian 3D printing company, has been with Racing Bulls since 2024. It works across racing, with teams in MotoGP and IndyCar too. When the deal was renewed, its founder called it 'a tangible confirmation of the value that our technology and advanced materials bring.'
>
> Through the partnership, the team prints parts in carbon-reinforced plastics that replace metal ones. The team says that's cut production times and costs.
>
> Roboze doesn't publish sales numbers from it. But less than a year in, the team renewed for three more years, and its CEO said Roboze had 'transformed our approach to producing critical components.' Coming from a customer, that's the line you want."

**Oracle at Red Bull, and CoreWeave at Aston Martin**

> "These are the AI and computing names. Oracle has been Red Bull's title partner since February 2022. Its CEO's reason is simple: the technology the team uses is the same technology that powers 'companies across every industry.' CoreWeave joined Aston Martin in May last year as its AI cloud partner.
>
> Through the partnerships, Oracle runs Red Bull's race simulations, helped develop the new engine, and is building an AI assistant to help the race engineers make calls. CoreWeave gives Aston Martin the computing power for its simulations and design work.
>
> Neither publishes a business return. Red Bull points to the results instead: three Drivers' titles and two Constructors' titles since Oracle came in. And in February, Oracle signed again."

**Hyundai and Boston Dynamics at the FIFA World Cup**

> "One from outside F1, because it's the closest thing to you. Hyundai has been a FIFA partner since 1999, and when it renewed in 2023 it brought in Boston Dynamics, its robotics company. Its marketing chief said they wanted to show 'that the future isn't something we imagine.'
>
> At a World Cup knockout match in New Jersey this July, the Atlas humanoid walked out of the tunnel at half-time and handed the referee the match ball.
>
> No return is published, and it was one moment, not a season. But it shows that a humanoid at a live sports event is now something a big brand will put its name to."

**What that adds up to**

> "So the honest picture is this. As far as we can tell, no humanoid robot company has a deal with an F1 team yet. The companies closest to you rarely publish numbers. What they do show is that they stay, they renew, and their role gets bigger, like Komatsu at Williams in September, or Siemens at Red Bull for twenty years.
>
> For you, I'd take three things from that. First, give your robot a job people can see, the way Arm did with the pit stop, rather than just a logo. Second, let the team's people help tell the story, because a mechanic showing why a job is hard is worth more than an advert. Third, use it to hire, the way Komatsu brings students into Williams. Most teams are within a couple of hours of your office in London, and their engineers are exactly the kind of people you're trying to find."

### "Content is really important for us. How would a team help us make it?"

> "It's one of the most useful parts of a deal, and for a company like yours it could be the main reason to do one.
>
> Most deals come with a set number of filming days each year with the drivers and the team's people, and the team's content people help you make it. The team's own channels matter too. F1 is the number one sport on social media, ahead of the NBA.
>
> One thing to know: the race footage is controlled by Formula 1 itself, not the teams. So partner content is filmed around the race: at the factory, in the garage, or on filming days.
>
> The best partner content usually isn't about the product. CrowdStrike made a series with Mercedes where the team's specialists explain the safety kit on the car, from the halo to the helmet, and Mercedes says it got 6.2 million views. Arm made a two-part podcast with Aston Martin's head of electronics about how the pit-stop kit works.
>
> For you, I'd start with teaching. In June you showed your robot doing a job one and a half times faster than the people it learned from, after a few days of practice. So get a mechanic to teach it a job from the team's stores, like picking parts or packing the boxes that fly to each race, then come back a week later and see who's quicker. People would share that. And since most teams are close to London, your own engineers could be in it too, which helps you hire.
>
> If content matters to you, we'd spell it out in the deal: how many days, which drivers and team people, what the team posts on its own channels, and how long and where you can use it."

### "Why are these brands in F1?"

> "Two reasons, mostly.
>
> The first is proof. A team is a small factory that keeps changing its car all season, under a spending cap, and every part has to be right. If your machine or your software works there, people believe it will work in their plant. That's why Siemens, Hexagon and Roboze are there.
>
> The second is people: the customers they want to host, and the engineers they want to hire. Komatsu's academy at Williams is about exactly that.
>
> For Humanoid, I'd guess it's both. Your founder said it in May, when you signed Schaeffler: 'Moving into real-world operations is where the true value of humanoid robots is proven.' An F1 team would say the same about every part on the car."

### "Who are our competitors in F1, and which teams are available?"

> "I don't want to promise you anything on that yet, because right now it would be a guess. As far as we can tell, no humanoid robot company has a deal with an F1 team. The nearest thing in motorsport is ABB, the automation group, and that's as title partner of Formula E, not F1.
>
> But a few teams have partners close to what you do. Arm on Aston Martin's pit stops, Exein on physical AI security at Haas, Siemens, Hexagon and DMG MORI in Red Bull's factory, and Komatsu at Williams. Some teams are also tied to carmakers with their own robot plans. Mercedes-Benz has invested in Apptronik, the American humanoid maker, and put money in again this year. And Toyota, which is Haas's title partner, does humanoid research with Boston Dynamics. These deals usually come with exclusivity.
>
> So the first thing we do is check where there's a conflict, and rule those teams out.
>
> The second thing is understanding what you want out of it. If it's mainly about meeting carmakers and their suppliers, that can point you to different teams than if it's about hiring engineers in London, or opening doors in the US. Then we come back with the teams where it fits.
>
> To give you an idea of what a partner like you does once they're in, look at Arm at Aston Martin. The team built a new pit-stop system in about nine months, around Arm's chips, and calls it nearly fully autonomous. The only part that stays manual is the trigger on the wheel gun, because the rules say so. Arm's chips check the wheel is locked before the car is released, in less than a millisecond. So Arm gets to say a team trusts it with the pit stop, where one mistake can cost a race. That's a much stronger story than a sticker on the car."

### "It's very crowded. Won't we get lost?"

> "It's a real risk. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and a lot of them are just logos. The ones that get noticed do one of two things.
>
> Some give their product a real job people can see, like Arm on the pit stop. Others make something people want to watch. You've got an advantage most partners don't: your product moves, and it's shaped like a person. A robot doing a mechanic's job is more interesting to watch than another logo on a car.
>
> And you've got the story. Your founder says robots shouldn't replace people, they should support them. F1 is very good at telling that kind of story: a team of people, with machines that help them do the job a bit better every week."

### "Why would a team want a partner like us?"

> "Teams aren't short of technology partners. Tech companies now spend more on F1 teams than any other kind of business, and a Reuters report in May counted eight new AI deals in six months.
>
> What's rarer is a partner whose product people can see working. Most of those AI deals are software in a data centre. A humanoid robot is something a team can film, show at an event, or put in front of its other partners.
>
> You'd also bring people a team would like to meet: the industrial groups you already work with, like Siemens, Bosch and Schaeffler.
>
> Whether there's room for that at a given team is what we'd check first."

### "What would a first deal look like for a company our size?"

> "You've just raised a big round, so you have options. A typical first deal for a company that sells to other businesses is one team, one clear role, some branding, a few race weekends with customers, and some content, over two or three years. Exein at Haas looks like that: a clear title, its name on the car, and it came in halfway through the season.
>
> Others start smaller, hosting customers at a few races to see whether it works before going further. Passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend. For you, Silverstone is the obvious one, as your home race, and Austria or Belgium are easy for German customers. Some sign a driver or a content deal instead of a team, which also costs less."

### "What does it cost?"

> "It depends a lot on the team and what's included. A supplier or entry-level partner deal starts in the low single-digit millions a year. Official partner deals sit above that. Hosting customers at a few races, a driver deal or a content deal costs a lot less. I'd rather understand what you want first, then show you options at different levels."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

> "You can go to a team directly, and most of them are a short drive from you. They'll tell you about their own team, which is fair enough. We worked only with Mercedes and Ferrari for over twelve years, and then stopped, so we could look at all eleven teams for the brand. The teams pay our fee, so having us compare them costs you nothing. And because several teams already have partners close to you, and some are tied to carmakers with their own robot plans, someone has to check each one for conflicts before you spend time on any of them."

### "Which team would fit us?"

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. It depends on what you want out of it, which markets matter most, whether that's Germany, the UK, the rest of Europe or the US, and where there's no conflict. That's what we'd come back to you with."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "We'd agree that before anything's signed. For you I'd look at three things. Business: which carmakers, suppliers and logistics firms you spent time with, and the trials and contracts that came from it. Hiring: how many good engineers applied, especially in London. And content: how far the films travelled, which we can measure.
>
> It can show up in the numbers. Airwallex says its revenue grew 50% year on year in its Europe and Americas regions during its McLaren partnership, and 57% in the UK from new customers."

### "We haven't shipped a commercial robot yet. Isn't this too early for us?"

> "It might be, and if it is, we'll say so. But timing matters less than what you're trying to do in the next two or three years. Your first robots go live at Schaeffler in Germany before the end of this year, and Bosch will build them for Europe. If F1 helps you win the next customers and hire the engineers you need, it can earn its place.
>
> So we'd start from your plan and work back. If the answer is a couple of race weekends with customers first, that's fine. If it's 'not yet', we'll tell you."

### "Isn't a robot at a race just a gimmick?"

> "It can be, if it's a stunt. Hyundai's Atlas at the World Cup was one great moment, and then it was over. The deals that last are the ones where the product does something real, again and again, like Arm on the pit stop or Hexagon checking the car every race weekend.
>
> Your story is already built that way. Your robot ran for more than eight hours in Siemens' factory in Erlangen and hit every target it was set. And the Schaeffler deal is for thousands of robots over the next few years, not a demo. That's the F1 version of the story: not a trick, but the same job done right, every time."

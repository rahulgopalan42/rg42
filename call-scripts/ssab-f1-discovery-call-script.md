# SSAB x SPORTFIVE: F1 Discovery Call Script

**Call type:** First call / discovery. Goal: introduce yourself and SPORTFIVE, understand SSAB's objectives, fill out the brief, show where fossil-free steel fits F1's sustainability era, agree next steps.

**How to use this script:** Every spoken block is written the way you would say it out loud. Do not read it word for word. Glance at the bold anchors, then talk. Short sentences, slow pace, warm tone. The listeners are from Sweden; English is strong there, but plain and short still wins. Let them talk more than you.

## Part 3: The brief. Key discovery questions (12 to 15 minutes)

## Part 4: How Formula 1 partnerships work (8 to 10 minutes)

*[Calibrate to their answer from Part 2. If they know the sport, compress. Invite questions as you go: "Stop me anytime."]*

### 4.1 The sport in thirty seconds

> "Let me give you a simple picture of how the sport is set up, because it explains the options you have.
>
> Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company. They own the commercial side: the calendar, the broadcast deals, the races.
>
> The FIA is the regulator. They write the rules.
>
> And then there are eleven teams. From this coming season it is eleven, including two American teams, Haas and Cadillac. Each team is an independent business with its own owners and its own partnerships. Some belong to car makers, like Ferrari, Mercedes and Audi. Some to consumer giants, like Red Bull. Some to investors.
>
> So a brand can partner at three levels: with the sport itself, with a team, or with a driver personally. Most brands enter through a team, and I can explain why when we get there."

### 4.2 What a team partnership includes

> "What do you get as a partner? Think of it in five parts.
>
> **One, branding.** Your logo on the car, the race suits, the garage. Around 24 race weekends a year. Last season the total audience was 1.8 billion, and the average race reached 76 million people.
>
> **Two, the official title.** You become, for example, the Official Steel Partner of a team. You use the team's name, logo and imagery in your own marketing and customer conversations.
>
> **Three, hospitality.** Paddock access, the Paddock Club, garage tours, time with the drivers. 66% of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. For a company selling to industrial leadership, every race is a room full of buyers.
>
> And one detail people miss. A Grand Prix runs over three days, and your passes work every day. Seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend, and hundreds across a season.
>
> **Four, content and digital.** Access to the team's channels and drivers for content. Formula 1 is the number one sport in the world for social engagement, 2.2 billion engagements, ahead of the NBA. A strong stage for the fossil-free story.
>
> **Five, people.** Driver appearances, team bosses at your events, filming days, hot laps for your top customers.
>
> And around all of this sits the business platform: every team carries 25 or more partners, more than 350 companies across the grid, meeting each other all season. That room is your pipeline."

### 4.3 The audience

*[Pure B2B framing for SSAB: the buyers first, the fan scale as the story's stage.]*

> "Now, who you reach. Two audiences matter for you.
>
> **The buyers.** Formula 1 has the highest concentration of business decision makers in sport: fans index at 160 for management and strategy roles, where the average person is 100. In the Paddock Club, 66% of guests are senior executives. And the paddock itself is your industry's home ground: the teams belong to car makers, engine makers and industrial giants, and the 350 partner companies around them include the manufacturers and builders you sell to. Your buyers are not just watching this sport. Many of them own it.
>
> **The world, because your story needs a stage.** 827 million fans, growing fast, young and global. Fossil-free steel is a story for customers, but also for regulators, investors, future employees and the public. Very few stages reach all of them at once. This one does, 24 times a year."

### 4.4 Why this could fit SSAB

> "Let me tell you why I find this opportunity interesting for SSAB. Three reasons.
>
> **First, same story, same year.** In 2026 you bring the world's first fossil-free steel to market. In 2026 every F1 car runs on 100% sustainable fuel. Two heavy, loud, supposedly dirty industries proving that clean and world-class go together. A partnership makes SSAB the industrial face of that story, on the world's fastest stage.
>
> **Second, the room is your customer list.** Car makers own the teams. Ford is back in the sport with Red Bull. General Motors stands behind Cadillac. And around them, 350 partner companies including manufacturers, builders and energy groups. As a partner, you meet them as a peer, race after race. That is a pipeline, not a billboard.
>
> **Third, steel is already at every race.** The barriers that protect the drivers, the fences, the grandstands, the team factories: all steel. The sport cannot happen without your product. Nobody has ever claimed that role. It is sitting there, waiting for the company with the best steel story in the world.
>
> One last thing on value. All of this sits on very small supply: eleven teams for the entire world, one seat per category, three to five year deals. Small supply, growing demand. That is why these partnerships hold their value."

### 4.5 Three activation ideas (examples, not proposals)

*[Offer these as sketches to make it concrete. Let them react; their reaction feeds the brief.]*

> "To make it concrete, three sketches. Early thoughts, nothing more.
>
> **One, the steel that protects.** SSAB high-strength steel in the barriers and safety structures at selected races or a team's home circuit. The story is simple and human: when things go wrong at 300 kilometers per hour, our steel is what stands between the driver and the wall. Safety is the most respected word in this sport.
>
> **Two, the fossil-free flagship.** A team's next building, garage structure or paddock installation built with SSAB Zero and fossil-free steel, and told as part of F1's road to net zero 2030. The first fossil-free steel structure in Formula 1 is a world story on its own.
>
> **Three, the customer program.** Your top automotive and industrial customers hosted across the season, with private roundtables on green materials alongside the team's engineers and fellow partners. Twenty guests per weekend from seven passes. The people you most want to convince, standing inside the proof."

### Q1. "What does a steel company get from Formula 1, in one minute?"

> "Three things.
>
> One, a stage. The biggest sustainability turnaround in sport, in the same year as your fossil-free launch. Your story and the sport's story are the same sentence.
>
> Two, a room. The teams are owned by car makers, and the paddock holds 350 partner companies, including the manufacturers and builders you sell to. You meet them as a fellow partner, all season.
>
> Three, a role. Steel already protects every driver at every corner of every track. Nobody has ever owned that story. You can.
>
> That is the one-minute version. The brief decides how big each of the three should be."

### Q2. "What is the difference between partnering with F1 itself, a team, or a driver?"

> "Three doors into the sport.
>
> **The series, Formula 1 itself.** You partner with the sport: branding at every race, race title rights, global reach, neutrality. Bigger deals, premium prices. For a story tied to the whole sport's net zero road, this door is worth a look.
>
> **A team.** The story lives here: the car, the drivers, the factory, the hospitality, the partner ecosystem. Many more entry points and price levels. For most brands, this is the right door.
>
> **A driver personally.** The smallest door: personal channels, appearance days, campaign rights. Useful as a face, but no team rights and no paddock hospitality.
>
> The brief will tell us which door, and at what level."

### Q3. "Teams have thirty partners. How does one brand stand out?"

> "Fair question. Three answers.
>
> First, structure. Partners sit in clear tiers, and your tier defines exactly what you own: positions, days, content, hospitality. All contractual.
>
> Second, exclusivity. Within your category you are alone. The team cannot sign another steel company.
>
> Third, attention is won through activation, not logo size. A barrier that saves a driver, a building made of fossil-free steel, a story people retell: these beat bigger logos every weekend.
>
> And the access, the hospitality, the introductions: those are private rights. They never get diluted by other partners."

### Q4. "How do teams help with B2B introductions?"

> "Very concretely. This is a managed process, not a cocktail party.
>
> Every team runs a family of at least 25 partners, and the team's partnership department actively connects them: compliant introductions, partner summits, business forums on race weekends, private dinners. And it continues off the calendar at industry events.
>
> Two examples from our own work. CommScope's RUCKUS brand at Haas: enterprise wireless powering the team's operations, with a live showcase in the garage; our agency ran the grid-wide process. And Airwallex at McLaren: during the partnership, revenue grew 50% year on year across EMEA and the Americas, and UK revenue grew 57% from new customer acquisition.
>
> For SSAB: a paddock owned by car makers, filled with manufacturers and builders, meeting you as a fellow partner. That is a pipeline, not a billboard."

### Q5. "Which team would fit us best?"

> "Today I do not know yet, and I would not respect you by guessing.
>
> This is exactly what the grid mapping is for. We look at each team's owners and industry ties, its sustainability agenda, its factory and infrastructure plans, its hospitality quality, and the overlap with your customer list. A car-maker team tells one story; a challenger team building a new factory tells another.
>
> Give us the brief today, and the recommendation comes back with names and reasons."

### Q6. "How is ROI measured in Formula 1?"

> "Four ways, usually.
>
> **Media value.** Independent firms like Nielsen track every second your branding appears, across TV and digital, and give it a money value against your fee.
>
> **Brand tracking.** Awareness and image shift, measured before and after: does the world connect SSAB with fossil-free steel? That number moving is the campaign working.
>
> **Business pipeline.** For B2B, you count introductions, meetings and deals closed through the platform. That is how Airwallex could point to 50% revenue growth during their partnership.
>
> **Hospitality outcomes.** Which customers you hosted, and what those relationships produced afterwards.
>
> You agree the framework upfront and review it with the team every quarter."

### Q7. "Who owns Formula 1? How does the money work?"

> "Three layers, very simple.
>
> Liberty Media, an American media company, owns Formula 1 itself. They bought it in 2017. They control the commercial rights: races, broadcast deals, the calendar.
>
> The FIA is the independent regulator.
>
> The eleven teams are independent businesses: Ferrari, Mercedes and Audi belong to car makers, Red Bull to the drinks company, others to investors. Each team earns a share of the sport's revenues plus its own sponsorships.
>
> Since 2021 the sport has a cost cap, so teams became profitable, stable businesses. Team sponsorship has doubled since 2020, to 2.2 billion dollars a year. You would be entering a healthy, growing market."

### Q8. "What does a partnership cost, and why is Formula 1 so expensive?"

> "Let me explain how the pricing works, because Formula 1 has its own logic.
>
> The price follows the rights inside the deal: your tier, your branding positions, driver time, content days, hospitality volume, and the category you lock. As a rule of thumb: entry-level and supplier positions start in the low single-digit millions per year. Meaningful official partner positions sit in the mid single-digit millions. Title partnerships are a different world, well above fifty million a year.
>
> Why more expensive than other sport? Four reasons.
>
> First, supply. Football has hundreds of clubs. Formula 1 has eleven teams for the entire world, one seat per category, locked for three to five years.
>
> Second, demand. Team sponsorship has doubled since 2020, and new money keeps arriving.
>
> Third, one deal covers the world: around 24 races across five continents in one contract.
>
> And fourth, access. The paddock and the hospitality carry the price, because nobody else can offer them.
>
> This is why we start with the brief. Our job is to match the level to your goals, then negotiate it hard on your side. And our advice costs you nothing."

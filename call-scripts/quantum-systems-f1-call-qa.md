# Quantum Systems x SPORTFIVE: F1 Call, Questions and Answers

**Call:** intro call with [Name, Job title] at Quantum Systems. From SPORTFIVE: Rahul, Bjorn and George.

---

## The intro

> "Hi [Name], thanks for making the time. I'm Rahul. I look after brand partnerships in SPORTFIVE's Formula 1 business, and Bjorn and George are with me.
>
> A bit about how we work, because it's different from what most people expect. We don't sell for one team. We look at all eleven, work out what you're trying to achieve, and then see whether any of it fits. The teams pay us, not you. And if it doesn't fit, we'll tell you."

### "So why did you reach out to us?"

> "Two simple reasons. The first is who you serve. Surveyors, mining and energy companies, farmers and foresters, and the people who map a flood zone after a disaster. Those industries are already in F1. Komatsu, which makes mining machines, has its engineers helping to develop parts for one team's future cars.
>
> The second is what you do. You build light, efficient aircraft from composites. They take off from a field, fly on a wing, and have to work first time in heat, dust and rain. Then they have to bring back good data. An F1 team cares about the same things: weight, aerodynamics, reliability and data. And you're close to it. Audi builds its F1 engine about an hour north of Munich, and the races in Austria and Italy are a short trip away.
>
> And you've just raised money to build more and grow abroad. So it felt like a good time to talk. But I'd like to hear from you first. Where are things heading?"

## Questions they might ask, and how to answer

### "Who watches F1? Is it our audience?"

> "More of it than you'd think. F1 fans index at 160 for business decision makers, where 100 is the general population. That's the highest of any sport. In plain terms, the person who decides whether a mine or an energy company buys a mapping aircraft is much more likely than average to follow F1.
>
> The other number I'd look at is age. 43% of fans are under 35. You're hiring engineers fast, and that's who you want to reach.
>
> And the sport goes where you are. There are more than twenty race weekends on five continents. Austria and Monza are close to home, Madrid joined this year, and there are three races in the US and one in Australia, where you also build."

### "Our buyers are public bodies, mines and survey firms, not fans. Who's in the paddock?"

> "Mostly business people. Two thirds of Paddock Club guests are senior executives. There are more than 350 partner companies across the grid, and every one of them brings guests.
>
> What I'd point out for you is how many of them dig, build or fly. Komatsu is at Williams. Aramco and Ma'aden, the Saudi mining group, are at Aston Martin, and so is Atlas Air, which flies the team's freight. Siemens is at Red Bull, next to Hexagon, which owns the survey brand Leica. Boeing is on Alpine's list. Teams also introduce their partners to each other. So the people your guests meet often run mines, energy companies and airlines.
>
> One thing to plan for. If some of your guests work for governments or public bodies, they'll have their own rules on accepting hospitality. We'd build around that from the start."

### "How are similar brands activating, and what are they getting back?"

> "As far as we can tell, no drone maker has a deal with an F1 team yet. So here are the closest ones: a technology and defense group, the mining, energy and aviation names, and an engineering company that helps build the car."

**Indra Group at Alpine**

> "Start with the closest. Indra Group has been with Alpine since January. It's a Spanish company, and it calls itself a technology and defense group. Its executive chairman, Ángel Escribano, said F1 lets it 'demonstrate the value of our capabilities in real high-demand environments.' Its CEO also tied it to the new race in Madrid, and to showing Spanish technology to the world.
>
> Through the deal, Alpine brings in IndraMind, Indra's AI platform. It makes sense of the data from each race weekend and helps with decisions at the track and in the factory. In March, Indra put an Alpine car on its stand at Mobile World Congress in Barcelona.
>
> It's too early for results, and none are published. What it shows is that a team will sign a company that describes itself as defense, when the story is the technology. Notice it leads with IndraMind, the AI brand. And we haven't seen a public row about it."

**Komatsu at Williams**

> "Komatsu, the Japanese maker of mining and construction machines, has worked with Williams on and off since the 1990s. Back then it made gear parts for the 1996 and 1997 title-winning cars. It came back as a partner two years ago. The reason, in its CEO's words: 'the greatest engineering advances come from tackling complex challenges.'
>
> Through the partnership, Komatsu's engineers help develop key parts for future Williams cars. It also runs an engineering academy that brings ten students a year into the team, found through Formula Student, the university race car competition.
>
> No business numbers are published. But in September it renewed with a bigger role, as Williams' Official Hydraulic Innovation Partner, and from next year it's in F2, F3 and F1 Academy too. A company that grows its role like that is telling you it's working."

**Ma'aden and Aramco at Aston Martin**

> "Two big Saudi industrial names on one team. Aramco has been with Aston Martin since 2022, and has been the only name in the team's title since 2024. Ma'aden, the mining group, joined two years ago. Its CEO, Bob Wilt, said the team's ambition 'resonates deeply with Maaden', and that the deal helps it 'reach new, younger audiences.'
>
> Through the deals, Aramco supplies the team's fuels and lubricants. Ma'aden uses its deal to hire, with university and internship programmes. It also ran a campaign called 'Unearth Your Greatness', about the craft in F1 and in mining.
>
> Neither publishes a return. The signal is how fast the commitment grew. Less than four months after joining, Ma'aden became the team's first ever Principal Partner. And Aramco's first deal grew into a five-year one, running to 2028, which made it the team's only title partner."

**Atlas Air at Aston Martin**

> "Atlas Air has been with Aston Martin since February last year. It runs the world's largest fleet of 747 freighters. The team's COO, Ben Fitzgerald, gave the reason in one line: 'Logistics is the lifeblood of a Formula One team.'
>
> Through the deal, Atlas flies the team's air freight to races around the world, and its name went on the car and the race suits. It uses races to host, too. After Miami this year, it held a dinner for senior aviation people with Jenson Button, the team's ambassador. And the team filmed Pedro de la Rosa, another ambassador, trying a 747 cockpit simulator.
>
> No return is published. The signal is the job itself: if the freight doesn't arrive, the team doesn't race. That's the promise you make to your customers too. The kit has to arrive and work first time."

**Siemens at Red Bull**

> "Siemens has been with Red Bull since 2004, before the team's first race, and calls it one of the longest technical partnerships in F1. When they marked twenty years together, the team's boss at the time said Siemens gives it 'the digital backbone that lets us turn ideas into reality faster than ever.'
>
> Through the partnership, Red Bull designs its carbon composite parts in Siemens software, and tracks around 10,000 different parts on each car.
>
> This is one of the few that's published numbers. Siemens says its composite software cut the time from design to finished part by 30%, and that design changes which took weeks to sign off now take hours. And it's still on Red Bull's partner list this year."

**What that adds up to**

> "So the honest picture is this. As far as we can tell, no drone maker has a deal with an F1 team. Indra at Alpine is the closest to you, and it's new. The industrial names rarely publish numbers. What they show is that they stay, they renew and their role gets bigger, like Komatsu in September, Ma'aden within four months, or Siemens for more than twenty years.
>
> For you, I'd take three things from that. First, lead with the civil work and the engineering, the way Indra leads with its AI platform. Second, give it a real job people can see, like Siemens on the car's parts or Atlas on the freight, rather than just a logo. Third, use it to hire, the way Komatsu and Ma'aden do. Their programmes find engineers through Formula Student and universities, and those are the people you need."

### "Content is really important for us. How would a team help us make it?"

> "It's one of the most useful parts of a deal. For a company that needs engineers, it may matter as much as the logo.
>
> Most deals come with a set number of filming days each year with the drivers and the team's people, and the team's content people help you make it. The team's own channels matter too. F1 is the number one sport on social media, ahead of the NBA.
>
> One thing to know: the race footage is controlled by Formula 1 itself, not the teams. So partner content is filmed around the race: at the factory, in the garage, or on filming days.
>
> The best partner content usually isn't about the product. CrowdStrike made a series with Mercedes where the team's specialists explain the safety kit on the car, from the halo to the helmet, and Mercedes says it got 6.2 million views. And Aston Martin filmed Pedro de la Rosa trying a 747 cockpit simulator with Atlas Air.
>
> For you, I'd start with wings. An F1 car and your aircraft both depend on light composite wings. This year's F1 cars move their wings on the straights. Your aircraft take off like a helicopter, then switch to flying on the wing. So put one of the team's composites people next to one of your wing builders. Each shows how they make a wing that's light and doesn't break, how they test it, and what goes wrong. No stunt. Just two people who build wings for a living. The engineers you want to hire would watch that.
>
> You might hear that drones already film F1. They do. F1 flies its own at about ten races a season, and two years ago Red Bull had a camera drone built that chased Verstappen's car round Silverstone. But that's F1's broadcast and a team project, and we don't know whose kit they use. So I wouldn't build your idea on it.
>
> If content matters to you, we'd spell it out in the deal: how many days, which drivers and team people, what the team posts on its own channels, and how long and where you can use it."

### "Why are these brands in F1?"

> "Two reasons, mostly.
>
> The first is proof. A team builds a light, fast machine, keeps changing it all season under a spending cap, and needs it to work first time at every race. If your kit works there, people believe it will work for them. That's why Siemens and Komatsu are there.
>
> The second is people: the customers they want to host, and the engineers they want to hire. Ma'aden says it wants to 'attract and retain top talent.'
>
> For Quantum, I'd guess it's both, with hiring near the top. When Skyports signed with you last year, its director said 'confidence in aircraft performance is paramount.' An F1 team would say the same about every part on the car."

### "Who are our competitors in F1, and which teams are available?"

> "I don't want to promise you anything on that yet, because right now it would be a guess. As far as we can tell, no drone maker has a deal with an F1 team.
>
> But some teams have partners or owners close to what you do. Boeing and Indra are both on Alpine's partner list. Hexagon, which owns Leica, is at Red Bull, and that's close to your survey business. And some of the carmakers that own teams are moving into drones themselves. Renault, which owns Alpine, is building a long-range drone with Turgis Gaillard for the French forces. And in June, Mercedes-Benz agreed to work with Tytan, the Munich drone company, on anti-drone vehicles. Deals like these usually come with exclusivity, and an owner's plans matter as much as a partner's.
>
> So the first thing we do is check where there's a conflict, and rule those teams out.
>
> The second thing is understanding what you want out of it. If it's mainly about hiring engineers, that can point you to different teams than if it's about meeting mining and energy companies, or opening doors in the US. Then we come back with the teams where it fits.
>
> To give you an idea of what a partner like you does once they're in, look at Siemens at Red Bull. A car has around 10,000 different parts, and the team releases thousands of design changes a season. Every one has to fit and work at the next race. Red Bull designs its carbon parts in Siemens software, and Siemens says that cut the time from design to finished part by 30%. So Siemens gets to say a championship-winning team builds its car with its tools. That's a much stronger story than a sticker on the car."

### "It's very crowded. Won't we get lost?"

> "It's a real risk. Most teams have 25 or more partners, and a lot of them are just logos. The ones that get noticed do one of two things.
>
> Some give their product a real job people can see, like Siemens on the car's parts or Atlas on the freight. Others make something people want to watch. You've got an advantage most partners don't: your product flies, and it's built from the same kind of materials as the car. That's easier to show than software in a data centre.
>
> And you've got the story. You started in 2015 with a patent for an aircraft that takes off like a helicopter and flies like a plane, and you've grown fast since. F1 is very good at telling that kind of story: a small group of engineers solving a hard problem, then building it at scale."

### "Why would a team want a partner like us?"

> "Teams aren't short of technology partners. Tech companies now spend more on F1 teams than any other kind of business, and a Reuters report in May counted eight new AI deals in six months.
>
> What's rarer is a partner that builds aircraft. You work on the same problems a team does: weight, aerodynamics, batteries and reliability. Your Trinity Pro is built to fly from minus twelve to fifty degrees. That's a conversation a team's engineers would enjoy.
>
> You'd also bring people a team would like to meet. Blackstone and Airbus invested in you in July, and your customers include mining, energy and survey companies in many countries.
>
> Whether there's room for that at a given team, and whether its owners are comfortable, is what we'd check first."

### "What would a first deal look like for a company our size?"

> "You've just raised a big round, and you say you're profitable, so money isn't the limit. The conflict check is. A typical first deal for a company that sells to other businesses is one team, one clear role, some branding, a few race weekends with customers, and some content, over two or three years. Indra at Alpine looks like that.
>
> Others start smaller, hosting customers at a few races to see whether it works before going further. Passes work all three days, so seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend. For you, Austria in July, and Monza and Madrid in September, are the obvious ones. And a hiring programme, like Komatsu's academy at Williams, can sit inside the deal from day one."

### "What does it cost?"

> "It depends a lot on the team and what's included. A supplier or entry-level partner deal starts in the low single-digit millions a year. Official partner deals sit above that. Hosting customers at a few races, a driver deal or a content deal costs a lot less. I'd rather understand what you want first, then show you options at different levels."

### "Why go through SPORTFIVE rather than a team directly?"

> "You can go to a team directly. They'll tell you about their own team, which is fair enough. We worked only with Mercedes and Ferrari for over twelve years, and then stopped, so we could look at all eleven teams for the brand. The teams pay our fee, so having us compare them costs you nothing. And for you, someone has to check each team, its owners and its partners quietly, before your name goes anywhere. That's better done by us than by a round of calls from you."

### "Which team would fit us?"

> "I don't know yet, and I'd rather not guess. It depends on what you want out of it, which markets matter most, whether that's Germany, the UK, the US or Australia, and where there's no conflict, with partners or with owners. That's what we'd come back to you with."

### "How would we know whether it worked?"

> "We'd agree that before anything's signed. For you I'd look at three things. Business: which mining, energy and survey customers and dealers you spent time with, and the orders that came from it. Hiring: how many good engineers applied, and how many came through Formula Student. And content: how far the films travelled, which we can measure.
>
> It can show up in the numbers. Airwallex says its revenue grew 50% year on year in its Europe and Americas regions during its McLaren partnership, and 57% in the UK from new customers."

### "Most of our business is defense. Will teams even talk to us?"

> "Fair question, and I'd rather be straight with you. As far as we can tell, neither F1 nor any team has published rules on this. So it comes down to each team, its owners and its partners. Some will rule it out. Others may not. Alpine signed Indra in January, and Indra calls itself a technology and defense group, so it isn't a closed door everywhere.
>
> So we'd check team by team, quietly, before anyone spends time on it and before your name goes to anyone. Where there's an opening, we'd lead with the civil work, the engineering and the hiring story.
>
> And where we don't know the answer for a team, we'll tell you that, rather than guess."

### "Won't F1 fans react badly, like with Rheinmetall?"

> "It's the right thing to ask. Dortmund signed Rheinmetall two and a half years ago, for three years, reportedly worth up to ten million euros a year. Nearly 90 fan groups protested when the season started. At the club's members' meeting that autumn, about two thirds of those who voted were against it. In October, Sport Bild reported the club is leaning towards letting it run out next summer. Nothing's been decided.
>
> Some things are different here. Rheinmetall makes weapons and ammunition, and Dortmund's members have a formal say in their club. F1 fans don't. That doesn't mean nobody would complain. Some would.
>
> What helps is what you lead with. Indra leads with its AI platform at Alpine, and we haven't seen a public row about it. For you, that's the mapping, the disaster work and the engineers. And we'd check how a team and its partners feel before anything is announced."

### "We're hiring hundreds of engineers. Can F1 help?"

> "It can, if it's built in from the start. 43% of F1 fans are under 35, and the deals that do this well make hiring part of the job.
>
> Komatsu's academy at Williams takes ten engineering students a year, found through Formula Student and Formula SAE in the US. Ma'aden uses its Aston Martin deal for university and internship programmes. And Formula Student Germany at Hockenheim brought around 3,000 students from 21 countries this August. A team's name helps you get noticed there.
>
> One thing to know: most teams are in England. The only F1 work near you is Audi's: the engine in Neuburg, near Ingolstadt, and the chassis in Hinwil, in Switzerland. That's a short list, and it'll have its own checks. But a team in England could still help you hire there, and you're growing in the UK."

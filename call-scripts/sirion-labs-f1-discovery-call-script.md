# Sirion Labs x SPORTFIVE: F1 Discovery Call Script

**Call type:** First call / discovery, brand-led. Goal: open with Sirion's own story to show real homework, keep the SPORTFIVE intro to three clear sentences, understand their objectives, fill out the brief, show why a sport built on promises kept fits a contracts company, agree next steps.

**How to use this script:** Every spoken block is written the way you would say it out loud. Do not read it word for word. Glance at the bold anchors, then talk. Short sentences, slow pace, warm tone. The listeners are enterprise software leaders selling to lawyers and procurement chiefs; they are precise people, so keep it plain, concrete and short. Let them talk more than you.

## Part 3: The brief. Key discovery questions (12 to 15 minutes)

*[These are conversation starters, not a form. Ask one, then stay quiet and follow what they give you. The bold labels are for you; the words are for them. Capture answers, they become the brief.]*

**Open the story**
- "Tell me where Sirion is right now, in your own words. What is going well, and what is the next hill to climb?"
- "When you and your team talk about the next three years, what comes up again and again?"

**Objectives, the dream and the fear**
- "Imagine we did something in Formula 1 together and it worked beautifully. Three years from now, what is different for Sirion?"
- "And the other side: what would make it a failure in your eyes? Sometimes that answer tells us more than the first one."

**The growth story**
- "Which is the harder fight for you right now: being known, or being trusted at the very top of the enterprise?"
- "How does a big new logo usually happen for you today? Who opens the door, and who signs?"

**Buyers and accounts**
- "Who exactly signs a Sirion contract: the General Counsel, the procurement chief, the CFO? Those are the people we would put in a room."
- "Are there named accounts you would build a program around?"

**Markets**
- "Which markets matter most: the US, Europe, Asia? The calendar covers all of them, and the US alone has three races now."

**Product and integration**
- "Here is one I am curious about. An F1 team is a bundle of contracts: twenty-five partners, hundreds of suppliers, freight, logistics, all under a hard cost cap. Would a real working role, a team running its commercial agreements on Sirion, interest you? Or is this more about the story and the access?"

**Budget and timing**
- "Now the unromantic question: is there a budget range in mind? Even a rough one helps me bring back only what is realistic."
- "And timing: is this a now conversation, or a next-year conversation? Both are fine. They just change what we bring back."

**Decision process**
- "Apart from you, who is part of this decision? The founders, the board, your investors?"

**Marketing context**
- "What is working for you today? And is there a story you wish more of the market knew?"
- "Would your team activate a sport partnership, content, events, campaigns, or would you want support there too?"

**Hospitality**
- "Last one, and it is my favorite. If I gave you a paddock weekend tomorrow, who would you bring, and why those people? That answer usually tells me what a partnership is really for."

*[If they hesitate on the budget question:]*

> "No problem. You do not need a number today. When we come back with recommendations, we will show options at different levels, and you will see what each level buys."

## Part 4: How Formula 1 partnerships work (8 to 10 minutes)

*[Calibrate to their answer from Part 2. If they know the sport, compress. Invite questions as you go: "Stop me anytime."]*

### 4.1 The sport in thirty seconds

> "Let me give you a simple picture of how the sport is set up, because it explains the options you have.
>
> Formula 1 itself, the series, is owned by Liberty Media, an American media company. They own the commercial side: the calendar, the broadcast deals, the races.
>
> The FIA is the regulator. They write the rules.
>
> And then there are eleven teams, including two American ones, Haas and Cadillac. Each team is an independent business with its own owners and its own partnerships. Some belong to car makers, like Ferrari, Mercedes and Audi. Some to consumer giants, like Red Bull. Some to investors.
>
> So a brand can partner at three levels: with the sport itself, with a team, or with a driver personally. Most brands enter through a team, and I can explain why when we get there."

### 4.2 What a team partnership includes

> "What do you get as a partner? Think of it in five parts.
>
> **One, branding.** Your logo on the car, the race suits, the garage. Around 24 race weekends a year. Last season the total audience was 1.8 billion, and the average race reached 76 million people.
>
> **Two, the official title.** You become, for example, the Official Contract Intelligence Partner of a team. You use the team's name, logo and imagery in your own marketing and sales conversations.
>
> **Three, hospitality.** Paddock access, the Paddock Club, garage tours, time with the drivers. 66% of Paddock Club guests are senior executives, and for a technology brand that room includes the leadership you sell to.
>
> And one detail people miss. A Grand Prix runs over three days, and your passes work every day. Seven passes can host twenty different guests in one weekend, and hundreds across a season.
>
> **Four, content and digital.** Access to the team's channels and drivers for content. Formula 1 is the number one sport in the world for social engagement, 2.2 billion engagements, ahead of the NBA.
>
> **Five, people.** Driver appearances, team bosses at your events, filming days, hot laps for your top customers.
>
> And around all of this sits the business platform: every team carries 25 or more partners, more than 350 companies across the grid, meeting each other all season. That room is your pipeline."

### 4.3 The audience

*[Pure B2B framing for Sirion: the buyers first, the fan scale as the stage.]*

> "Now, who you reach. For Sirion I would be precise about this.
>
> **The buyers.** Formula 1 has the highest concentration of business decision makers in sport: fans index at 160 for management and strategy roles, where the average person is 100. In the Paddock Club, 66% of guests are senior executives: the CIOs, the CFO organizations, the legal and procurement leadership of the companies around this sport.
>
> **The room itself.** The 350 partner companies around this grid are your market in miniature: airlines, banks, telecoms, manufacturers, technology giants. Every one of them holds thousands of contracts, and every one of them leaks value from those contracts somewhere. As a partner, you meet them as a peer, race after race, all season.
>
> **The world, when you want it.** 827 million fans, young and online, the number one sport for social engagement. You do not need mass reach to sell enterprise software. But when the Sirion name needs to mean something in a boardroom, the stage is already yours.
>
> And the US, a key growth market for enterprise software, is the sport's fastest growing one: three races, two American teams, Apple broadcasting."

### 4.4 Why this could fit Sirion

> "Let me tell you why I find this opportunity interesting for Sirion. Three reasons.
>
> **First, the story writes itself.** This sport is a web of promises kept in public. Eleven teams, 350 partnerships, hundreds of suppliers per car, every one of them on a multi-year contract, every one of them performing on a deadline that cannot move. Your one-sentence story here is simple: the sport of agreements kept, partnered with the company that keeps agreements. Very few brands get a stage where their product is the sport's own logic.
>
> **Second, the paddock is your prospect list.** The enterprises around this grid are the exact companies whose contract books you manage, and the software giants have shown the way: Atlassian on Williams, Oracle on Red Bull, HP on Ferrari, Microsoft with Mercedes as reported in January. When your buyers and your peers both spend here, the room is worth a serious look. A partnership puts you in it as a peer, at a fraction of what the giants pay.
>
> **Third, a real working role.** A team is a bundle of contracts under a hard cost cap: partners, suppliers, freight, hospitality, all of it. Imagine the partnership where a team's commercial office visibly runs its agreements on Sirion. We have built that shape before: RUCKUS at Haas is a partner whose product powers the team's operations, with a live showcase in the garage. Marketing you can demo is the most believable marketing there is.
>
> One last thing on value. All of this sits on very small supply: eleven teams for the entire world, one seat per category, three to five year deals. Small supply, growing demand. That is why these partnerships hold their value."

### 4.5 Three activation ideas (examples, not proposals)

*[Offer these as sketches to make it concrete. Let them react; their reaction feeds the brief.]*

> "To make it concrete, three sketches. Early thoughts, nothing more.
>
> **One, the contracts behind the car.** A content series with a team's commercial and operations people: what it takes to get a car to 24 races, the suppliers, the freight, the deadlines, the cost cap, and the agreements underneath it all. Nobody has ever told that story, and it makes Sirion instantly understandable.
>

Your best sales line becomes: the team that manages 25 partners and hundreds of suppliers under a cost cap does it on our platform.
>
> **Three, the deal room.** A curated season of Paddock Club weekends for the people who matter to you: General Counsels, procurement chiefs and CFOs of your named accounts, hosted with the team and the race as the backdrop. Seven passes host twenty guests in a weekend. Your pipeline for the year fits in a few of those rooms."
